### [1:05] Chapter 1 — What a single audit actually is

[VISUAL: A split panel. Left card: "Financial statement audit — is your bookkeeping accurate?" Right card: "Federal compliance audit — did you follow the grant rules?" A brace joins both under the label "Single Audit".]

Let's start with what the thing even is, because the name is misleading. A single audit is not a small audit, and it's not one audit. It's called "single" because it rolls what used to be a pile of separate agency audits into one yearly review.

A single audit has two halves. The first half is the part you might already know: an audit of your financial statements, the normal question of whether your books are accurate. The second half is the one that catches people. It's a compliance audit of your federal money. Did you spend it on what you said you would, did you follow the rules attached to each award, and can you prove it with records. That second half is the real work, and it's why a single audit is a much bigger deal than the financial audit many nonprofits already get every year.

[VISUAL: Timeline strip. "Single Audit Act, 1984 / amended 1996" → "OMB Circular A-133" fades out → "2 CFR Part 200, Subpart F (Uniform Guidance)" highlights in emerald.]

The rules come from the Single Audit Act, which Congress passed in nineteen eighty-four and amended in ninety-six. Today they live in a regulation called the Uniform Guidance, specifically Title two of the Code of Federal Regulations, Part two hundred, Subpart F. If you've been doing this a while and you remember something called Circular A-one-thirty-three, that's the old version. Same idea, new home. When somebody on your board says "the A-133 audit," this is what they mean.

### [2:15] Chapter 2 — Who needs one (and the number that just changed)

[VISUAL: Big centered figure "$1,000,000" in emerald. Below it: "Federal awards EXPENDED in one fiscal year". A small ochre note: "Up from $750,000 — 2024 Uniform Guidance".]

So who actually has to do this. Here's the rule in one sentence. If your organization spends one million dollars or more in federal awards during a single fiscal year, you are required to have a single audit for that year.

That one-million-dollar figure is the headline, and it's new. For years the threshold sat at seven hundred and fifty thousand. The 2024 update to the Uniform Guidance raised it to one million, and that change takes effect for fiscal years beginning on or after October first, twenty twenty-four. In plain terms, for most organizations that means fiscal years ending on or after September thirtieth, twenty twenty-five. So a lot of the advice still floating around online is quoting the old number. If a video or a checklist tells you the line is seven hundred fifty thousand, it's out of date.

[VISUAL: A short list of entity types fades in with checkmarks: Nonprofits (501c3) · States · Local governments · Tribal governments · Universities. A separate greyed line with an X: "For-profit companies — different rules".]

One more piece of the "who." The single audit applies to what the rules call non-federal entities. That's nonprofits, states, local governments, tribal governments, and universities. It does not apply to for-profit companies the same way. If you're a 501(c)(3) spending federal money, you're squarely in scope, and the threshold is that same one million.

### [3:30] Chapter 3 — The word that trips everyone up: "expended"

[VISUAL: Three stacked rows. "Awarded $1.4M" (greyed, X). "Received in cash $900K" (greyed, X). "Expended this fiscal year $1.05M" (emerald, check). Caption: "Only this number triggers the audit."]

Now the part people get wrong, and it's the most expensive thing to misread. The trigger is not how much you were awarded. It's not how much cash hit your bank account. It's how much you expended, how much you actually spent, in your fiscal year.

This matters in both directions. You could be awarded a two-million-dollar grant that runs over three years and never trip the threshold in any single year, because you only spend a portion each year. Or you could draw down several smaller grants quickly and cross one million without any single award looking that big. The rule looks at the total federal money you spent in the year, full stop. So the question to ask every year is not "how big are my grants," it's "how much federal money did we spend."

[VISUAL: A funnel graphic. Inputs: "Direct federal awards" + "Pass-through / subawards (money from a state or another nonprofit that started as federal)". Both flow into one total. A side note: "Contracts for goods & services — not counted".]

And spending counts whether the money came straight from a federal agency or passed through somebody else first. If a state agency or another nonprofit hands you federal dollars as a subaward, that still counts toward your million. A lot of organizations forget the pass-through money and undercount. The one thing that doesn't count is money you earn as a contractor selling goods or services. So you add up your direct federal awards and your federal pass-through money, and that combined number is what you're watching.

### [4:45] Chapter 4 — Single audit vs. program-specific audit

[VISUAL: Two paths from a single node. Path A: "Federal money across MULTIPLE programs → full Single Audit". Path B: "ONE federal program only → Program-Specific Audit (a narrower option)".]

There's one fork in the road worth knowing, because it can save a smaller organization real money. The default, if you're over the threshold, is the full single audit. But there's a narrower option called a program-specific audit.

You can elect the program-specific version only if you spent your federal money under a single federal program, not several, and as long as no law or award term separately requires you to audit your full financial statements. If all your federal funding flows through one program, your auditor can focus the compliance work on just that program instead of your whole organization. Most multi-grant nonprofits won't qualify, because they're running several programs at once. But if you're a smaller shop with one big federal stream, ask your auditor about it. It's a legitimate, lighter path, and it's written right into the rules.

### [5:40] Chapter 5 — What the auditor actually tests

[VISUAL: A document titled "Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards (SEFA)" with rows of programs and dollar amounts. An arrow points from it to a magnifying glass labeled "Major programs — risk-based selection".]

Okay, you're over a million. What actually happens. The first thing you'll build is a document called the Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards, the SEFA. It's exactly what it sounds like: a complete list of every federal program you spent money on this year and how much. The SEFA is the backbone of the whole audit. If it's wrong, everything downstream is wrong, and reconstructing it at year-end is where a lot of the pain lives. Hold that thought, we'll come back to it.

[VISUAL: A grid of program boxes; the auditor highlights two larger ones as "Major" and leaves the small ones unshaded. Caption: "Not every dollar gets tested — the riskiest, biggest programs do."]

Here's the part that surprises people: the auditor does not test every federal dollar. They use what's called a risk-based approach to pick certain programs, your "major programs," for deep compliance testing. Bigger programs and ones with a history of problems are more likely to get picked. So you don't get audited evenly across everything. You get audited where the risk and the money are concentrated.

[VISUAL: Card: "Performed by an independent CPA you hire" + "Follows Government Auditing Standards — the 'Yellow Book'". A small icon of a yellow manual.]

And to be clear about who runs it: a single audit is performed by an independent CPA firm that you hire and pay. The government doesn't send someone. That auditor follows a specific rulebook called Government Auditing Standards, which everyone calls the Yellow Book. The independence is the point, which is part of why you can't just have your own bookkeeper sign off on it.

### [7:00] Chapter 6 — Findings, questioned costs, and your corrective action plan

[VISUAL: A "Schedule of Findings & Questioned Costs" sheet. One row flags a red "Questioned cost — unsupported $14,200". Beside it a panel: "Corrective Action Plan — what you'll fix and by when".]

When the auditor finds something that doesn't line up, that becomes an audit finding. Maybe a cost you charged to a grant wasn't allowed, or you couldn't produce a receipt to back it up. Money the auditor can't verify, or thinks was spent improperly, gets labeled a questioned cost. And questioned costs are exactly what they sound like: dollars a funder may later ask you to give back.

For every finding, you have to write a corrective action plan. That's your written answer: here's what went wrong, here's who owns fixing it, here's when it'll be done. None of this is automatically a disaster. Clean audits happen all the time. But findings follow you, because next year's auditor reads last year's findings first, and a pattern of repeat findings is the kind of thing that makes a funder nervous.

[VISUAL: A small badge "Low-risk auditee" with a two-year checkmark trail. Caption: "A clean track record means the auditor has to test less next time."]

There's even an upside to a clean history. If you've had clean audits and filed them on time for the past couple of years, you may qualify as what's called a low-risk auditee, which means the auditor is allowed to test a smaller slice of your programs. So the reward for getting it right is a lighter audit the next year.

### [8:10] Chapter 7 — How and where you file it

[VISUAL: A box labeled "Reporting package" containing: Financial statements + SEFA · Auditor's reports · Schedule of findings · Corrective action plan · Data collection form (SF-SAC). An arrow to a portal: "Federal Audit Clearinghouse — fac.gov (now run by GSA)".]

Once the audit is done, you don't just file it in a drawer. The whole thing, the financial statements, the SEFA, the auditor's reports, your findings and corrective action plan, plus a summary form called the data collection form, all of that gets bundled into a reporting package and submitted electronically to one place: the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. If you've filed before and you're hunting for the old Census Bureau site, note that the Clearinghouse moved to the General Services Administration back in October of twenty twenty-three. It now lives at fac dot gov.

[VISUAL: A deadline graphic: two arrows, "30 days after you receive the auditor's report" and "9 months after your fiscal year ends," with a label "Whichever comes FIRST".]

And there's a deadline that catches people off guard. Your reporting package is due the earlier of two dates: thirty days after you receive the auditor's report, or nine months after the end of your fiscal year, whichever comes first. So if your year ends December thirty-first, your single audit is generally due by the following September thirtieth. Miss it, and a late filing is itself the kind of thing that can put you on a funder's watch list.

### [9:10] Chapter 8 — Where preparing for this breaks down

[VISUAL: A frantic spreadsheet scene: someone rebuilding a SEFA at year-end from scattered tabs labeled federal_2025_v3.xlsx, drawdowns_FINAL.xlsx; a "Did we cross $1M?" cell with a question mark.]

I build software for this, so let me be honest about where it gets hard, because the rule is simple and the doing is not.

The first problem is just knowing where you stand. The threshold is about money expended across the whole year, across every federal source including pass-through. If your federal spending lives in a dozen places, a few spreadsheets, your accounting system, somebody's email, you often can't answer the one question that matters, "did we cross a million," until you sit down at year-end and add it all up. By then it's too late to plan for the audit you just learned you need.

The second problem is the SEFA itself. Rebuilding a complete, accurate schedule of every federal dollar by program, at year-end, from records that weren't kept with the SEFA in mind, is where teams lose weeks. And the third is the audit trail. When the auditor asks for the documentation behind a questioned cost, you're either pulling it up in a minute or you're digging through eighteen months of files hoping it exists. None of that is anyone doing their job badly. It's what happens when a continuous, year-long tracking job gets treated as a year-end project.
