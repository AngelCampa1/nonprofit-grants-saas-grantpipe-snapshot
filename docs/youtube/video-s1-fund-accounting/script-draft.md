### Two different questions

[VISUAL: Warm paper title card. Two questions stack in. Top, in muted ink: "Did we make a profit?" Bottom, in emerald: "Did we use each dollar the way we promised?" A small ochre tag: "What is fund accounting?"]

Every business answers one question at the end of the year. Did we make a profit? A nonprofit has to answer a different one. Did we use each dollar the way we promised? Those are not the same question. That gap is the whole reason fund accounting exists.

If you run a nonprofit, you've probably been handed a report full of words like restricted, unrestricted, and net assets, and just nodded along. Today I'll explain what those actually mean, in plain English, in about seven minutes. No jargon for its own sake, and no pitch to go hire a big accounting firm.

Quick note on where I'm coming from. I build grant compliance software. To make a computer follow these rules, I had to learn exactly how the money is supposed to move. I'm not an auditor, and I won't talk like one. You're getting the version a busy director actually needs.

### What fund accounting is

[VISUAL: One bank account icon on the left. An arrow fans out to a row of labeled envelopes on the right: "After-school", "Building", "Use for anything". Cash flows into the envelopes, not into one pile.]

So what is it? Fund accounting is a way of tracking money by purpose, not by profit. Instead of pooling everything into one pot and asking what's left over, you split your money into separate funds. Each fund is its own little set of books, with its own rules about what it can pay for.

Picture one bank account versus a row of labeled envelopes. A regular business runs the one account and watches the balance. A nonprofit runs the envelopes. This one is the after-school program. This one is the building. This one is money you can spend on anything. The cash might sit in the same bank, but on paper, every dollar knows which envelope it belongs to.

The point isn't to make accounting harder. It's accountability. Fund accounting is built to answer one thing. Can you prove each dollar did the job it was given?

### Why nonprofits need it

[VISUAL: A grant award document with a tag tied to it reading "after-school only". Beside it, a plain income statement with a red "can't show this" stamp.]

Here's why a normal profit-and-loss report doesn't work for a nonprofit. Most of the money comes with strings. A funder gives you fifty thousand dollars, but only for the after-school program. A donor leaves money for the building, and nothing else. That's called restricted money. You're allowed to spend it, but only on the thing they named.

A regular income statement just shows money in and money out. It can't show whether you kept those promises. Fund accounting can. It keeps the restricted money in its own envelope, so at any moment you can show that their dollars went where they were supposed to.

And that question does get asked. When a funder or an auditor reviews you, they want to see that restricted money was spent on its restricted purpose. If you can't show it, that's where trust breaks. Fund accounting is what makes it provable.

### The building blocks

[VISUAL: Two large buckets side by side. Left, emerald: "Without donor restrictions — the flexible money". Right, ochre: "With donor restrictions — the promised money". Below, a faded, struck-through old label: "(old rule: 3 groups)".]

Okay, the building blocks. Money in a nonprofit gets sorted into two groups. The official names are net assets without donor restrictions, and net assets with donor restrictions. Don't let the words scare you. It's just free money and promised money.

Without donor restrictions is the flexible money. You can spend it on whatever the mission needs. Salaries, rent, the lights. With donor restrictions is money a donor or grantor tied to a specific purpose, or a specific time. The after-school grant lives here. So does a gift you're asked to hold forever, like an endowment.

If you've read older articles, you might have seen three groups. Unrestricted, temporarily restricted, and permanently restricted. That was the old rule. The accounting standards changed it to just two groups, for financial years starting after late twenty seventeen. So if a guide still lists three, it's out of date. There are two.

One more trap. If your board decides to set aside money for a rainy day, that is not restricted money. The board can change its own mind later. Only an outside donor can truly restrict a gift. Board savings stay in the flexible group.

### A worked example

[VISUAL: One org at top. Two funds below as labeled jars. Left jar "After-school fund" fills with $50,000 (restricted). Right jar "General fund" fills with $30,000 (flexible). Then a dotted arrow tries to move money from the after-school jar to cover "Rent", and a red line blocks it.]

Let me make this real with a tiny example. Say a nonprofit gets two pots of money this month. A fifty-thousand-dollar grant, restricted to the after-school program. And thirty thousand in general donations, with no strings.

In fund accounting, those don't merge. The fifty thousand goes into the after-school fund. The thirty thousand goes into the general fund. Now say the after-school program is running fine, but the office is short on rent. Can you borrow from the after-school grant to cover it? No. That money is promised. Spending it on rent would break the restriction, and it would show.

That's the whole idea in one picture. Two funds, two sets of rules, and a line you can't quietly cross. When the after-school program actually spends its grant on the program, the promise is kept, and that money is now free and clear. Accountants call that releasing it from restriction. You can just call it a promise kept.

### How it differs from regular accounting

[VISUAL: A two-column contrast table. Header left "Business", header right "Nonprofit". Rows reveal one at a time: "Balance sheet → Statement of financial position", "Income statement → Statement of activities", "Owner's equity / profit → Net assets".]

Because the goal is different, the reports look different too. A business has a balance sheet. A nonprofit has a statement of financial position. Same idea, new name. A business has an income statement. A nonprofit has a statement of activities.

And a business has owner's equity, or profit. A nonprofit has net assets. There are no owners to pay, so there's no profit line. What's left over isn't profit. It's net assets, carried forward to keep doing the work.

One quick aside, so I'm not hiding anything. Governments use a version of fund accounting too, under a different rulebook, with its own kinds of funds. That's a separate world. Everything here is about nonprofits.

### One idea to remember

[VISUAL: Clean recap card. The one line, large: "Track money by the promise attached to it."
