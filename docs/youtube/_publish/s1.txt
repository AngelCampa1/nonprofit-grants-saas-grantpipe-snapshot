THE ENVELOPE IDEA
The simplest way to picture fund accounting: one bank account, several envelopes. The money all sits in the same place, but each envelope is labeled with what it's allowed to pay for. Restricted gifts get their own envelope; general donations go in the free one. Fund accounting is just the discipline of never spending from the wrong envelope, and being able to show, at any time, how much is left in each.

WHY OLD GUIDES TRIP PEOPLE UP
If you've read older nonprofit accounting articles, you may have seen three categories: unrestricted, temporarily restricted, and permanently restricted. That model is out of date. The rules now use two: net assets with donor restrictions, and net assets without. The change applies to financial years that start after late 2017, so plenty of advice still floating around online teaches the old three. It's a small thing to get right, but it's the kind of detail an auditor or a sharp board member will notice, and it's why I call it out in the video.

WHY THE REPORT NAMES MATTER
The name changes aren't just vocabulary. A statement of financial position and a statement of activities are built to show net assets by restriction, which is the whole point. A regular balance sheet and income statement aren't. So when someone hands you a nonprofit's financials, the report names themselves tell you whether the books are set up to answer the question that actually matters: did each dollar go where it was promised.
