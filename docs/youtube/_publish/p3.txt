A restriction is a promise. A funder gives you money for one thing, and from then on you have to prove how much is left. A normal activity report won't tell you that. This video shows you how to track restricted funds so you can answer that question any time, on the spot.

WHAT'S IN THE VIDEO
- What "restricted" really means, and the two groups nonprofit books use now: net assets with donor restrictions, and without. The old three-bucket model is gone, and a lot of older guides still teach it.
- The one trap to skip: money your board sets aside on its own is not restricted. Board-designated funds feel restricted, but the board can un-designate them, so they don't count the same way.
- The running balance every award needs, in four moves: start with the award, add money as it comes in, release it as you spend, and end with what's left. That ending number is the one a funder asks for.
- Setting up a fund in the real app and picking its type, so the software knows it's tracking a restriction.

- Proving the restriction in the Restrictions tab, with the app flagging a release that has no support attached. That flag is the difference between a balance you can defend and one you just hope is right.

WHY A REPORT ISN'T ENOUGH
A profit-and-loss report answers one question: did money come in and go out. It can't show that the money you spent was the money you were allowed to spend. Restricted-fund tracking sits on top of that, keeping each promise as its own running balance. When an auditor or a program officer asks how much of their grant remains, you don't estimate. You read it off the screen, with the support to back it up.

THE FOUR MOVES, IN PRACTICE
Every restricted award follows the same shape, and once you see it the bookkeeping stops feeling mysterious. You start with the amount the funder committed. You add money as it actually arrives. You release it as you spend, which is the step most people skip, because a release is the moment you formally say "this expense used part of this restriction." Then you end with a balance that is simply the start, plus what came in, minus what you released. The reason a tool helps here is the release step: each one should point to a real cost, and when it doesn't, that gap is exactly what an auditor circles. The video shows the app flagging a release with no support attached, so the gap gets caught while you can still fix it, not a year later.
