**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Under 2 CFR 200.430, employees on multiple federal awards cannot use semi-annual time certification — but most organizations apply it to them anyway.

Semi-annual certification is permitted only for employees working solely on a single federal award or cost objective. An employee working 80% on one federal award and 20% on administrative duties does not qualify. Monthly certification is required for that employee.

Auditors know this rule and test for it by looking at how employees are classified and whether the certification frequency matches the classification. It is one of the most reliable single-audit findings in the personnel cost category.

The correct process: pull a list of every employee with federal grant charges for the period, determine certification frequency based on whether they are single-award or multi-award, distribute certification forms within two weeks of month-end, collect and review for completeness, then reconcile certification percentages to posted payroll allocations.

That reconciliation step — comparing what employees certified against what payroll actually posted — is the control that makes the entire system credible. Filing certifications without reconciling provides no assurance.

Certifications requested 90 days after the period they cover are reconstructions from memory. Auditors can tell the difference.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Form 990 is a public document. That sentence changes how you should think about filing it.

Every officer, director, trustee, and key employee's compensation is disclosed by name in Part VII. The five highest-compensated employees earning over $100,000 are also listed. A mid-sized nonprofit with $350,000 in gross receipts and $600,000 in assets files the full Form 990 — gross receipts over $200,000 or assets over $500,000 triggers the full return.

The due date is the 15th day of the 5th month after fiscal year end — May 15 for calendar-year organizations. An automatic 6-month extension is available by filing Form 8868 before that date, extending to November 15. That extension is not a grace period; it is a formal mechanism that requires affirmative action before the due date.

The IRS revokes tax-exempt status of approximately 30,000 organizations per year for failure to file for three consecutive years under IRC Section 6033(j). Reinstatement requires a new application and may attract additional IRS scrutiny.

Grant funders, major donors, and watchdog organizations review 990s on ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer — which has indexed more than 3 million filings — before engaging with an organization. Inaccurate restricted fund accounting flows directly into 990 inaccuracies on Parts VIII, IX, and Schedule D.

The 990 is both a compliance filing and a credibility document. Treat it like both.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

A youth program managing 21st Century Community Learning Centers funds, an AmeriCorps grant, and two foundation grants is operating four separate compliance frameworks simultaneously.

21st CCLC compliance is not only financial — it requires participant attendance tracking, outcome reporting (teacher surveys, grade progression data), and demographic records submitted to both the federal Department of Education and the state education agency. The state layer varies significantly: some states require quarterly submissions, others semi-annual. The format and data requirements differ by state.

AmeriCorps compliance runs through Corporation for National and Community Service systems — a separate portal, separate member hour tracking, and prohibited activities compliance that has nothing to do with 21st CCLC documentation.

Foundation grants add a third reporting format and a third deadline calendar.

For a lean program team — often one director managing 15 part-time program staff — this is not a coordination problem that more effort solves. It is a systems problem. The complexity of tracking multiple federal compliance frameworks simultaneously exceeds what any individual can manage manually without documentation failures.

36% of nonprofits ended FY2024 with an operating deficit, the highest rate in a decade. Administrative overhead in multi-grant environments is part of that pressure.

Youth programs carrying federal grants need compliance infrastructure, not just a spreadsheet with color-coded deadlines.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

A grant reporting deadline tracked only by due date is a deadline that will eventually be missed.

The fields that distinguish a reporting system that works from one that produces scrambles: named primary owner (a person, not a role), named backup owner, prep start date calculated from the report type, submission method and portal URL, and current status.

For a combined interim report — narrative plus financial in a single submission — the prep lead time is 12–15 business days before the due date. For a final financial SF-425, it is 20 business days. For a final combined report, 25 business days. These are not arbitrary buffers; they are the minimum time needed to pull financial data, write the narrative, obtain internal review, and submit through the correct portal.

A portfolio of 10 active grants with mixed quarterly and annual reporting typically generates 40–80 report deadlines per year. When prep periods overlap, months with 4 or more reports due should be flagged for capacity planning before the pressure arrives, not during it.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five federal grant compliance tasks that most grant tracking software does not handle — and where the audit findings originate.

First: SEFA preparation. The Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards requires every federal program expended during the year to be listed by Assistance Listing Number (formerly CFDA number), with pass-through entity information for any sub-awards received. Most grant trackers record grant amounts and deadlines. They do not store ALN numbers or pass-through entity data in the structure the SEFA requires.

Second: time-and-effort certification. Under 2 CFR 200.430, salaries charged to federal awards must be supported by signed certifications from employees or supervisors confirming actual time distribution. Most general-purpose grant trackers have no certification workflow.

Third: NICRA indirect cost rate application. A Negotiated Indirect Cost Rate Agreement specifies the rate at which overhead can be charged to each federal award against a Modified Total Direct Costs base. Manual rate lookup across multiple concurrent awards produces both under-recovery and disallowances.

Fourth: SF-425 federal financial report data organization. SF-425s are due 30 days after each calendar quarter for most awards. The data must reconcile to the general ledger. Assembling this manually from accounting exports each quarter is where errors compound.

Fifth: budget-to-actual monitoring by object class. Federal awards are awarded against approved budget categories. Expenditures that cross category lines above defined thresholds require prior agency approval. Software that tracks total award balance but not category-level spend misses this control entirely.

The gap between a pipeline tracker and a federal compliance system shows up at audit time, not at setup.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

How to request a no-cost extension on a federal grant. Eight steps.

Step 1: Confirm your award allows an NCE. Review the Notice of Award. Some awards prohibit extensions or cap them at one extension of up to 12 months. Confirm before drafting the request.

Step 2: Confirm an NCE is the right tool. An NCE is for time, not money. If the real problem is insufficient funding, that is a budget supplement request — a different process with much lower approval odds. Be honest about which problem you have.

Step 3: Request at least 30 days before the period end date. Most agencies require 30–60 days minimum. If your grant ends September 30 and you request on September 15, a 30-day processing timeline means the approval arrives October 15 — after expiration. Costs incurred after the end date but before the extension is approved are unallowable.

Step 4: Contact your program officer before submitting. An informal conversation gives you early indication of approval likelihood and guidance on what justification the agency wants to see. Program officers often have preferences not documented in written guidance.

Step 5: Write a specific justification. Name external factors that caused the delay — personnel vacancy, regulatory approval delay, disrupted program delivery — and describe exactly what work remains. Vague justifications ("we need more time") are routinely denied.

Step 6: Prepare a spend-down projection by budget category. Show what funds remain in each line and the expected expenditures during the extension. Unexplained balances in categories with no spending plan raise flags.

Step 7: Submit through the correct channel. HHS uses different portals than NIH eRA Commons, NSF Research.gov, or HUD's IDIS. Confirm the channel with your program officer.

Step 8: After approval, update your grant record immediately. The new period end date shifts the final SF-425 due date (120 days after period end under 2 CFR 200.344), the final programmatic report, and any compliance calendar items tied to the original end date. File the approval letter in the grant file as an amendment to the Notice of Award.
