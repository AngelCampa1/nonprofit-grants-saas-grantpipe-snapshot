**Post 1 — stat_bomb**

The 2024 Uniform Guidance revision raised the federal single audit threshold to $1,000,000 in federal expenditures.

That change pulled smaller cost-reimbursement awardees out of single-audit scope, but it did not change how revenue gets recognized.

FASB ASU 2018-08 still governs the two tests for grant revenue under ASC 958-605: contribution vs exchange, then conditional vs unconditional.

Cost-reimbursement federal awards are typically conditional contributions. Revenue is recognized only as allowable costs are incurred, not when the award letter arrives.

Finance teams that book the full award upfront overstate revenue and end up restating it during audit fieldwork.

If your grant ledger does not match your expenditure schedule line for line, the conditional accounting is breaking somewhere.

GrantPipe attaches a ledger to each award so revenue recognition tracks expenditures automatically.

**Post 2 — myth_buster**

Program service revenue is not unrelated business income.

This is where mid-sized nonprofits over-file Form 990-T and pay UBIT they do not owe.

Program service revenue is income earned from activities substantially related to the exempt purpose. A health center charging sliding-scale fees is program service revenue. A literacy nonprofit selling workbooks tied to its curriculum is program service revenue.

Earning it does not jeopardize tax-exempt status. The risk only appears when an activity stops being substantially related and starts looking commercial in scope and substance.

Excessive unrelated commercial activity raises private benefit and inurement concerns, which is a separate analysis from whether the revenue itself is taxable.

The practical test: review activities annually against the exempt purpose stated in your determination letter. Document the connection. Keep that memo with the 990 workpapers.

**Post 3 — workflow_step**

For advance federal awards, interest earned above $500 per year across all awards must be remitted annually to HHS PSC under 2 CFR 200.305(b)(9).

Most mid-sized nonprofits never compute this until the auditor asks.

Steps 5 and 6 of a monthly grant drawdown reconciliation handle it cleanly.

Step 5: calculate interest on advance balances. Pull the average daily cash balance for each advance award, apply the operating account interest rate, accumulate the monthly figure. Twelve clean monthly numbers turn the annual remittance into addition.

Step 6: document variances and reclassifications. If a draw exceeded expenditures, write down whether it was a timing difference, an accrual, or an overdraw requiring return. If expenditures exceeded draws, plan the next draw to close the gap. Every variance gets a written explanation filed with the reconciliation.

Monthly cadence catches errors while they are still fixable. It also makes the quarterly Federal Financial Report a 30-minute task instead of a fire drill.

**Post 4 — term_explained**

Questioned costs.

Defined at 2 CFR 200.516, a questioned cost is an expenditure flagged during a single audit because it appears unallowable, unsupported, or unreasonable.

Three categories. Unallowable: violates statute, regulation, or terms of the award. Unsupported: missing documentation. Unreasonable: exceeds what a prudent person would incur in similar circumstances.

The reporting threshold is $25,000 per federal program. When questioned costs attributable to a single program exceed that, they are listed in the Schedule of Findings and Questioned Costs.

Two examples that show up frequently. An auditor finds $32,000 in payroll charged to a federal award without time-and-effort documentation — reported as unsupported, exceeds threshold. A nonprofit charges $18,000 in alcohol-related catering to a training grant — unallowable, under threshold, still disclosed.

Questioned costs are not automatic disallowances. The pass-through entity or federal agency makes the final determination. The audit just flags them.

**Post 5 — tool_teardown**

Bloomerang is a well-designed donor CRM. The retention analytics, constituent timeline, and lapse-tracking tools were built around a real insight: nonprofits lose nearly half their donors every year, and retention is where development economics get solved.

For donor work, the product knows what it is.

The breakdown happens when the same development director managing Bloomerang also manages 3 or more active grants with restricted-fund obligations.

Bloomerang's grant fields are essentially custom fields on a constituent record. They cannot model the funder reporting calendar, restricted-fund balances, allowable cost categories, or the conditional revenue recognition that ASC 958-605 requires.

The choice at that point is binary. Add a separate grant compliance tool and pay for two systems that do not share data. Or move to a platform that handles both donors and grants in one record set.

The two-tool path looks cheaper for about six months. After that, the manual reconciliation between systems usually costs more than either subscription.

**Post 6 — vertical_hook**

A mature Federally Qualified Health Center typically manages 15 to 20 simultaneous federal and state awards.

The base HRSA Section 330 grant. HRSA supplemental awards. Ryan White HIV/AIDS program grants across multiple Parts. CDC prevention grants. State health department contracts.

Each award has distinct allowable cost categories, reporting calendars, and matching requirements. HRSA's Uniform Data System reporting intersects with financial compliance, so program data and expenditure data have to reconcile across systems that were never built to talk to each other.

Most donor CRMs cannot support the expenditure coding this requires. Most general accounting systems cannot model funder restrictions in a way that produces a clean spend-down report.

The result at most FQHCs: a finance team running parallel spreadsheets for grant tracking, drawdowns, and UDS-aligned program metrics, reconciled by hand at month-end.

The compliance environment is not getting simpler. The toolset has to.

**Post 7 — free_resource**

A grant that projects $40,000 unspent with two months remaining is not a financial planning problem. It is a compliance problem that requires action now.

A spend-down tracker answers one question per active award: given what has been spent and when the period ends, are we on pace to use the full award correctly?

The structure is straightforward. One worksheet per grant. Budget vs actual by allowable cost category. Monthly burn rate. Projected end-of-award balance based on current pace. A decision framework for low burn rate situations — accelerate eligible expenditures, request a no-cost extension, or rebudget within funder rules.

The point is not the spreadsheet. The point is catching underspend in month 9 instead of month 11 of a 12-month award, when the options are still real.

The Grant Spend-Down Tracker is a free download. PDF with the template structure and the decision framework.

**Post 8 — comparison_insight**

Givebutter and GrantPipe solve different problems. Putting them head to head only makes sense once you know which problem you actually have.

Givebutter is a fundraising-page tool with a free starting tier. Solid peer-to-peer, event ticketing, embeddable donation forms. The CRM piece is essentially gift record keeping. No grant management, no restricted-fund tracking at any tier — including the paid tiers that start at $29/mo.

GrantPipe runs $329 to $1,079/mo and bundles donor CRM with grant compliance and restricted-fund tracking in one record set.

The decision is not about features. It is about stage.

If the organization is early, primarily event- and campaign-funded, and has no active grant portfolio, Givebutter's free tier is the right call. Pay for tools you need.

Once there are active grants, restricted-fund obligations, or a board asking for a release-from-restriction report, a fundraising-page tool stops being the right tool. The reconciliation work between Givebutter and a separate grant tracker eats the savings.

**Post 9 — question_post**

A $3M nonprofit typically runs Bloomerang for donors, QuickBooks Online for accounting, a spreadsheet for grants, Google Workspace for documents, and a separate payroll system.

That stack is normal. It is also where the operations manager's week disappears.

The question for ops managers reading this: if you wrote down every system the development, finance, and program teams touch in a normal week, how many show up?

For most $500K to $10M nonprofits the count lands between 8 and 14.

What is the one consolidation that would actually free up the most time — not the most license cost, the most time?
