# Form 990 Filing: What Mid-Sized Nonprofits Get Wrong, and How to Fix It Before May 15

A $4M nonprofit with a December 31 fiscal year owes the IRS a Form 990 by May 15. Miss the deadline three years running and tax-exempt status is automatically revoked under IRC Section 6033(j). Reinstatement is a separate application, and the lapse covers the period of non-filing — meaning donations received during the gap may not have been deductible.

That is the headline risk. The day-to-day risk is more mundane and more common: the wrong form filed, the wrong schedules attached, the public copy of Schedule B mishandled, or executive compensation reported in a way that pulls an audit trigger that should never have been pulled.

This guide walks the filing decisions that mid-sized nonprofits get wrong most often — and the practitioner answers for each.

## Which version of the 990

The form an organization files is a function of two numbers: gross receipts and total assets.

Form 990 (full): required if gross receipts are $200,000 or more, or total assets are $500,000 or more. Either threshold triggers the full return.

Form 990-EZ: available only if gross receipts are less than $200,000 and total assets are less than $500,000. Both thresholds must be under.

Form 990-N (e-Postcard): available if gross receipts are normally $50,000 or less. Eight pieces of identifying information, filed online, no financial detail.

Form 990-PF: required for all private foundations regardless of size.

Two practitioner notes. First, gross receipts is not net revenue. A $190,000 gross-receipts nonprofit that processes $400,000 through a fiscal sponsor relationship may need a closer look — gross receipts in some scenarios includes amounts received before netting. Second, total assets is the year-end number from the balance sheet, not an average. A one-time gift that lands on December 30 can push an organization across the $500,000 threshold for that year only.

## Due date and the automatic extension

The return is due on the 15th day of the 5th month after fiscal year end. For calendar-year filers, that is May 15.

The automatic 6-month extension is granted by filing Form 8868 by the original due date. It is not discretionary. No reason is required. The extension is the default move for any organization that does not have a clean reason to file early — audited financial statements are usually not finalized in time for the original deadline anyway, and filing a 990 that conflicts with the audited statements is one of the most cited inconsistency triggers.

For December 31 fiscal year filers, the extended deadline is November 15. That is enough time to align the 990 with audited statements, finalize Schedule J compensation analysis, and reconcile restricted net asset categories — three places where rushed returns produce inconsistent figures.

## Schedules: which ones and why

The full 990 is a 12-page form. The schedules are where most of the actual work and most of the audit exposure lives.

Schedule A (Public Charity Status and Public Support): required for all 501(c)(3) public charities. The public support test is a five-year calculation. Get it wrong and the organization can be reclassified as a private foundation, which carries excise taxes and stricter rules on grants.

Schedule B (Schedule of Contributors): required when contributions meet specific thresholds. The big change is on the public copy, covered below.

Schedule C (Political Campaign and Lobbying Activities): required if any lobbying or political activity occurred. The 501(h) election changes the calculation, and organizations that filed Form 5768 should know which method applies.

Schedule D (Supplemental Financial Statements): required for restricted endowments, conservation easements, art and museum collections, escrow arrangements, and other items. Restricted-fund nonprofits almost always file Schedule D.

Schedule G (Fundraising Activities): required if gross fundraising receipts exceed $15,000. Special events are reported here in a way that ties back to Part VIII of the core form.

Schedule J (Compensation Information): required when any officer, director, or key employee earns more than $150,000, or when other thresholds are met. This is where executive compensation gets scrutinized.

Schedule L (Transactions with Interested Persons): required for certain related-party transactions. Loans to officers, business dealings with board members, grants to related organizations.

There are more — Schedules F, H, I, K, M, N, O, R each apply in specific scenarios. The point for finance teams: confirm the schedule list before fieldwork starts, not in mid-April.

## The Schedule B donor disclosure change

Revenue Procedure 2018-38 and subsequent litigation reshaped the public-disclosure rules for Schedule B.

The current state: most tax-exempt organizations other than 501(c)(3) public charities are exempt from including donor names and addresses on the publicly-disclosed copy of Schedule B. Public charities still report contributor names and addresses on the IRS copy, but the publicly available version may omit donor identifying information.

Two failure modes show up in practice. First, tax software that has not been updated correctly produces a public copy that still includes donor names — this is the more common error and it has resulted in inadvertent donor disclosure that violated state-law expectations. Second, organizations that wrongly believe they are fully exempt from Schedule B and do not file it with the IRS at all.

The fix is procedural. Before the return is filed, someone other than the preparer reviews both the IRS copy and the public-inspection copy of Schedule B. The two copies should be different documents in the workpapers. If they look identical, the public copy was probably never redacted.

## The Form 990 to audited financials reconciliation

The single most common audit trigger for mid-sized nonprofits is not high executive compensation. It is inconsistency between the Form 990 and the audited financial statements.

The IRS knows the audited statements exist. State charity regulators ask for both. Watchdog sites pull both. Discrepancies between the two are the cheapest red flag a reviewer can find.

Common discrepancies. Net asset categories that do not match — temporarily restricted on one document, with donor restrictions on the other, with no reconciliation. Special event revenue reported gross on the 990 but net on the audit. Functional expense allocations that differ because they were prepared by different people from different worksheets. Restricted contribution revenue recognized on different bases.

The fix is sequencing. Audit first. 990 second. Reconciliation memo in the workpapers explaining any presentation differences and why each is appropriate. The audited financials are the primary source. The 990 follows.

## The most-cited audit triggers

Six patterns get cited most often in IRS Form 990 examination summaries.

High executive compensation relative to organization size, especially when comparable-data documentation is missing. Significant related-party transactions on Schedule L. Large unexplained changes in program service descriptions year over year. Discrepancies between Part VII compensation and Schedule J detail. Failure to consistently report restricted and temporarily restricted net assets — often a leftover from the FASB ASC 958-205 transition that some organizations never fully completed. Inconsistent figures between the 990 and audited financial statements.

None of these are aggressive positions. All of them are documentation gaps. The trigger is not the underlying activity — it is the lack of supporting workpapers when the IRS asks.

## The state extension question

The federal Form 8868 extension does not automatically extend every state charitable-registration renewal.

California's RRF-1 follows the federal extension automatically when properly documented. Many other states do not. New York's CHAR500 has its own extension form. Pennsylvania's BCO-10 is independent. Several states do not recognize the federal extension at all.

Multi-state filers should keep a one-page matrix: state, registration form, extension policy, due date with extension. Update it annually. The matrix takes 30 minutes to build the first time and prevents the late filing that triggers state penalties.

## The single most common failure

The most common failure mode is filing the 990 before the audit is complete and assuming the figures will match.

They do not match. The audit adjusts net asset categories, reclassifies restricted contributions, finalizes functional expense allocations, and trues up accruals. The 990 filed in April with preliminary numbers does not survive the audit unchanged. The result is either an amended return — which itself flags the file — or two public documents that disagree.

The fix is to use Form 8868. The automatic 6-month extension exists for exactly this reason.

## What to do this week

For a calendar-year organization with May 15 approaching:

File Form 8868 before May 15 if the audit is not finalized. The extension is automatic and costs nothing.

Confirm the filing version. Recompute gross receipts and total assets at year-end values. If either crosses the threshold, the full 990 applies regardless of last year's filing.

Build the schedule list before the preparer does. Schedule A, B, D, G, J, L are the high-frequency ones for mid-sized nonprofits. Confirm whether each applies.

Pull the 2024 audited financials and the prior-year 990. The current-year 990 needs to reconcile to both — to the audit for current numbers, to the prior 990 for trend comparability.

Have someone other than the preparer review the public copy of Schedule B against the IRS copy. They should not be identical.
