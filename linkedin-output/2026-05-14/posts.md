**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

Procurement deficiencies are among the top 10 compliance findings in HHS, DOE, and DOJ-funded single audits every year — and the most common specific finding is inadequate competition documentation.

The 2024 Uniform Guidance revision (89 FR 30046, effective October 1, 2024) did not raise the simplified acquisition threshold. It clarified it at $250,000. Organizations whose written procurement policies still reference older figures are technically out of compliance before a single purchase is made.

There are five procurement methods under 2 CFR 200 Subpart D, each with different competition requirements. Micro-purchases up to $10,000 need no competition. Small purchases between $10,001 and $250,000 require price quotations from adequate sources. Sealed bids and competitive proposals apply above $250,000. Sole source requires one of four specific written justifications.

The written policy itself is the first finding. 2 CFR 200.318 requires it in writing. An undocumented process — even a competitive one — fails the standard.

SAM.gov debarment checks are required before any federally funded award over $25,000. The check must be documented and dated before award, not at the start of solicitation.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Bloomerang has grant fields. That does not mean it handles grant compliance.

The difference matters operationally. Bloomerang can record a funder name, an award amount, and a report due date. What it cannot do is model the restriction on those funds, track expenditures against approved budget categories, flag when a budget line is approaching its ceiling, or generate a financial report to send to the funder.

A $75,000 grant restricted to youth programming is not the same as $75,000 in unrestricted revenue. Every expenditure charged to that grant must fall within the approved scope. Bloomerang records the grant. It does not monitor compliance with it.

The three-grant threshold is real. Organizations managing one or two active grants with light restrictions can hold the compliance workflow in a spreadsheet alongside Bloomerang. At three to five active grants — different funders, different fiscal years, different restriction types — that spreadsheet becomes a liability.

Federal grants accelerate the timeline. SF-425 quarterly reports require expenditure-level data that Bloomerang's grant fields cannot supply. The compliance overhead increases substantially the first time a federal award enters the portfolio.

The clearest signal: your grants manager maintains more compliance spreadsheets than Bloomerang grant records. That gap is the workflow Bloomerang was not built to cover.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

The most common pledge accounting error is not the math. It is the classification.

Under FASB ASC 958-605, a pledge is conditional when a donor-imposed barrier must be overcome before the organization is entitled to the funds — a matching requirement, a milestone, a future event. Without a barrier, the pledge is unconditional and must be recorded as contribution revenue and receivable when the promise is made, not when cash arrives.

ASU 2018-08 sharpened the definition of a barrier: it must be specific, measurable, and related to the purpose of the agreement. A donor who says "I will give $50,000 if you hire a program director" has imposed a barrier. A donor who says "I hope you will hire a program director with this $50,000" has not.

Misclassifying unconditional as conditional understates current-year revenue. The financial statements are misstated, not just conservative.

Multi-year pledges add another layer. A $100,000 pledge payable $20,000 per year over five years is worth less than $100,000 today. ASC 958-605 requires discounting to present value using a risk-adjusted rate, typically 3-5%. Skipping the discount overstates both pledge receivables and contribution revenue.

Every pledge portfolio also needs an allowance for uncollectible pledges. An allowance of zero is almost never defensible — even strong portfolios see 1-5% attrition.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

The difference between a grant pipeline and a grant portfolio matters operationally.

A grant pipeline is the pre-award stage: opportunities in research, applications in progress, submissions awaiting decision. The development director uses it to forecast revenue and prioritize staff time. A well-maintained pipeline shows every opportunity by funder, amount, probability of award, and expected decision date — enabling a 6-18 month revenue projection.

A grant portfolio is the post-award stage: active awards with restrictions, spending trajectories, compliance deadlines, and reporting obligations. Finance and program staff operate here. This is where compliance failures actually occur.

These phases connect but are managed differently. When a pipeline opportunity becomes an award, it enters the portfolio — and the work shifts from relationship management and writing to expenditure tracking, restricted fund monitoring, and funder reporting.

Most compliance problems happen in the portfolio phase, not the pipeline phase. Organizations that treat the grant lifecycle as ending at the award decision lose visibility into the post-award work at exactly the point where the real risk sits.

Revenue risk concentrates in the pipeline. Compliance risk concentrates in the portfolio. A development director who only manages pre-award data is managing half the picture.

Probability weighting works better than raw totals for forecasting. A $100,000 application with a 60% probability of award has an expected value of $60,000. Assigning 90% to every open application is not forecasting — it is wishful thinking with extra steps.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

Charter schools receiving ESSER III funds had a spend-down deadline of September 30, 2024. Schools that could not document expenditures by that date face recoupment from state agencies.

ESSER allocations totaled approximately $122 billion nationally. Many schools that received ESSER for academic recovery or mental health services also received private foundation grants for similar purposes — creating potential double-billing exposure that requires careful cost allocation documentation.

The compliance framework for charter schools is layered. Title I and IDEA Part B funds fall under EDGAR (34 CFR Parts 75-79) and Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200), with the supplement-not-supplant requirement under ESEA Section 1118 adding another documentation layer on top of standard federal grant rules.

Student information systems — PowerSchool, Infinite Campus, Illuminate — track students, not compliance obligations. They do not produce restricted fund balances, reporting deadline calendars, or cost allocation documentation that shows which expenditure was charged to which federal program and why.

The schools managing this well are the ones that recognized the SIS limitation early and built a separate layer for grant compliance and donor management. Not because the SIS is inadequate at what it does — but because managing federal grant compliance and donor relationships is a different job.

36% of nonprofits ended FY2024 with an operating deficit. As per-pupil funding tightens and ESSER ends, philanthropic funding matters more — which means donor management infrastructure matters more too.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

A restricted fund tracking spreadsheet works until it does not. Knowing which point that is matters.

The template structure is straightforward: one row per active restricted grant, with fund name, funder, restriction type (purpose, time, or perpetual), award amount, received to date, monthly actuals by period, cumulative expenses, remaining balance, award end date, release conditions, and documentation reference.

The monthly update process has six steps: pull current period expenses from the general ledger after month-end close, enter the period actuals exactly (no rounding, no estimating), verify the cumulative total against the GL, recalculate remaining balances, update statuses for funds that closed, and date and initial the spreadsheet to establish the documentation trail.

The reconciliation is not optional. Every month, total receipts, total expenses by fund, and remaining balance by fund in the spreadsheet should match the general ledger exactly. When they do not, investigate immediately. Small discrepancies caught early are traceable. Accumulated discrepancies require forensic-level investigation at audit time.

The signals that the spreadsheet has reached its limit: more than six to eight active restricted grants, a federal grant with 2 CFR 200 compliance requirements, staff turnover creating knowledge gaps, the monthly reconciliation taking more than two hours, or an auditor noting the manual methodology as a finding.

At any of those points, the tracking system has been outgrown — not the people managing it.

**Post 8 — COMPARISON_INSIGHT**

Network for Good's rebrand to Bonterra does not change the product's feature ceiling.

The acquisition created a product now managed at the Bonterra portfolio level. That changes roadmap priorities, pricing decisions, and long-term service terms in ways that existing and prospective customers cannot fully predict until contract renewal. Nonprofit CRM churn increases significantly in the 12-18 months following a vendor acquisition, per NTEN research.

The more fundamental comparison point is what each product is designed to do. Network for Good is a donor management CRM with bundled coaching and advisory services. The coaching is genuinely useful for organizations with smaller or newer development staff. For organizations where the primary bottleneck is fundraising strategy and relationship skills, the coaching premium addresses a real need.

What coaching cannot address is grant compliance workflow. Network for Good does not include restricted fund tracking, grant deadline management, or the documentation infrastructure required for SF-425 reports or audit-ready SEFA schedules. Organizations managing federal awards or multiple foundation grants with programmatic reporting requirements will find that no service tier solves this gap.

Only 33% of nonprofits rate their CRM as effective, per the Fifty & Fifty 2025 Nonprofit Peer Report. The gap is often not that the chosen platform is inadequate at what it does — it is that organizations chose a platform optimized for donor management when their real operational burden is grant compliance.

Identifying the dominant bottleneck before selecting a platform saves the cost of switching eighteen months later.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five questions that cut through grant compliance software evaluations faster than any feature list:

1. Where are restricted fund balances visible right now, without opening a spreadsheet? If the answer requires a phone call or a manual pull, the tracking system is not working.

2. How is documentation attached to the underlying spend? Receipts and supporting documentation need to live alongside the transaction, not in a separate folder that has to be assembled at audit time.

3. Who sees the reporting calendar before deadlines become urgent? The compliance calendar should be visible to finance, development, and leadership — not just the grants manager with the spreadsheet.

4. Can finance and development describe the status of the same grant without translating between two systems? Dual-system workflows — a CRM for development, a spreadsheet for compliance — produce two versions of the same data and one recurring reconciliation problem.

5. What still depends on a named individual after implementation? Any system that works only because one specific person knows how to maintain it is not a system — it is institutional knowledge with a tool on top.

The answers expose where the real operational burden sits: before award in the pipeline, or after award in the portfolio. Purpose-built compliance workflow focuses on post-award because that is where the compliance risk actually lives.

Spreadsheets stay common for too long because they are cheap and already present. The cost shows up later as coordination time, delayed reporting confidence, and the recurring risk that the next answer leadership needs will take too long to assemble.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

How to set up restricted fund tracking that survives an audit:

Step 1: Separate each restricted grant into its own fund code in the accounting system before any expenses are posted. Coding transactions retrospectively creates gaps that compound. Set up the fund code at award, not at first expenditure.

Step 2: Build the restriction profile from the grant agreement — not from memory. Record the purpose restriction exactly as the funder wrote it, the approved budget by category, the award period start and end dates, and the conditions under which the restriction is released. If the agreement says "direct program costs for youth ages 10-18 in the Riverside service area," that is what goes in the system.

Step 3: Pull monthly actuals from the general ledger after month-end close, by fund code. Enter exact figures — no rounding, no estimates. The cumulative total in the tracking system must match the GL for the same period exactly. If they do not match, investigate before entering the next period.

Step 4: Monitor budget-to-actual by category, not just by total award. A grant budget with $10,000 approved for program supplies that has been charged $12,000 in supplies is a compliance issue, even if the total award balance is still positive.

Step 5: Flag restrictions for release when the condition is met — either the purpose has been fulfilled or the time period has lapsed. Delayed reclassification overstates restricted net assets on the statement of financial position and distorts financial trends.

Step 6: Reconcile the tracking register to the GL before every funder report, not after. A discrepancy discovered during report preparation creates pressure to submit incorrect numbers.
