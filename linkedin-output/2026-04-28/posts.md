**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

New York's CHAR500 audit threshold sits at $1 million in gross revenue plus support — exactly the same dollar figure as the federal single audit threshold, but governed by NY Executive Law 172-b, not 2 CFR 200.501.

Same number, different authority. A New York charity that crosses $1M in total revenue and also expends $1M in federal awards in the same year owes both the state-required independent audit under Article 7-A and a single audit under the OMB 2 CFR Part 200 October 2024 revision. Coordinated through one CPA engagement, but two distinct compliance obligations with separate reporting paths.

Below $1M but above $250K of gross revenue plus support, New York requires a CPA review — not the full audit. Below $250K, neither.

The CHAR500 itself is due May 15 for calendar-year filers, four months and fifteen days after fiscal year end. Filing fees range from $25 to $750 depending on Article 7-A and EPTL registration. Most rejection notices come from missing the Form 990 attachment, missing the second officer signature, or miscalculating the fee tier.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: a subrecipient with no single audit history is a low-risk subrecipient.

Reality: 2 CFR 200.332(b) requires the pass-through entity to evaluate risk before issuing the sub-award, and "no audit history" is not the same as "clean audit history." Many smaller subrecipients have never crossed the $1,000,000 single audit threshold in any prior year. The audit silence is informational absence, not risk evidence.

When a subrecipient lacks audit history, the regulation still requires a documented assessment. The weight shifts to the pre-award questionnaire, financial statement review if available, organizational references, and adequacy of financial management systems. A composite risk score under 1.5 across the six factors in 200.332(b) suggests low risk; between 1.5 and 2.5 suggests medium; above 2.5 suggests high.

The most common audit finding on subrecipient monitoring is not that the monitoring was inadequate. It is that the pre-award risk assessment was never done. Auditors look for the document specifically because the regulation requires it before the sub-award is issued — and its absence is an automatic finding that cannot be cured after the fact.

Document the rationale, score each factor, and file the assessment alongside the sub-award agreement. The matrix is the answer when the auditor asks how the risk rating was reached.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Step 2 of a nonprofit pledge write-off: determine the treatment under FASB ASC 958-605 before you book the entry.

The instinct from for-profit accounting is to debit bad debt expense and credit the receivable. Under ASC 958, that is wrong. The write-off of an unconditional pledge receivable reduces the allowance for uncollectible pledges and the pledge receivable — provided the allowance was appropriately estimated when the pledge was originally recorded. It is contra-revenue mechanics, not an operating expense.

If the original allowance was set to zero, the write-off has nowhere to go but contribution revenue. Organizations that use a blanket zero-allowance policy create exactly that scenario: every write-off becomes a reduction to current-year contribution revenue on the Statement of Activities.

Step 3 is approval. Typical thresholds under ASC 958-605 documentation discipline: under $1,000 approved by the finance director, $1,000 to $10,000 by the executive director, over $10,000 by the board. Whatever your policy specifies, the written approval must precede the journal entry.

Multi-year pledges add one more wrinkle. Per ASC 958-605-30-6, multi-year pledges carry a present-value discount that unwinds annually. When the pledge is written off, the unamortized discount on the unpaid portion must be eliminated proportionally. Leaving it in place overstates the discount contra-asset.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

A grant expenditure report is not a budget-versus-actual report.

The two documents look similar on paper. Both compare what was spent against the approved budget by category. The difference sits in the legal weight. A budget-versus-actual is an internal management tool. A grant expenditure report is a funder-facing certification — signed, submitted, and binding. Signing it carries compliance responsibility for the truth of the numbers.

For federal awards, the standard expenditure report is the SF-425 Federal Financial Report. Under 2 CFR 200.328, financial reports are due no less than annually and within 90 days of the end of the award period. The SF-425 captures cumulative obligations, period-specific expenditures, federal cash on hand, program income, and indirect cost rate.

The reconciliation rule is non-negotiable. Every dollar shown on the SF-425 must trace to the grantee's general ledger by grant fund code for the corresponding period. A discrepancy between the submitted report and the accounting records is an audit finding. Single audits routinely flag this. The funder may refuse to reimburse the questioned costs and require corrective action.

Practical discipline: pull the GL by grant fund code, reconcile to the planned SF-425 numbers, resolve any variance, then submit. Never the other order.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

The NEA awarded $174.5 million to arts organizations across all 50 states in FY2023, and most of those grants required 1:1 matching documented contemporaneously.

That last word — contemporaneously — is where museums get burned. NEA project grants typically require the organization to document non-federal contributions equal to the federal award. Match can come from earned revenue, foundation grants, non-federal government awards, and in-kind contributions. What it cannot do is appear retroactively. An organization that assembles match documentation after the award period ends does not have match documentation; it has a reconstruction project that auditors recognize on sight.

Museums managing NEA, NEH, and IMLS awards simultaneously face three overlapping compliance frameworks under 2 CFR 200, plus state arts council pass-throughs, plus private foundation grants from places like Mellon and Getty. Each one with its own reporting format. NEA reports through REACH. NEH preservation grants restrict funds narrowly to the approved conservation activity — prior approval required before any reallocation. IMLS Museums for America awards layer program-specific deliverables on top of standard 200 financial requirements.

Multi-year exhibition grants compound the problem. Spending in Year 2 on activities budgeted for Year 1 may require prior approval and documented reallocation. Cumulative tracking is not a reporting convenience — it is the compliance mechanism.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

Donor CRM and grant accounting records describe the same funders from two different operational angles, and that is exactly why they drift apart.

Development enters a gift when the award letter arrives. Finance posts the receipt when cash clears. Two workflows, two timestamps, two restriction classifications, two amounts (pledged versus net of fees). Without an annual reconciliation, the gap compounds until no one can reliably answer "how much restricted funding did we receive from Funder X this year."

Four discrepancy types account for almost all the variance. Type 1: CRM-only — gift entered but never posted in accounting. Type 2: accounting-only — government grant that bypassed development entirely. Type 3: amount mismatch — full pledged amount in CRM, first installment in accounting. Type 4: classification mismatch — development called it unrestricted general support; finance read the grant agreement and coded it restricted.

The tie-breaker is always the source document. Grant agreement section reference, award letter, signed pledge card. Not staff recollection. Run the full reconciliation annually before audit fieldwork. Spot-check monthly for funders representing more than 10% of restricted revenue.

**Post 9 — QUESTION_POST**

Board treasurers carry personal fiduciary exposure for an organization they see for two hours a month.

The BoardSource Leading with Intent 2021 survey found 75% of board members rate their own financial literacy as basic or below. That is not a character flaw — it is structural. Volunteer boards are composed for governance breadth, not accounting depth. The treasurer is liable for restricted fund misuse they "knew or should have known" about, while reviewing a Statement of Activities that reports net assets with donor restrictions as a single aggregate line.

FASB ASC 958 does not require grant-level detail on the face of the statements. A nonprofit managing 10 restricted grants can satisfy the standard with one number. The treasurer either trusts that number or asks for a supporting schedule, which usually arrives Friday evening before a Tuesday meeting via spreadsheet.

**Post 10 — PRODUCT_INSIGHT**

The split between pre-award tools and post-award tools is the single biggest reason grant data goes stale.

Most nonprofits run an Instrumentl or GrantHub subscription for the pipeline, then re-key the awarded grant into a spreadsheet or QuickBooks class for compliance tracking. The same grant gets modeled twice, in two systems, with two custodians, and two slightly different sets of facts. By month six of the award, the pipeline tool says "awarded" with a stale amount, the accounting system shows posted expenses, and no one is sure which restriction language is the latest.

The architectural fix is to make the opportunity and the grant the same record at different lifecycle stages. Pre-award fields stay editable through prospect, LOI, and applied. On award, post-award fields open up — reporting schedule, fund restrictions, allocation rules — and the pre-award history becomes read-only. No re-keying because there is no handoff.

Default stage probabilities anchor the forecast: prospect 10%, LOI 30%, applied 50%, awarded 100%, declined 0%. Override per opportunity when funder history justifies it. AFP's Fundraising Effectiveness Project shows cold application win rates in the 10-20% range, which is what the prospect default reflects.

GrantPipe runs the pipeline, the awarded grant, and the restricted fund as one connected record so the forecast a board sees is the same forecast the development director and the finance lead are running on.
