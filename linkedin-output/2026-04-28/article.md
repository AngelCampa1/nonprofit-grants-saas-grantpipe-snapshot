# The $846M Nonprofit CRM Market: What the Consolidation Wave Means for Mid-Sized Buyers

The nonprofit CRM market reached approximately $846 million in 2025. That figure captures a sector mid-restructuring: mega-mergers are reshaping the mid-market, AI features are arriving faster than buyers can evaluate them, and 48% of nonprofits told Omatic's 2025 Nonprofit Integration Report they are considering switching CRMs in the next 12 months. The year prior, that number was 10%.

For an Executive Director or board member sizing up a CRM investment, this matters for one practical reason. The vendor on the contract today may not be the same company in two years. Knowing the structure of the market is part of the buying decision now in a way it was not a decade ago.

## The Two-Tier Top of the Market

Two vendors hold the enterprise tier. Blackbaud accounts for roughly 40-50% of U.S. mid-to-large nonprofit CRM deployments and processes nearly $31 billion in charitable donations annually, per AppsRunTheWorld 2024 and PortersFiveForce.com 2025. The portfolio spans Raiser's Edge NXT for fundraising, Financial Edge for accounting, and Luminate Online for digital engagement. For nonprofits with $10M+ budgets, Blackbaud is often the default choice — backed by the deepest functional surface area and the longest tenure in the sector.

Salesforce serves approximately 55,000 nonprofit organizations through its Power of Us program, which donates up to 10 Enterprise Edition licenses to qualifying nonprofits. In 2026 the Nonprofit Success Pack was rebranded as Agentforce Nonprofit, with AI-powered donor insights and predictive analytics added to the stack. Salesforce remains the most customizable nonprofit CRM available, which is both its strength and its cost driver. Free licenses do not eliminate implementation cost, admin cost, or the managed services often required to keep the platform operational.

Despite the dominance of those two names, the top five vendors collectively hold only 40-45% of global nonprofit software revenue. The remaining 55-60% sits across dozens of smaller platforms, each addressing a specific segment, geography, or functional gap.

## The Mid-Market Consolidation Wave

The most dramatic structural change is happening below the enterprise tier. In 2022, a single transaction created Bonterra by merging Network for Good, EveryAction, Social Solutions, and CyberGrants. Bonterra now serves over 15,000 organizations and covers fundraising, advocacy, case management, and grant management under one corporate parent.

Consolidation continued. In October 2025, Bonterra acquired OneCause, adding 14,000 clients and event fundraising capability. In January 2024, Bloomerang acquired Qgiv, combining its donor CRM with Qgiv's online fundraising and peer-to-peer tools.

For buyers, consolidation cuts both directions. Larger vendors can invest more in product development and integration. They can also raise prices, change support models, and force migrations between legacy products that customers chose specifically because they were independent. Bonterra's post-merger integration has drawn criticism from customers who experienced disruption during platform transitions — an outcome that surprised buyers who chose what they thought were three different products.

The takeaway is not that consolidation is good or bad. It is that the buying decision now includes a forecast of where the vendor will be in two years, not just what the product does today. Data portability and contract flexibility are weight-bearing factors in a way they were not when the market was more stable.

## The Switching Wave

The single most consequential market data point for 2025-2026 is the 48% switching consideration rate. That is the figure from Omatic's 2025 Nonprofit Integration Report, up from 10% the year prior — the largest migration intent the sector has produced in available survey history.

The drivers, per Omatic, are feature gaps and organizational growth, not primarily cost. Nonprofits that grew past their original CRM's design point are looking for systems that fit their current shape. Organizations whose grant portfolios outgrew donor-CRM-only tooling are looking for unified records.

Switching is not free. Data migration, staff retraining, and 6-12 months of reduced productivity during transition are real costs. The upside is that organizations actively considering a change have a window to evaluate the full market rather than defaulting to the incumbent. Vendors are competing harder for mid-market customers than at any point in the last decade — pricing flexibility and onboarding investment are genuinely on the table for buyers willing to ask.

## The AI Layer, Unevenly Distributed

Every major vendor now ships or has announced AI features. Salesforce Agentforce Nonprofit includes AI-powered donor insights and predictive analytics. Blackbaud has integrated AI into reporting and prospect research. Bloomerang and Neon CRM offer AI-generated communication suggestions.

The practical effect for most mid-sized nonprofits is limited. AI features in nonprofit CRMs are primarily useful for organizations with large enough datasets to train meaningful models — typically 10,000+ donor records and several years of giving history. For a nonprofit with 1,500 active donors and three years of data, the AI additions are marketing positioning more than operational tools. That can change as models get more sample-efficient, but the current state should be evaluated honestly.

The buying advice is to weight AI claims by data scale. Ask what the feature does on a database your size, not on the vendor's reference customer.

## The Underserved Middle

The market's blind spot is the intersection of donor management and grant compliance. Most nonprofit CRMs were built around individual giving — prospect research, cultivation, solicitation, stewardship. Grant management, restricted fund tracking, post-award compliance, and funder reporting are treated as secondary features or pushed off to separate tools entirely.

For the estimated 1.8 million nonprofits in the U.S. that receive both individual donations and institutional grants, this gap means running two systems and reconciling data between them. The Nonprofit Finance Fund's 2025 State of the Nonprofit Sector Survey found that organizations managing restricted grants without integrated compliance tracking spent an average of 11 additional hours per month assembling manual reports. That is more than a full work week per quarter, recurring, on a task that should be a system output.

The pricing structure of the existing market makes the gap worse. Salesforce Agentforce Nonprofit is free for the first 10 licenses but typically requires $30,000 to $100,000 in implementation and ongoing admin support. Blackbaud Raiser's Edge NXT starts around $4,000 per year and runs higher with multi-year contracts. Bloomerang starts at $125 per month, DonorPerfect's published entry tier is $99 per month, Neon CRM at $99 per month, and Little Green Light at $45 per month — but each of those is a donor CRM, not a unified system.

For mid-sized nonprofits with $500K to $10M budgets — the segment squarely in the gap — the choice has typically been between an oversized enterprise platform that needs an admin to operate or a focused donor CRM plus a separate compliance layer. Both options carry coordination cost the buyer absorbs in staff time.

## DonorPerfect, Bloomerang, and the Donor-CRM Anchor

DonorPerfect publishes a 93% customer retention rate and a 13-year average customer tenure. Bloomerang anchors on donor retention as a core thesis. Both are mature donor CRMs with strong fundraising workflows, and both serve their market segment well.

The boundary case is the same for both: fund designation labels are present, fund balances are not. A development director using either platform can tag a gift as restricted to a specific fund. The current balance of that fund — accounting for posted expenses, releases from restriction, and reporting cadence — is calculated outside the CRM, in the accounting system or a spreadsheet. For nonprofits where the restricted fund question rarely comes up, that boundary is fine. For nonprofits where the ED gets asked the restricted balance question every other week, the missing layer is a recurring tax.

## What This Means for Buying Decisions in 2026

Three concrete takeaways. First, contract flexibility and data portability matter more than they did in 2018. The vendor on the cover page may be acquired, rebranded, or merged into a portfolio within the contract term. Read the data export clauses. Confirm formats are non-proprietary. Negotiate exit terms before signing.

Second, the 48% switching intent rate gives buyers real negotiating room. Vendors expect to compete harder for mid-market customers this year than last. Multi-year discounts, implementation credits, and honest reference customer conversations are achievable. The buyer who walks away from one option to evaluate another is rewarded in the current market in a way that was not true when switching consideration sat at 10%.

Third, the top-five vendors hold less than half the market. There are viable options beyond the obvious names, and the best fit often comes from a purpose-built tool that matches operational requirements rather than a platform trying to serve every nonprofit segment. The implementation tax on the larger platforms is the actual cost. License fees are the visible portion; admin time, consultant fees, and the productivity dip during onboarding are the rest.

## What to Evaluate This Month

If a CRM evaluation is on the calendar this year, four questions structure the decision better than a feature checklist.

What information do you actually need every week? Not what the vendor demos. The five questions an ED gets asked between board meetings — restricted fund balances, grant compliance status, pipeline forecast, recent giving activity, audit readiness — are the dashboard the system needs to produce.

What is the total cost over three years? License plus implementation plus admin staff time plus the second tool that handles whatever the primary one does not. The honest comparison is total operational cost, not the line item on the contract.

What happens if the vendor is acquired in year two? Read the data export clause. Confirm migration paths. Talk to a customer who went through a vendor merger.

What does your current workflow actually require — and what is configuration debt versus genuine capability need? Organizations carrying years of accumulated workarounds in their current CRM often discover, on evaluating alternatives, that the workarounds were patches for missing features the new system handles natively.
