**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Virginia gives nonprofits 90 days to file their charitable registration renewal after fiscal year end.

Most states allow 4.5 to 10 months. New York's CHAR500 deadline is 4.5 months out. California's RRF-1 gives 4 months 15 days. Connecticut allows 10 months.

Virginia's 90-day window under Va. Code Ann. § 57-48 is among the shortest in the country — and it creates a real operational problem for organizations that need audited financial statements to complete the filing.

The financial statement tiers make this harder. If gross revenue is between $250,000 and $999,999, a CPA-reviewed statement is required. At $1,000,000 and above, a full audit is required. Neither can realistically be completed and delivered in 90 days if the CPA engagement starts after year-end.

The practical implication: audit or review fieldwork must begin before fiscal year close, not after.

Virginia's audit threshold is also revenue-based, not expenditure-based. An organization with $900,000 in gross revenue and $800,000 in federal expenditures clears the federal Single Audit threshold under 2 CFR 200.501 but not Virginia's state audit requirement — though it still needs a CPA review for the Virginia filing.

Registration fees range from $0 (under $5,000) to $325 (over $10M). The fee is the easy part. The 90-day CPA coordination is where organizations fall short.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

A Single Audit finding requires a written Corrective Action Plan under 2 CFR 200.511(c). The plan must include five elements: finding reference, the auditee's position (agree or disagree), the specific corrective action, a named responsible party, and a target completion date.

Missing any one of those five elements invites awarding-agency follow-up — and often leads to enhanced reporting requirements or specific conditions on future draws.

The most common weakness is vague language. "We will improve our procurement controls" is not a CAP. "The CFO will update the procurement policy by May 31, distribute it to all staff with signed attestations, and present completed attestations to the audit committee at the June meeting" is a CAP.

The second most common weakness is addressing the effect rather than the cause. A finding that expenditures were unallowable because procurement policy was not followed requires a procurement policy fix — not just refunding the questioned costs.

Questioned costs do not automatically require repayment. The awarding agency reviews and determines whether costs are allowable, unallowable, or resolvable through additional documentation. Include the repay/dispute/allow decision in the CAP rather than waiting for the agency to ask.

The submission deadline is tight: the CAP goes to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse with the full Single Audit report, due the earlier of 30 days after the auditor's report or nine months after fiscal year-end under 2 CFR 200.512(a)(1).

Repeat findings in two or more consecutive audits trigger specific conditions under 2 CFR 200.208 — including prior approval requirements and high-risk designation.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

The ACFR — Annual Comprehensive Financial Report — is what state and local governments publish instead of audited financial statements.

Most nonprofits do not prepare one. But grant-active nonprofits encounter ACFRs when they receive pass-through funding from a governmental entity.

The ACFR has three sections: an introductory section with letters of transmittal, a financial section with the audited statements and management's discussion and analysis, and a statistical section with ten years of trend data. When a government entity expends $1,000,000 or more in federal awards, the Single Audit compliance report is included too.

GASB governs the ACFR. FASB ASC 958 governs nonprofit financial statements. They are different frameworks with different fund accounting structures and measurement models. Receiving government funding does not change a nonprofit's accounting framework.

The name changed in 2021. GFOA renamed the Comprehensive Annual Financial Report (CAFR) to the Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR) to remove an acronym associated with a historical slur. The content and structure are unchanged. Legacy documents still say CAFR.

When a nonprofit receives a state-passed sub-award of federal funds, the state's ACFR contains the prime recipient's compliance audit results and federal expenditure disclosures. Understanding the pass-through entity's ACFR audit status helps subrecipients anticipate additional monitoring requirements before they are imposed.

Over 4,000 governmental entities receive the GFOA Certificate of Achievement for Excellence in Financial Reporting for their ACFR annually.

**Post 6 — HOW_TO**

Donor-restricted funds and board-designated funds are not the same category. Treating them as equivalent creates fiduciary exposure that can reach board level.

Donor restrictions are external legal obligations. When a donor or grantor attaches conditions to a contribution, those conditions are contractually binding under the gift agreement. The state attorney general has enforcement jurisdiction over charitable assets. A board vote cannot override a donor restriction — only the donor can release it, or the restriction terms must be satisfied.

Board designations are internal governance decisions. A board can resolve to set aside funds for a capital project, a reserve, or a strategic initiative — and the same board can reverse that decision at a later meeting. The designation is organizational policy, not a legal obligation to an outside party.

Under ASU 2016-14, donor-restricted funds appear in the with-donor-restrictions net asset class on the Statement of Financial Position. Board-designated funds appear within without-donor-restrictions — disclosed in notes, but not classified as restricted.

Commingling is where things break. If restricted and unrestricted funds share a bank account without project-level sub-account tracking, it becomes impossible to demonstrate that restricted dollars were spent on authorized purposes. That appearance of misuse is the problem — even if actual spending was correct.

Practical controls: assign a unique GL code to each restricted grant, code expenditures to the grant at transaction time (not in batch), and run a restricted fund balance report monthly showing opening balance, receipts, expenditures, and closing balance by grant.

A negative balance on a restricted fund means you spent more than was in it. That is a misappropriation signal, not an accounting rounding issue.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

2 CFR 200.430 requires that salary charges to federal awards be based on records reflecting actual work performed — maintained contemporaneously.

"Contemporaneous" is the operative word. A record completed two days after the fact is acceptable. A record completed two months after the fact is not.

There are two documentation standards under the regulation. Employees working 100% on one federal program may use semi-annual certifications — signed attestations that all compensated time went to that program. Employees working across multiple grants or activities must maintain personnel activity reports (PARs) that account for 100% of compensated hours, prepared at least monthly, and signed by the employee.

The most common mistake: entering budgeted percentages instead of recording actual time. An employee budgeted at 50% FTE on a grant who writes "50%" on every time report — without recording actual daily hours — is documenting the budget, not actual effort. Auditors flag this pattern.

The second most common mistake: completing all time logs at year-end or before the auditor arrives. When records for 12 months are all dated within a two-week window in December, the records are not contemporaneous.

A free grant staff time tracking template — structured to meet 2 CFR 200.430 requirements — is available at grantpipe.com. It includes the daily time log structure for split-funded employees, the semi-annual certification form for single-program employees, and a monthly reconciliation worksheet for finance staff.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five questions that determine whether a nonprofit's software can actually handle both donors and grants:

1. Does it track restricted fund balances against actual expenditures in real time? Not "does it have a grants module" — does it tell you today how much of each restricted fund remains after approved expenses?

2. Can development and finance see the same grant record simultaneously, or does reconciliation happen between two separate system views at quarter-end?

3. Does the system generate funder-required compliance reports — SF-425 for federal grants, programmatic reports for foundations — or does report production require exporting data to a separate tool?

4. What happens at grant closeout? Does the system support final reporting, document retention, and audit trail, or does the grant record just sit closed with no post-award lifecycle management?

5. What is the realistic year-one cost, including implementation, migration, and staff time — not just the subscription line?

Most mid-sized nonprofits discover the real answer to question 1 when the first federal grant arrives. The restricted fund balance is either in the donor CRM (usually not), in the fund accounting system (sometimes), or in a spreadsheet (most often).

The gap between development's view of the grant and finance's view of the expenditures is where compliance risk accumulates — not loudly, but consistently, until an audit surfaces it.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

The SEFA cluster-identification error appears in approximately 30% of audited entities with multiple federal programs, according to OMB's Single Audit Quality Study.

The Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards is the foundation of every single audit. Auditors use it to determine which programs are "major programs" requiring audit opinions. A misclassified program means the major-program selection is wrong — and audit scope expands.

The cluster problem: OMB defines program clusters in Appendix VI to 2 CFR Part 200, updated annually in the Compliance Supplement. When an organization receives funding under two ALNs that belong to the same cluster, those ALNs must be grouped together and labeled with the cluster name. Listing them separately understates cluster expenditures and can cause the auditor to undercount which programs cross the major-program threshold.

The pass-through problem: for every program where the organization passes federal funds to a subrecipient, the SEFA must identify the subrecipient name and total amount passed through, per 2 CFR 200.510(b). A $5,000 subaward from a $500,000 federal grant must still be disclosed. This is one of the most common HHS-funded audit findings.

Indirect costs belong on the SEFA too. Organizations that omit indirect cost recovery from SEFA expenditures understate their federal program activity and may misidentify which programs cross the major-program threshold.

Each year's cluster definitions must be verified against the current OMB Compliance Supplement — cluster membership changes annually.

GrantPipe tracks expenditures by award and ALN as costs are incurred, so SEFA preparation is a report run, not a manual assembly exercise. Pass-through subrecipient data is captured at grant setup, not reconstructed at year-end.
