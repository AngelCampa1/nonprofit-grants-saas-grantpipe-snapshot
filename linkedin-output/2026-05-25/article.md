# How Nonprofits Are Leaving Real Money on the Table: The Indirect Cost Rate Problem

A finance director at a $2M nonprofit once described the grant budgeting process at her organization as "writing checks to the government." Every federal grant her team received was partially subsidized by unrestricted operating funds. The grants showed up in reports as revenue, but the organizational overhead those programs consumed was never recovered. After three years, the operating reserve was gone.

The problem was not bad programming or weak fundraising. It was indirect cost underrecovery — a structural financial issue that is common, measurable, and largely avoidable.

## What Indirect Costs Actually Are

Indirect costs — also called facilities and administrative costs, or F&A costs — are real organizational expenses that support program delivery without being directly traceable to any single grant. Rent, utilities, executive director time spent on management rather than specific programs, central bookkeeping, IT infrastructure, general supplies: every dollar spent running the organization as a functioning entity is an indirect cost.

These costs are not overhead in the pejorative sense. Every dollar your organization spends on administration is a dollar that enables your programs to operate. Indirect cost recovery through grants is the mechanism by which grant-funded programs pay their proportional share of those enabling expenses, rather than drawing down organizational reserves.

Organizations that do not claim indirect costs are effectively asking unrestricted donors to subsidize federally funded programs. That is a defensible choice in some situations. In most, it is an unexamined habit.

## The De Minimis Path Most Organizations Miss

Under 2 CFR 200.414(f), any nonprofit that has never had a federally negotiated indirect cost rate may use 10% of Modified Total Direct Costs as an indirect cost charge on any federal grant — without submitting a rate proposal or negotiating with any federal agency.

This is the 15% de minimis rate. It is available to organizations that have never negotiated a NICRA. It requires no paperwork beyond consistent application and disclosure in the grant budget.

MTDC is direct costs minus specific exclusions: equipment above the capitalization threshold (typically $5,000), capital expenditures, subcontract costs above $25,000 per subcontract, patient care costs, tuition remission, and participant support costs.

A practical example: a grant with $200,000 in direct costs — $120,000 personnel, $30,000 supplies, $20,000 in a single equipment item, $30,000 in a subcontract — has an MTDC of $175,000 ($200,000 minus $20,000 equipment minus $5,000 of the subcontract above the $50,000 threshold). At 15% de minimis: $17,500 in recoverable indirect costs. Per year, across a portfolio of five active federal grants, that math compounds significantly.

The consistency requirement matters: once an organization elects the de minimis rate, it applies to all federal awards until the organization formally negotiates a NICRA. The rate cannot be applied selectively.

## The Three-Step Rate Calculation

The Uniform Guidance Mechanism for Indirect Cost Recovery

Even organizations using the de minimis rate benefit from knowing their actual indirect cost rate — if only to understand how much they are underrecovering.

**Step 1: Define the indirect cost pool.** List every organizational cost that is not directly attributable to a specific program: executive and administrative salaries (the portion not directly charged to grants), rent and utilities for shared space, general accounting, HR, IT support, software licenses used across the organization, and general office supplies.

**Step 2: Sum the pool and identify the allocation base.** Calculate total indirect costs for a full fiscal year. The most common allocation base is MTDC — consistent with the Uniform Guidance calculation.

**Step 3: Divide.** Total indirect costs ÷ MTDC allocation base = actual indirect cost rate. For example: $180,000 in indirect costs ÷ $600,000 MTDC = 30% actual indirect cost rate.

If the organization is using the 15% de minimis rate on a $600,000 MTDC base, it is recovering $60,000 of its $180,000 in actual overhead. The remaining $120,000 is absorbed by unrestricted funds or operating reserves. That is not a sustainable structural position for a grant-dependent organization.

## When to Negotiate a NICRA

The Negotiated Indirect Cost Rate Agreement

A NICRA is appropriate when the 15% de minimis rate materially underrecoves actual overhead, when the organization receives federal funding from multiple agencies (the NICRA applies to all of them), or when a specific federal program requires a negotiated rate.

The process starts with identifying the cognizant federal agency — typically the agency that provides the most federal funding to the organization. For community health and social service nonprofits, this is often HHS. For workforce development organizations, it is often DOL.

The rate proposal submitted to the cognizant agency includes audited financial statements, a cost allocation plan describing how the organization classifies costs as direct versus indirect, a breakdown of the indirect cost pool, and the proposed allocation base with the calculated rate. The negotiation typically takes several months; a provisional rate can be used in the interim.

NICRA rate types offer different levels of certainty:

A **predetermined rate** is fixed for the agreement period regardless of actual costs. It provides budget certainty at the cost of potential over- or underrecovery.

A **fixed rate with carryforward** is fixed for the period, but over- or underrecoveries carry into the next negotiation. It tracks actual costs more closely over time.

A **provisional/final rate** uses an estimated rate during the year, settled to a final rate after year-end cost analysis. It is the most accurate but requires adjustment billing after close.

Organizations with actual indirect rates above 20% should seriously evaluate whether negotiating a NICRA is worth the administrative investment. On a portfolio of active federal grants, the additional recovery can be substantial.

## The Foundation Indirect Cost Gap

Private foundations operate outside the Uniform Guidance. Many impose their own indirect cost limits: no indirect allowed, a fixed cap of 10–15% regardless of NICRA rate, or — at the larger end — acceptance of the federally approved rate.

When a foundation's allowable rate is below the organization's actual overhead rate, the grant is partially subsidized by operating funds. The net revenue on that grant is less than the face value of the award.

The calculation every grants manager should run before accepting a foundation award: (grant amount) minus (unrecovered overhead). A $100,000 grant with 10% indirect allowed but an actual 25% overhead rate generates $100,000 in revenue against $125,000 in total program cost. After the indirect recovery, the net contribution to operations is $85,000, not $100,000. Across a portfolio of ten foundation grants under-recovering at 15 points, the annual structural deficit compounds into a real operating exposure.

This is not a reason to decline foundation grants. It is a reason to include the underrecovery calculation in financial planning and to build unrestricted fundraising goals that account for the gap.

## The Common Mistake That Costs the Most

The single most common failure in indirect cost management is not calculating the rate at all.

Organizations that have never run the calculation do not know whether they are recovering 10%, 30%, or 5% of their actual overhead through grants. The de minimis rate feels adequate because there is no comparison number. The NICRA process feels bureaucratic because the recovery gap has never been quantified.

The consequence is not a finding — no auditor will cite you for leaving indirect cost money on the table. The consequence is a structural deficit that compounds quietly over years until the operating reserve runs low and the organization faces a financial crisis that looks, from the outside, like a sudden problem.

Running the indirect cost rate calculation once — a half-day exercise using audited financial statements — produces the comparison number that drives the decision. If the actual rate is close to 15%, the de minimis rate is adequate. If the actual rate is 25% or 30%, the case for NICRA negotiation is significant.

## What to Do This Week

Pull your most recent audited financial statements. Identify your indirect cost pool: every organizational expense not directly charged to a specific program or grant. Sum it. Divide by your estimated MTDC base (total direct costs, minus equipment and subcontracts above $50,000). That is your actual indirect cost rate.

Compare it to what your current grants are recovering. If the gap is more than 10 points, the NICRA conversation with your cognizant agency is worth having.
