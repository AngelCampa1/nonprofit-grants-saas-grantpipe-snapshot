**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: if a cost is in the grant budget, it is allowable on a federal award.

Under 2 CFR Part 200, every cost charged to a federal grant must pass three separate tests simultaneously. A cost can appear in the approved budget and still be disallowed.

Test 1 — Allowability: the cost must not be explicitly prohibited under 2 CFR 200.420–200.475. Alcoholic beverages (200.423), entertainment (200.438), lobbying (200.450), and fundraising costs (200.442) are never allowable — regardless of what appears in the budget.

Test 2 — Allocability: the grant must have actually benefited from the cost. Charging 100% of a shared cost to one grant when multiple programs benefited is a common allocability error, even when the total amount is small.

Test 3 — Reasonableness: would a prudent person pay this amount for this good or service in the ordinary course of business? Above-market contracted services, procurements without competitive quotes above the micro-purchase threshold, and equipment that exceeds program requirements can produce partial disallowances based on reasonableness alone.

Budget approval does not substitute for any of these three tests. Document each cost on its own merits at the time of the transaction.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

The most common subaward budget modification error is not forgetting to file paperwork. It is approving a change before checking who actually has the authority to approve it.

Under 2 CFR 200.308(e)(4), pass-through entities can approve direct cost transfers across budget categories without federal prior approval — but only when the transfer does not exceed 10% of the total award budget and does not alter the project scope. That threshold is calculated against the total federal award, not the category being modified.

An organization with a $500,000 award can transfer up to $50,000 across categories on its own authority. Beyond that, a written request must go to the federal program officer before the subrecipient incurs costs under the new budget.

Approving first and seeking federal sign-off after is itself a compliance violation. Expenditures incurred under an unauthorized modification may be questioned or disallowed.

The second-most common error: treating a reallocation that funds an activity not in the original scope as a routine budget transfer. If the modified budget funds something new, federal prior approval is required regardless of the dollar amount.

Federal prior approval timelines vary by agency — HHS Grants Management often responds within 30 days; DOJ and HUD can take 60 to 90 days. Build those timelines into every subaward monitoring calendar from award day.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

The OMB Compliance Supplement is the document auditors use to decide what to test in your federal programs. Most nonprofit staff have never read it.

It is published annually by the Office of Management and Budget, organized by federal agency and CFDA/Assistance Listing number. For each significant federal program, it identifies which of 12 standard compliance requirements apply — Activities Allowed, Allowable Costs, Cash Management, Eligibility, Equipment, Matching, Period of Performance, Procurement, Program Income, Reporting, Subrecipient Monitoring, Special Tests and Provisions — and describes exactly what the auditor will examine.

Not all 12 requirements apply to every program. The Supplement designates each requirement as applicable ("A") or not applicable ("N/A") for each specific program. Auditors test the applicable ones.

For a nonprofit managing a HRSA health workforce grant under CFDA 93.884, reading the Supplement section for that program tells the organization precisely what documentation it needs to survive fieldwork — before the auditor arrives.

The Supplement is written for auditors, but its most practical use is audit preparation by the organizations being audited. Reading the sections covering your major programs is the most efficient pre-audit activity available.

It is updated annually. Check which version applies to your audit period.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

EPA awarded approximately $3.6 billion in environmental justice grants under the Inflation Reduction Act. Individual nonprofit grants ranged from $100,000 to $20 million.

Each of those awards comes with compliance requirements that go beyond standard nonprofit financial reporting.

EPA grants require environmental outcome metrics alongside financial data — community engagement counts, environmental improvements measured, program reach figures. There is no standard template for these in most nonprofit accounting systems. Tracking outcomes separately from financial records creates reconciliation work at every reporting period.

A significant portion of EPA funding reaches nonprofits through state environmental agencies as pass-through sub-awards. That structure means dual compliance: file reports with the state in the state's format, while maintaining documentation that meets federal audit standards if the award is reviewed at the federal level. Procurement rules, conflict-of-interest policies, and Title VI civil rights compliance requirements flow through from EPA to the sub-recipient.

USDA NRCS conservation grants — particularly through the Regional Conservation Partnership Program, which obligated over $4 billion in FY2023 — add project-phase complexity. Annual financial reports must document expenditures by phase, tied to approved budget line items and conservation plan milestones.

Most environmental nonprofits manage this compliance load with general-purpose tools that were not designed for it.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

Before issuing a sub-award, the first question to answer in writing is: is this organization a subrecipient or a contractor?

The 2 CFR 200.331 five-factor test determines the classification. Subrecipient indicators include: the entity determines who is eligible for the federal assistance, its performance is measured against federal program objectives, it has programmatic decision-making responsibility, it is accountable for federal compliance requirements, and it uses the funds to carry out its own program.

Contractor indicators are the opposite: the entity provides goods or services in normal business operations, serves many purchasers, operates in a competitive market, and is not subject to federal program compliance requirements.

The classification matters because the monitoring obligations in 2 CFR 200.332 — pre-award risk assessment, ongoing financial report review, documentation desk review for medium/high-risk organizations, site visits for high-risk subrecipients, and fac.gov single audit verification for organizations expending $1,000,000 or more in federal awards — apply to subrecipients, not contractors.

**Post 8 — COMPARISON_INSIGHT**

A realistic first-year cost for a mid-sized nonprofit implementing Salesforce NPSP: implementation partner fees ($20,000–$60,000 depending on scope), AppExchange packages for grant management or financial integration ($2,000–$12,000/year), and admin support either internally or externally ($5,000–$20,000). Total first-year cost for organizations at the $500K–$10M budget range commonly lands between $50,000 and $200,000.

A 2024 NTEN survey found that 64% of nonprofits with budgets under $5M reported their Salesforce configuration was not meeting their needs — most commonly because they lacked in-house admin capacity to maintain and improve it.

NPSP includes grant records and deadline tracking. What it does not include is post-award compliance: budget vs. actual tracking against approved grant budgets, restricted fund balance monitoring, and funder-facing financial reports. Getting those capabilities requires purchasing a specialized AppExchange app or building custom functionality — both of which add cost and ongoing admin requirements.

Most mid-sized nonprofits discover this gap after go-live, when the first funder financial report is due.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five questions that separate self-serve donor management platforms from ones that sound self-serve until you try to run them:

Can a staff member change a field label or add a custom attribute without opening a support ticket or writing code? If the answer is no, routine configuration requires outside help within 12–18 months of deployment.

Can the organization migrate donor data from a spreadsheet or legacy CRM without hiring a migration specialist? Most CRM imports fail not because of the tool but because of inconsistent household formatting in the source file — look for a platform that surfaces those errors at preview time, before any record writes.

Does the platform produce a segmented donor retention report — major, mid-level, general — automatically, or does it require a manual export and calculation? Only 33.3% of nonprofits rate their CRM systems as effective, per the 2025 Fifty & Fifty Nonprofit Peer Report. Reporting is where the gap shows up most clearly.

Is grant compliance included, or does managing restricted funds require a second tool? For nonprofits where grants represent more than 25% of revenue, the second tool becomes a weekly reconciliation burden.

What is the first-year total cost, including migration, training, and any supplementary software — not just the subscription? A platform that costs $99/month but requires $30,000 in implementation is not a $99/month platform.
