# What Donor Retention Reports Should Actually Tell Your Board

The quarterly board packet lands and the development director flips to the fundraising page. Total gifts received: $312,000. Same slide as last quarter, different number.

That number does not tell the board whether the donor base is healthy or eroding. It does not distinguish between an organization that retained 70% of its donors and grew revenue from existing relationships versus one that retained 35% and spent heavily on new acquisition to offset losses. Both can show identical totals in a given quarter.

The metrics below take roughly the same time to report as total revenue, assuming the CRM tracks giving history year over year. They produce fundamentally different board conversations.

## Why Total Revenue Conceals the Real Picture

The Fundraising Effectiveness Project has consistently documented that nonprofits in aggregate lose more donors than they gain each year. The organizations that grow despite this pattern retain existing donors at above-average rates. The ones that stagnate or decline are often replacing lapsed donors with new acquisition — which costs money and masks the structural problem in the revenue line.

A board that sees only total revenue cannot ask the right questions. "How do we grow revenue?" is not the same question as "why did we retain 38% of general donors last quarter and what specifically are we doing differently?" The first question produces strategy conversations. The second produces corrective action.

Donor retention rate is the single most useful leading indicator of long-term revenue stability. An organization with strong current revenue but declining retention is heading for a funding gap that the revenue number will not reveal until it arrives. Boards that track retention quarterly can intervene before the gap materializes.

## The Four Metrics That Matter

**Overall retention rate, with year-over-year trend.** The calculation is straightforward: donors who gave this year who also gave last year, divided by total donors who gave last year. The sector average is approximately 45%, meaning more than half of donors from any given year do not return the following year. Organizations above 60% have strong donor relationship programs.

The trend matters as much as the rate. Retention at 48% this year versus 46% last year looks like minor improvement. If retention was 55% three years ago, the year-over-year comparison is misleading — the trend is downward. Present retention on a rolling view covering the past four quarters or twelve months.

**Retention rate segmented by giving level.** Most organizations track at least three segments: major donors (typically $1,000+ annually), mid-level ($250–$999), and general (under $250). Each segment has a different expected range and a different response if it falls below it.

Major donor retention should be 80% or higher. If it drops below that threshold, the board needs to know immediately — major donors typically represent a disproportionate share of total revenue. Mid-level retention in the 50–60% range is strong; this segment often represents the largest improvement opportunity because mid-level donors respond to personal outreach but rarely receive it. General donor retention at 40–50% is sector average; below 35% signals a systemic problem with acknowledgment timing, communication frequency, or impact reporting.

Segmented retention shifts the board conversation from "how is fundraising going" to "which specific segment needs attention and what should change." That is a board-level strategy conversation.

**LYBUNT count with estimated recapture value.** LYBUNT — Last Year But Unfortunately Not This — identifies donors who gave in the immediately preceding year but have not given in the current year. They have a recent relationship with the organization. Recapture rates for LYBUNT donors targeted with specific outreach are typically 15–25%.

The board does not need the acronym. They need the translation: "We have 200 donors who gave last year and have not yet given this year. At their average prior gift of $150 and a 20% recapture rate, a targeted campaign could recover approximately $6,000." A board that sees a dollar figure can decide whether to fund a recapture initiative. A board that sees a percentage cannot.

SYBUNT donors — Some Years But Unfortunately Not This — gave in prior years but not the most recent year. Their connection is more distant; recapture rates run 5–10%. The pool is larger. SYBUNT recapture requires a different approach: reintroduction to current work, not a renewal ask. Both counts belong in the quarterly report because they represent different revenue opportunities with different outreach strategies.

**Average gift size, with trend.** An average gift increase from $180 to $195 looks positive in isolation. If that increase is driven by a single large gift while median gift size is declining, the trend data reveals it. Present average gift size with a year-over-year comparison and a rolling view. When average gift is rising alongside retention, the donor relationship is deepening. When average gift is rising while retention is declining, the organization is becoming more dependent on fewer, larger donors — a concentration risk the board should understand.

## The Framework: Four Numbers, One Narrative, One Table

Board members read in volume. A donor retention report that requires 15 minutes of close reading will not receive careful attention in a board meeting.

The format that works: one page, four key metrics at the top in dashboard layout, one paragraph of narrative context in the middle, and a segmented retention table at the bottom. Supporting detail — the full donor list, gift-by-gift breakdown, LYBUNT roster — goes in an appendix available on request.

The top section is four numbers with context: total active donors (with year-over-year change), overall retention rate (with trend direction), LYBUNT count with estimated recapture value in dollars, average gift size (with trend direction).

The narrative paragraph is specific, not general. Not "fundraising is strong" but "major donor retention declined 8 points to 74%. Three major donors from the capital campaign cycle completed their pledges and have not renewed at a sustaining level. The development director recommends personal outreach to all three within 30 days." The board can act on a specific recommendation. They cannot act on a status summary.

The segmented table shows retention rate for major, mid-level, and general donors with year-over-year comparison. Three rows, three columns. Preparation time under 30 minutes if the CRM tracks the data.

## The Most Common Mistake: Annual Reporting

Annual donor reports arrive too late to change course within the fiscal year. If retention drops 8 points in Q1 and the board does not see it until the annual report in Q4, nine months of corrective action opportunity is gone.

Quarterly retention reporting gives the board advance notice: approve a recapture campaign before the window closes, question a development strategy that is producing declining results, or reallocate resources between acquisition and retention before the budget cycle ends. The preparation overhead is minimal if the CRM tracks the data — approximately 1–2 hours per quarter to pull the numbers, write the narrative paragraph, and format the board packet page.

If the current reporting cadence is annual, the practical starting point is adding a single quarterly one-page retention summary to the next board meeting agenda. It does not require restructuring the entire board reporting process.

## Translating Retention Into Dollars for Board Decisions

Finance boards are comfortable with dollar figures and uncomfortable with sector benchmarks. Lead with the financial consequence, not the metric.

"Our retention rate is 48%" produces a note in the board minutes. "Roughly half our donors from last year did not give again, which represents approximately $48,000 in revenue we need to replace through new acquisition or lapsed donor outreach — and new acquisition costs money to produce" produces a decision about strategy.

The calculation takes 90 seconds: total lapsed donors multiplied by average prior gift equals the revenue gap. Run it every quarter as part of the standard retention summary. When the board consistently sees the dollar consequence of retention trends, they develop the intuition to ask better questions about development strategy — including questions about whether acquisition spending is efficiently replacing lapsed revenue or merely obscuring a systemic retention problem.

## What the CRM Should Do Automatically

Generating these four metrics should take under an hour if the CRM tracks donor history and year-over-year giving. Platforms with native retention reporting — automatic retention rate calculation, LYBUNT and SYBUNT identification, giving-level segmentation, rolling trend views — eliminate the manual work of exporting two years of donor lists, matching records in a spreadsheet, and calculating who is missing.

If pulling a segmented retention report from the current system takes more than an hour, that is a platform limitation worth factoring into the next CRM evaluation. Board-ready donor reporting is a basic function, not an advanced feature. CRMs that require a consultant or custom report builder to produce retention segmentation by giving level have an ongoing cost embedded in every board meeting.

The capabilities to verify before selecting a platform: automatic retention rate calculation with year-over-year comparison, LYBUNT and SYBUNT identification from giving history, giving-level segmentation without manual filtering, rolling twelve-month or four-quarter trend views, and exportable formatting that fits a one-page board packet without reformatting.

## One Practical Change for the Next Board Meeting

If none of these metrics are currently in the board packet, the lowest-friction starting point is the LYBUNT count with a dollar estimate. Export last year's donor list and this year's donor list, match on email address, count the ones in last year's list who are absent from this year's, multiply by average prior gift, and add two sentences to the current board narrative.

That single addition shifts the board's understanding of development performance from revenue received to relationship health. From that starting point, segmented retention and trend data follow naturally as reporting cadence and CRM capability allow.
