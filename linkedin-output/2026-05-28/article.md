# What to Do When You Find a Misposting on a Closed Federal Grant

A grants manager at a mid-sized workforce development nonprofit discovers in October, during annual audit prep, that a $14,000 personnel cost was charged to a federal DOL grant that closed in June. The expense was incurred in March — inside the period of performance — but was posted to the wrong award. The correct award is still open. The problem is the closed one.

This is a post-closeout cost transfer situation. It is also one of the most procedurally demanding compliance scenarios a grantee organization can face — and one of the most preventable.

## Why Post-Closeout Transfers Are Different

A standard cost transfer happens while an award is open. The grantee identifies a misposting, prepares a journal entry with supporting documentation, and corrects the record — typically within 90 days of the original transaction, which is the timeliness standard cited in NIH Grants Policy and most federal agency cost transfer policies.

A post-closeout transfer involves a closed award, which means the financial records have already been finalized and submitted. When a grantee submitted the final SF-425 Federal Financial Report, it certified those figures. Adjusting them after the fact requires admitting an error in a certified submission — and it requires written federal prior approval before a single correcting journal entry can be booked.

Under 2 CFR 200.344, grantees have 120 calendar days after the period of performance ends to submit final reports and liquidate obligations. An award that cleared that window without the error being caught is, for practical purposes, administratively closed. A post-closeout transfer then requires explicit federal agency authorization — and that authorization is not guaranteed.

The difference in consequence: a timely cost transfer correction is a documentation exercise. A post-closeout transfer is a compliance event.

## The 120-Day Window Is the Central Variable

The first thing to determine when a potential misposting surfaces on a closed award is where that award stands in the closeout calendar.

An award whose final reports have been submitted but not yet accepted by the federal agency is still in an open administrative state. A correction at this stage — with agency notification — is far less fraught than one attempted after full administrative closure.

An award inside the 120-day window but with final reports not yet submitted is in the most favorable position. There is still time to correct the books, update the financial report before it goes out, and avoid a post-closeout situation entirely.

An award that has been fully closed — final reports accepted, drawdowns reconciled — requires the full prior approval process. This is the scenario most organizations face when the error surfaces during audit preparation six to eight months after fiscal year end.

The lesson: monthly reconciliation that checks expenditures against period-of-performance dates catches these errors while the award is still open and correctable through normal procedures.

## The Prior Approval Process

### The Four-Phase Approach to Post-Closeout Corrections

**Phase 1: Document the error completely before contacting anyone.**

Identify exactly which cost needs to transfer: the original posting date, the dollar amount, the account it currently sits in, the grant it was incorrectly charged to, and the grant it should have been charged to. Confirm that the receiving grant's period of performance included the date the expense was incurred. A cost incurred outside that window cannot be transferred in — no authorization will change that.

**Phase 2: Contact the federal program officer and grants management specialist immediately, in writing.**

Early, transparent notification is treated more favorably than a correction request that arrives after auditors find the problem. An email that describes the situation clearly — the amount, the affected awards, what happened, and what correction you're requesting — starts the clock on a documented, proactive response.

Do not attempt to book a correcting entry before this contact. An unauthorized journal entry on a closed federal award creates a second compliance problem on top of the original misposting.

**Phase 3: Prepare a complete prior approval request package.**

The package should include: a written narrative explaining the error and proposed correction, copies of the original expenditure documentation (invoice, payroll register, or both), copies of the general ledger entries showing where the cost is currently coded, a statement that the expenditure was incurred during the period of performance of the receiving grant, and confirmation that the cost is allowable, allocable, and reasonable under both awards' terms.

A request that arrives without supporting documentation generates follow-up questions that add weeks to the review timeline. Include everything in the initial submission.

**Phase 4: Wait for written authorization from an official with grants management authority.**

A program officer's verbal agreement or a helpful email saying "sounds fine" is not sufficient. The authorization must come from the grants management specialist, contracting officer, or an equivalent official with financial authority — on agency letterhead or official agency email. Average response times range from 30 to 90 days, with HHS and NSF generally faster and DOJ or HUD taking longer for complex requests.

## The Most Common Mistakes

**Booking the entry without written approval.** This is the most consequential error. The correcting journal entry creates a second unauthorized transaction. Always wait.

**Assuming the program officer can authorize the correction.** Program officers are typically knowledgeable about the programmatic substance of awards, but they do not hold independent financial authority to approve post-closeout adjustments. The grants management specialist or contracting officer must be involved.

**Missing the FFR amendment.** If the transfer changes the figures reported on the final SF-425, the final FFR is misstated and must be amended. Leaving the original FFR in place while correcting the general ledger creates a permanent inconsistency between the organization's books and the federal record. The amended FFR submission should be coordinated with the grants management specialist before the corrected financial records are finalized.

**Inadequate documentation of root cause.** Federal auditors reviewing a post-closeout transfer will look for evidence that the organization identified what went wrong and changed the process. A file that contains only the approval letter and the correcting entry, without any analysis of how the error occurred, signals that the failure mode is still present.

## After Authorization Arrives

Once written authorization is in hand, book the transfer entry. Credit the grant that currently holds the cost — returning the expenditure — and debit the correct grant. Attach the federal authorization letter, the original expenditure documentation, and the prior approval request to the journal entry record. Date the entry accurately; do not backdate to the original expenditure date.

File the complete documentation package in both the closed award's file and the receiving award's file. Auditors will look for it in both places.

Then document the root cause. What reconciliation process should have caught this error before closeout? At what point in the award's lifecycle would the misposting have been visible if someone had been looking? What changes to the monthly close or the grant expenditure review will prevent recurrence?

This memo does not need to be long. It needs to be honest and specific. An auditor reviewing a post-closeout transfer wants to see that the organization understands what failed and has addressed it — not a general statement about improving internal controls.

## The Single Audit Disclosure Question

If the transfer involved a material amount, or if the error reflects a systemic weakness in cost allocation controls, the auditor may include it as a finding in the Single Audit. Proactive disclosure to the auditor before fieldwork begins — rather than waiting for them to discover it — changes how the finding is characterized.

Auditors treat self-disclosed corrected errors differently from errors they identify first. An organization that found the problem, got federal authorization, corrected the books, amended the FFR, and documented root cause has demonstrated functional controls even in the presence of an error. An organization where the auditor found it first has demonstrated the opposite.

## What This Event Signals About the Monthly Close

Post-closeout cost transfers are the visible symptom of a reconciliation process that did not catch the error in time. Organizations with strong monthly practices — reconciling grant expenditures against period-of-performance dates before each close, running budget-vs-actual by award every month, reviewing any cost that posts to a grant in its final quarter — rarely encounter this situation.

The 120-day closeout window exists precisely to provide time for final reconciliation. Organizations that use that window for administration only — submitting final reports and drawing down remaining funds — rather than for a final budget and expenditure review are most vulnerable to discovering errors in audit prep six months later.

Building a grant-by-grant expenditure review into the monthly close is not an onerous process for most mid-sized organizations. It takes a few hours per close and eliminates the category of problem entirely.

## This Week

Pull the period-of-performance end dates for any federal awards that closed in the last twelve months. Confirm whether the 120-day closeout window is still open for any of them. If a cost review hasn't happened since closeout, run one now. Any mispostings discovered in the next few weeks can still be corrected with standard procedures. Discovered during audit prep, they become post-closeout transfer events.

For ongoing awards, set a reminder to run an expenditure-against-period-of-performance review in the final 60 days before each grant's end date. Catching a misposting with 45 days left in the period is a 30-minute correction. Catching it after closeout is a 90-day approval process.
