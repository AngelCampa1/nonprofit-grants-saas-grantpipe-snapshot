# Grant Compliance 101: What Actually Happens After You Win the Grant

The award letter arrives. The organization celebrates. Then, within a week, someone asks: "So what do we need to do now?"

That question marks the beginning of a multi-year operational obligation that most organizations are less prepared for than they were for the application itself. Grant compliance — what funders actually require after an award, and what happens when those requirements are not met — gets a fraction of the attention that proposal writing receives. The result is that compliance failures are more common than application rejections at many mid-sized nonprofits.

This article covers what post-award compliance actually requires, why restricted funds are the centerpiece of the compliance obligation, where the most expensive failures occur, and how to build a compliance posture that holds up through the entire grant period.

## The Reverse Pipeline

The application process has a clear goal: win the award. The post-award process has a different kind of goal: satisfy ongoing legal and financial obligations for a period that may last two to four years, plus additional time if the organization is selected for audit.

A federal grant with a 24-month performance period will typically generate: a grant agreement execution, an initial budget submission, quarterly SF-425 Federal Financial Reports, semi-annual or annual programmatic reports, budget modification requests, and a final report with supporting documentation. Each step has deadlines. Missing a reporting deadline can trigger a significant deficiency finding. Significant deficiency findings affect future eligibility.

Foundation grants vary more. Some require only an annual narrative report. Others require quarterly updates, site visits, and detailed financial reconciliations. The grant agreement specifies what is required. Reading it before the funds arrive — not when the first report is due — is the first compliance decision an organization makes.

## Restricted Funds: The Core Obligation

When a funder awards a $75,000 grant for a workforce development program, that money carries a restriction. It can only be spent on workforce development program expenses as defined in the approved budget and grant terms. Spending it on general operating costs — even temporarily, with every intention of repaying it — is a compliance violation.

Restricted fund accounting requires tracking grant dollars separately from unrestricted revenue. Every expenditure charged to the grant must meet three tests:

Allowable — the cost type is permitted under the grant terms and applicable cost principles. Federal grants use 2 CFR 200 (the Uniform Guidance) as the cost principles framework. Foundation grants specify their own allowable costs.

Allocable — the expense is directly attributable to the approved program activities. A salary charged to a workforce development grant should represent time actually spent on workforce development program work, documented accordingly.

Documented — the organization can produce records that support the expenditure. For personnel costs under federal grants, that means time records identifying the grant or cost objective for each hour worked — not a general timesheet that reads "40 hours, program work."

The most common compliance failure is fund commingling: grant dollars deposited into the general operating account and tracked only by a spreadsheet that someone updates irregularly. When an auditor asks for documentation of how $75,000 was spent, the answer cannot be "we believe it went to these things." The auditor needs to trace each dollar from the grant account to a specific, allowable, documented expenditure.

## The Three Most Expensive Compliance Failures

Audit findings in federal grant programs cluster in predictable categories. Understanding where failures happen is more useful than a general instruction to "maintain good records."

**Personnel cost documentation.** Salaries charged to grants must be supported by time records that identify the grant or cost objective for each hour. Under 2 CFR 200.430, federal grants require that payroll charges reflect an after-the-fact determination of actual activity. A time sheet completed weeks after the pay period — particularly one that shows the same allocation pattern every period — raises audit questions. Organizations with multiple grants and staff who split time across programs need a time-tracking discipline that captures actual activity, not estimated allocations.

**Budget modification violations.** Most grants allow reallocation of funds between budget categories up to a threshold, with anything above that threshold requiring advance funder approval. Federal awards under the Uniform Guidance permit up to 10% rebudgeting without prior approval in most cases, though some programs and DOJ awards may have stricter thresholds that are specified in the Notice of Award. Organizations that spend down one budget line and begin charging costs to another — without confirming the threshold or requesting approval — create findings that can result in cost disallowances.

**Subrecipient monitoring gaps.** When a nonprofit passes federal funds through to a subgrantee or contractor, the primary grantee retains compliance responsibility for how those funds are used. This means conducting risk assessments, reviewing subrecipient expenditure reports, and monitoring subgrantee compliance with award conditions including confidentiality requirements. Pass-through entity liability is real: findings at the subrecipient level become findings at the primary grantee level.

## The Compliance Calendar as Operating Infrastructure

Federal grants require two parallel reporting tracks. Financial reports — the SF-425 Federal Financial Report — go to the grant's financial management system on a quarterly or semi-annual schedule. Programmatic reports go to the program officer on a separate schedule with different deadlines.

For an organization managing five active federal grants, that can mean 20 or more distinct reporting deadlines per year, not including foundation reports. Each deadline has a different submission method, a different contact, and a different format.

Organizations that manage this with Outlook reminders and shared spreadsheets function until staff turnover or workload concentration creates a miss. A single missed SF-425 deadline can generate a significant deficiency finding. Two missed deadlines in the same grant period can trigger enhanced monitoring.

The compliance calendar is not an administrative convenience. It is operating infrastructure. It should be populated at grant setup — before the first expenditure — with every deadline for the full period of performance. Not just the final report deadline.

## The Reporting Threshold That Changes Everything

Organizations that expend $1,000,000 or more in federal awards in a fiscal year must undergo a Single Audit under the Uniform Guidance (2 CFR 200 Subpart F). The Single Audit examines both financial statements and compliance with specific federal program requirements.

Crossing the $1,000,000 threshold for the first time is a significant compliance event. The audit scope changes, the documentation requirements become more formal, and findings carry consequences that ripple beyond the affected grant. A GAO analysis from April 2024 (GAO-24-106173) found that roughly 17% of all federal award spending from 2017 to 2021 was linked to single audit findings that were both severe and persistent.

Organizations approaching this threshold for the first time should treat it as a readiness milestone, not a surprise. The audit preparation that a Single Audit requires — organized grant files, complete time records, documented expenditure methodology, reconciled restricted-fund balances — is the same preparation that good compliance management produces as a natural output.

## The Framework: Four Pillars of Compliance Readiness

Mid-sized organizations managing multiple grants across different funders need a compliance framework they can actually operate without a dedicated compliance staff. The four-pillar structure that works at this scale:

**Fund separation.** Every grant has its own accounting code or project code. Expenditures are coded to grants in real time, not at month-end. Restricted balances are visible from the accounting system without manual compilation.

**Documentation discipline.** Personnel costs have contemporaneous time records. Direct costs have invoices, receipts, or purchase orders filed in the grant record. Budget modifications are documented and approved before costs shift between categories.

**Reporting calendar management.** Every reporting deadline for every active grant is in one place, assigned to a named owner, with a preparation window scheduled in advance. The calendar includes both financial and programmatic reporting tracks.

**Audit-ready grant files.** Each active grant has a complete file: award letter and all amendments, all submitted reports with submission confirmation, all expenditure documentation, time records, and the current compliance calendar. The test: could a new grants manager pick up any active grant file and understand the compliance status within an hour?

## The Most Common Mistake: Treating Setup as an Afterthought

The setup work done when a grant is received determines how easy or difficult compliance management will be for the entire period. Organizations that enter a grant name, a total award amount, and a final report deadline have created a record, not a compliance tool.

A compliance-ready setup includes: the exact project period start and end dates from the Notice of Award; the approved budget broken down by category; the restriction type; the complete reporting schedule with every deadline for the full period; funder contact information; prior approval requirements from the award terms; and the accounting system grant code, confirmed with finance before any invoices are approved.

The hour spent on a complete setup at award receipt pays for itself many times over. An incomplete setup is corrected under time pressure, usually when the first report is due and someone realizes the compliance record does not reflect what actually happened.

## What to Do This Week

If your organization has any active grants, run the four-pillar check on each one. For each active award: confirm that expenditures are coded against the correct grant in the accounting system; confirm that time records are current for any staff charging time to the award; pull the compliance calendar and confirm that every upcoming deadline has a named owner and a preparation date scheduled; and open the grant file to verify that submitted reports have saved confirmation documentation.

The gaps this exercise surfaces are the gaps an auditor would find. Finding them now is better than finding them when a program officer asks an unexpected question or a funder schedules a site visit.
