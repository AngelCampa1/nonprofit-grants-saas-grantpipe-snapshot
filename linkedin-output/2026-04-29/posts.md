**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

Audit fees for organizations stuck at the 40 percent major-program coverage threshold typically run $8,000 to $20,000 higher per cycle than for comparable low-risk auditees.

The five low-risk criteria under 2 CFR 200.520 are mostly inside management's control: a clean Single Audit two years running, no material weaknesses in internal controls over major programs, no GAGAS qualified opinion, no fraud findings affecting major programs, and timely Federal Audit Clearinghouse submission.

The catch is that you cannot reverse-engineer eligibility at audit time. The two prior fiscal years either qualify or they do not.

The shops that consistently keep low-risk status track the criteria themselves quarter by quarter, the same way they track cash. The shops that lose it find out from the auditor in March.

Per the AICPA Government Audit Quality Center benchmarking work, the additional testing required for non-low-risk auditees is the single largest avoidable line item in most Single Audit engagements.

GrantPipe surfaces low-risk status indicators alongside grant compliance so the criteria do not get rediscovered at fieldwork.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: interest earned on a federal advance drawdown counts as program income.

Reality: it does not. Program income is governed by 2 CFR 200.307 and means revenue earned by the recipient that is directly generated by activities supported by the federal award. Interest on cash advances is a separate animal handled under 2 CFR 200.305(b)(9), which requires recipients to remit interest above $500 per year to the HHS Program Support Center.

The two get conflated because both involve money sitting in the recipient's bank account that was not in the original budget. They are not the same thing and they get reported on different schedules.

The other place this matters: the additive-versus-deductive treatment election under 2 CFR 200.307. Once made at award time, that election is binding for the life of the award. Most program officers never ask about it until the auditor does, and by then changing it requires retroactive correction.

If your sub-award says nothing about program income treatment, that is itself a finding waiting to happen.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Steps 5 and 6 of the in-kind contribution valuation workflow are where most organizations create their next audit finding.

Step 5 is the journal entry. Under ASU 2020-07, debit the asset account (food inventory, supplies, property) or the relevant expense account if the goods are consumed in program delivery, and credit contribution revenue with or without donor restrictions depending on intent. Do not net in-kind gifts against expenses. Netting is the most common error and it understates both revenue and program expense on the 990.

Step 6 is the donor acknowledgment letter. IRS rules require written acknowledgment for any contribution of $250 or more. For in-kind gifts, the letter describes the goods or services in enough detail to support the donor's deduction but does not state a value — that is the donor's responsibility to substantiate.

The standard took effect for fiscal years beginning after June 15, 2021. Most organizations updated their journal entries and forgot to update their footnote disclosures. Audit firms catch this in year three.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Pass-through entity, defined at 2 CFR 200.1: a non-federal entity that receives a federal award and provides a sub-award to a subrecipient that carries out part of the federal program.

Pass-through status is functional, not optional. The moment a recipient issues a sub-award, it becomes a pass-through entity for that portion of the award and inherits the responsibilities under 2 CFR 200.332.

Two examples that show how broad the definition is. A state department of health receives a $5M HHS grant and sub-awards $3M to 12 county health agencies — the state is the pass-through. A nonprofit receives a $1.2M DOL workforce grant and sub-awards $400,000 to a training partner for one program track — the nonprofit is a pass-through for that $400,000.

The 2 CFR 200.332 obligations include classifying every downstream relationship as subrecipient or contractor, performing risk assessments before issuing sub-awards, and monitoring throughout the period of performance. Pass-through amounts also flow onto the SEFA filed with the Federal Audit Clearinghouse.

If your organization writes checks to a partner under a federal grant, you are probably a pass-through entity. The right time to find out is before the agreement is signed.

**Post 5 — TOOL_TEARDOWN**

Neon One is not a single product. It is a portfolio assembled through acquisition: Neon CRM at the center, Arts People for arts organizations, Rallybound for peer-to-peer, and a few others stacked around them.

The pitch is vendor consolidation. The reality for buyers is uneven module quality and a UX that varies depending on which acquired team built the surface you are looking at. Cross-module reporting is the place this shows up first — pulling a clean view across donor activity, event registrations, and grant tracking usually requires either a custom report build or an export-to-Excel step.

For organizations that mostly need donor management, Neon CRM on its own is a reasonable choice. For organizations that need donor CRM plus active grant management in one system, the bundle approach asks the buyer to integrate modules that were not designed together.

The tradeoff to know going in: you trade a single login for a sprawl that grows as Neon One acquires more products. Mid-sized nonprofits that already feel stretched thin tend to feel that sprawl most.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

Church donor management runs on a weekly cadence — tithes, offerings, designated gifts — not the quarterly cycle most nonprofit CRMs assume.

A typical secular nonprofit drops appeal letters in Q4, runs an end-of-year campaign, and cultivates major donors across a 12-month plan. A church processes giving every Sunday and every online gift in between, with designated funds for building campaigns, missions, benevolence, and youth programs running in parallel year-round.

When the same church also receives grants for secular programs — early childhood, food pantry, after-school — the data model gets more complicated. Restricted grant funds cannot be commingled with the General Fund or the Building Fund in the books, even if one bank account holds all of it.

Most church-specific tools handle the giving side well and ignore grants entirely. Most nonprofit CRMs handle grants and treat weekly tithing as an afterthought. The shops that get this right pick a system that separates donor giving records from restricted grant funds at the data layer, then maps both up to the appropriate fund balance under FASB ASC 958.

If your accounting system and your donor system disagree about which fund a check belongs to, you have a reconciliation problem waiting to happen.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

A sub-award that is missing a required provision is a compliance finding even when the program runs perfectly.

2 CFR 200.332(a) lists the data elements that every sub-award document must contain before execution: federal award identification number, federal award date, sub-award amount, federal award project description, name of the federal awarding agency, CFDA/Assistance Listing number and title, indirect cost rate, and a statement of applicable requirements. Missing any one of them is a citable finding regardless of how well the subrecipient performs.

The most common gap on the audited list is the indirect cost rate — pass-through entities often leave it blank or use language that does not reference the negotiated rate or the de minimis 15 percent rate at 2 CFR 200.414(f).

The Subrecipient Agreement Checklist walks through every required provision under 2 CFR 200.332(a), the additional provisions that reduce monitoring risk, and program-specific additions that some federal programs require on top of the baseline. Use it before the agreement is signed, not after.

**Post 9 — QUESTION_POST**

A grant writer starts the year with 30 to 50 prospects on a spreadsheet. By Q2 the deadlines have shifted, two program officers have changed jobs, the organization has pivoted one program, and the spreadsheet has not kept up.

The writing itself is maybe 70 percent of the role. The other 30 is pipeline hygiene — tracking deadlines, reusing narrative content across submissions, and handing off won awards to the grants manager with the budget intact.

The handoff is where most awarded grants quietly start losing money. The proposal said one thing, the grants manager inherits another, and by the second drawdown the budget categories no longer line up with what the funder approved.

Question for the grant writers and development directors here: when you win a grant, what does your handoff to compliance actually look like? Is it a meeting, a folder, an email thread, or a system that carries the budget and narrative forward without retyping?

The answer says a lot about how your post-award reporting will go six months from now.

**Post 10 — PRODUCT_INSIGHT**

The average mid-sized nonprofit uses three donor segments — everyone, major donors, and lapsed donors. Organizations with strong retention programs use fifteen or more.

The difference is not the technology. It is whether the segmentation tool stays current as donor records change, or whether it requires a manual Excel export every time someone wants a list.

Donor segmentation in GrantPipe is a saved-filter system. Build a segment once using any combination of giving history, recency, source, fund, custom fields, and demographics. The segment stays current automatically as gifts post and contact records update. A segment named "Major Gift Prospects — Not Yet Asked" might filter on lifetime giving between $5,000 and $24,999, last gift within 18 months, no open ask in the pipeline, and a custom field flagging board referral.

The segments drive everything downstream — appeal lists, stewardship workflows, board reports, and renewal campaigns — without re-exporting. When a donor's giving crosses a threshold, they move into the new segment automatically the next time the filter runs.

If your org is operating on three segments because building more is too much work, the tool is the bottleneck.
