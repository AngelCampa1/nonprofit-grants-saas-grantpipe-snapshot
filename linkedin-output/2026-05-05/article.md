# The SF-SAC Dual-Signature Trap: Why Single Audits Sit Unfiled Past the 30-Day Deadline

The single audit is complete when the auditor signs the reports. The filing is complete when the SF-SAC clears the Federal Audit Clearinghouse. Those two events are not the same, and the gap between them is where organizations routinely miss the 30-day deadline.

Most organizations know the deadline exists. Most believe they are complying with it. What they miss is that the SF-SAC requires two separate, authenticated actions — and the second one cannot happen until the first is finished. When the auditee certifying official signs on day 28 and the auditor gets around to certifying on day 33, the submission is late. The public record shows it. Awarding agencies see it.

## The Dual-Signature Process

The SF-SAC is not a single form submitted by a single party. Under 2 CFR 200.512, the reporting package submitted to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse (fac.gov) requires both the auditee certifying official and the auditor to sign — separately, through their own FAC accounts.

The sequence is fixed. The auditee certifying official (typically the executive director or CFO) creates the submission in FAC, enters all required organizational and federal awards data, uploads the reporting package documents, and electronically signs the auditee certification. This action triggers a notification to the auditor. The auditor then independently logs into FAC, reviews the submitted data, and certifies the auditor section. The submission is not complete — and FAC will not mark it accepted — until both signatures are in place.

This is the single most common source of missed SF-SAC deadlines. Organizations submit the auditee portion and assume the auditor will handle the rest automatically. The auditor receives an email. The email may sit in a queue. The 30-day window closes.

## The Deadline Math

Under 2 CFR 200.512(a)(1), the submission deadline is the earlier of two dates: 30 days after the auditor's report date, or 9 months after the end of the fiscal year.

For a December 31 fiscal year end with an audit report dated April 15, the deadline is May 15 — the 30-day rule controls, and the 9-month deadline (September 30) is later.

For a June 30 fiscal year end with a report dated February 28, the 30-day deadline (March 30) is essentially concurrent with the 9-month deadline (March 31). Both land in the same window, but the 30-day rule controls.

The FAC timestamp records when the submission is fully complete — both signatures in place. This matters because "I submitted my portion on time" is not a defense. The submission date for public record purposes is the date the final signature is received.

OMB's 2024 revision to the Uniform Guidance (89 FR 30046) added provisions allowing federal awarding agencies to impose additional conditions on recipients with late or incomplete single audit submissions. The FAC itself does not impose a monetary penalty, but late filings are publicly searchable. Program officers see them.

## FAC Account Requirements

The FAC's 2023 transition from Census to GSA management (now at fac.gov) introduced Login.gov as the authentication mechanism for both parties. This created a new class of delay for organizations submitting their first single audit.

The auditee certifying official must have a Login.gov account. Identity verification through Login.gov can take 3–5 business days. For first-time filers, account setup should happen during audit fieldwork — not after the report is signed. FAC also requires a separate auditee authorization step that links the certifying official to the organization.

The auditor must be registered in FAC as an audit firm, and the individual CPA certifying must have a Login.gov account linked to that firm. Many audit firms pre-register during planning; many do not. Confirming auditor registration status before the report is signed eliminates a common last-week scramble.

The practical rule: both parties should have active FAC accounts and confirmed access before audit fieldwork begins. Treating account setup as a post-audit administrative task is how organizations find themselves doing identity verification with nine days left in the 30-day window.

## What the SF-SAC Contains

The SF-SAC is a data collection form, not the audit itself. It summarizes the audit in a structured format that allows oversight agencies and the public to search, compare, and monitor single audit results across thousands of organizations.

The form requires: general organizational information (EIN, UEI, fiscal year end, audit type, total federal expenditures); financial statement information including audit opinions and any going concern disclosures; federal awards data — each Assistance Listing Number, program name, expenditures, and whether the program was identified as a major program; findings information including finding reference numbers, types, federal program ALNs, questioned cost amounts, and whether findings are repeats; and the auditee and auditor certifications.

All data entered in FAC must be consistent with the uploaded reporting package documents. FAC runs limited automated validation, but inconsistencies between the SF-SAC data entries and the uploaded reports surface during oversight agency review. A finding numbered "2026-001" in the data field and "Finding 1" in the uploaded schedule causes rejection.

The Federal Audit Clearinghouse received approximately 30,000 single audit submissions in FY2023. Every one of them is publicly searchable at fac.gov.

## Common Errors That Delay Acceptance

Five errors account for most SF-SAC delays:

**Mismatched report dates.** The date entered in FAC must exactly match the date on the signed audit report. A one-day discrepancy triggers rejection. This sounds trivial. It happens regularly when the auditee enters the date from a draft report rather than the final signed version.

**Password-protected PDFs.** FAC requires accessible PDFs — not password protected, not encrypted. PDF/A compliance is required. Large documents should be compressed before upload; file size limits apply. Organizations that generate their reporting package from accounting software exports often produce PDFs that fail FAC's upload validation on the first attempt.

**Inconsistent finding numbers.** Finding reference numbers in the SF-SAC data entry must match the numbering in the uploaded schedule of findings exactly. If the data field reads "2026-001" and the document reads "Finding 2026-1," the submission fails validation.

**Missing corrective action plan.** Every finding requires a corresponding corrective action plan entry and an uploaded CAP document. Under 2 CFR 200.511(c), the CAP must include the finding number, the auditee's position, the specific corrective action, the responsible party, and the expected completion date. A finding without a complete CAP prevents submission completion. Vague responses — "management will strengthen controls" — may pass FAC's technical validation but create problems when awarding agencies review the substance.

**Auditor delay.** This is the most common reason deadlines are missed. After the auditee certifying official signs, the auditor receives an email notification to log in and certify. If that email is not acted upon within the remaining window, the submission stalls. The fix is coordination: before the auditee signs, confirm explicitly with the auditor what date they will certify. Build the certification date into the submission timeline. Do not treat it as a follow-on task.

## The Repeat Finding Risk

A finding that appears in two consecutive single audit cycles is treated differently by awarding agencies than a first-time finding. Repeat findings trigger specific conditions under 2 CFR 200.208 — prior approval for draws, enhanced monitoring, or high-risk designation. These conditions persist until the awarding agency is satisfied that the underlying problem has been resolved.

Breaking a repeat finding pattern typically requires more structural change than the corrective action plan for the first finding suggested. New staff, new systems, documented policy changes, or external controls review. Organizations that produce corrective action plans that address symptoms rather than causes produce repeat findings.

The SF-SAC data at fac.gov makes repeat findings visible across organizations and years. Program officers review this history when making funding decisions.

## The Single Most Preventable Outcome

Late SF-SAC submissions are overwhelmingly preventable with one practice: treat the auditor certification as a calendared commitment, not an assumption.

After the audit is complete and the report is signed, schedule the auditee certification date and the auditor certification date before either party acts. Confirm both dates with the auditor in writing. The auditee certifies first. The auditor certifies on the agreed date — at least three to five business days before the deadline, to allow for corrections if something fails validation.

The organizations that miss the 30-day window almost always had enough time. They ran out of coordination, not calendar.

## The Common Mistake

The single most common failure is treating the SF-SAC as an administrative task to complete after the audit is done, rather than a parallel process to coordinate during the audit's final phase.

The consequence is predictable: the report is signed, everyone breathes, and then someone initiates FAC access setup, discovers the auditor is not registered, realizes the PDF from accounting software is password-protected, and starts scrambling with 10 days left in the window. Ten days is not enough time to recover from all three of those simultaneously.

Start the process before the report is signed. Confirm FAC accounts, auditor registration, and document format before fieldwork is complete. The administrative setup is not complicated. The timing is.

## What to Do This Week

If your fiscal year ends in the next six months, confirm two things now: that your certifying official has an active Login.gov account linked to your organization in FAC, and that your audit firm is registered in FAC with at least one active Login.gov credential. If either is missing, initiate setup before audit fieldwork begins.

If your single audit is already in progress, schedule the auditee and auditor certification dates explicitly — in writing, with the audit firm — before the report is issued. This one step eliminates the most common source of missed deadlines.
