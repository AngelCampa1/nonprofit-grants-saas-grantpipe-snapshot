# Grant Closeout Record Retention: When the Clock Starts and What to Keep

A federal grant ends on September 30. The final SF-425 is submitted on January 28 of the following year, inside the 120-day window. Three Octobers later, someone on the finance team purges the file as part of an annual records cleanup, believing the three-year retention period has expired.

It hasn't. The retention clock started January 28, not September 30. The records were destroyed roughly four months too early. If a program review opens that grant in the next eight months, the organization has no defense.

This is the most common record retention failure at mid-sized nonprofits, and it has nothing to do with not knowing the rule. It has to do with not knowing when the rule starts running.

## The Three-Year Baseline and Its Exceptions

Under 2 CFR 200.334, the federal record retention baseline is three years from the date of submission of the final expenditure report. That date is the trigger — not the period-of-performance end date, not the closeout letter, not the date the auditor signed off on the single audit.

Three details change the calculation in practice.

The clock starts at submission. The grant period might end September 30. The final SF-425 might land in late January under the 120-day rule. The retention period runs from January, not September. A four-month difference is invisible until someone destroys records too early.

Program-specific requirements can extend the period. 2 CFR 200.334(b) explicitly allows longer retention when required by federal statute, regulation, or terms of a federal award. USDA grants commonly require seven years. Some HUD and DOE programs do too. Property records on equipment purchased with federal funds run until disposition is final, which can be far longer than the grant period itself.

Pending matters extend retention indefinitely. If a federal audit, litigation, claim, or program review is open at the three-year mark, retention continues until the matter is fully resolved. There is no automatic expiration. Before purging anything at year three, someone has to confirm in writing that no pending matters exist.

## Why Closeout Files Have Gaps

Files assembled under deadline pressure during closeout are almost always incomplete. The first quarter of the grant period is usually the thinnest section — onboarding documentation, initial procurement files, early time-and-effort records. The team was getting the program started, not building the audit binder.

By the time closeout arrives, some receipts have been thrown out. Some general ledger entries have no supporting documentation attached. T&E records covering staff who have left the organization are reconstructed from memory by people who weren't there. The file passes through closeout without scrutiny because closeout is a financial reconciliation, not an audit.

Then a single audit, monitoring visit, or program review opens that closed grant two years later, and the gaps surface.

The organizations that handle retention well treat closeout as the moment to finish the file, not the moment to file it away.

## The Closeout-as-Audit-Prep Framework

A defensible record retention practice has four named components. None of them is unusual. The discipline is doing all four every time, on every grant.

**1. Define the retention trigger date in writing.**

For each closed grant, record the actual date the final expenditure report was submitted. Not the period-of-performance end date. Not the closeout letter date. The submission date. Calculate the retention end date from there using the longer of: 2 CFR 200.334's three-year baseline, the program-specific period (check the grant agreement and the OMB Compliance Supplement), or the property records timeline if equipment was purchased.

Store the trigger date and the calculated end date as a structured field on the grant record. Never as a free-text note.

**2. Build the file by compliance area, not by document type.**

Organize the closed grant file the way auditors test it. The OMB Compliance Supplement structures testing around requirements: allowable costs and cost principles, cash management, eligibility, equipment and real property, matching, period of performance, procurement and suspension, program income, reporting, subrecipient monitoring, and any special tests applicable to the program.

A folder for each applicable compliance area. Documents filed against the area they support. When an auditor asks for the procurement file, you open the procurement folder. You don't search across a chronological dump of PDFs.

**3. Reconcile the file at closeout, not at audit.**

Before submitting the final SF-425, run a documentation review against the GL. Every expenditure posted to the grant should have supporting documentation in the file. Every direct charge above whatever the organization's threshold is should have an invoice or receipt. Every payroll allocation should have time-and-effort documentation behind it.

Items that can't be reconciled are the work product of the closeout. Either the documentation is found, or the cost is removed from the final report. Closing the grant with unreconciled costs in the GL is how questioned costs become disallowed costs years later.

**4. Lock the file and document the lock.**

Once closeout is complete, the file gets a hold. Read-only access for everyone except a designated records custodian. Any addition to the file post-closeout is logged with a date, an author, and a reason. Auditors will accept added documentation if there's a chain of custody. They will not accept a file that was edited freely for three years.

## The Single Most Common Mistake

The single most common record retention failure isn't destroying records too early. It's storing them in a way that makes them unfindable.

A closed grant file lives on a former program director's laptop. The laptop was reimaged when she left. Records existed at the moment of audit, but no one can produce them. From the auditor's perspective, that's the same as having destroyed them. The finding reads "documentation not provided," and the cost gets questioned.

The fix is institutional storage from day one of the grant. Not a personal drive. Not an email folder. Not a shared drive that no one has rights to after the program closes. A document management location owned by the organization, with permissions that survive staff turnover, and a retention policy that fires automatically at the calculated end date.

If the records exist but the only person who knows where they are is gone, the records effectively don't exist.

## A Worked Example

Consider a $400,000 HUD CoC grant with a period of performance ending June 30, 2026. The team submits the final SF-425 on October 18, 2026 (within the 120-day window). HUD CoC retention follows the standard three-year baseline.

The retention end date is October 18, 2029.

Equipment purchased under the grant — say, a $12,000 server — has its own clock. Property records for federally-funded equipment must be retained until disposition is final under 2 CFR 200.313. If the server is in service through 2031 and then donated to another nonprofit with HUD's approval, the property records run until the disposition documentation is filed in 2031.

The grant file as a whole can be purged in October 2029. The equipment records inside it cannot. A defensible system separates them at closeout so the equipment records survive after the rest of the file is destroyed.

## What to Do This Week

Pull a list of every federal grant your organization has closed in the past four years. For each one, confirm three things: the date the final expenditure report was submitted, the calculated retention end date based on the longer of 2 CFR 200.334 or any program-specific extension, and the location of the closed file.

Any grant where the location is a personal drive, an email folder, or "I think it's in the network somewhere" is a finding waiting to happen. Move those files into institutional storage this quarter.

For currently active grants, build the closeout-as-audit-prep practice into the closeout checklist now, not when the report is due. The reconciliation work is faster when documentation is fresh.
