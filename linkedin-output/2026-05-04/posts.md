**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

The 2024 OMB revision raised the Single Audit threshold to $1,000,000 — effective for fiscal years ending September 30, 2025 or later.

That change matters for how organizations track federal expenditure totals, not just whether they file. The threshold applies across all federal awards combined, not per grant. An organization holding three federal awards totaling $980,000 is below the line. At $1,000,001, a licensed independent auditor must complete and submit the Single Audit package to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse within 9 months of fiscal year end.

The same revision raised the de minimis indirect cost rate from 10% to 15% under 2 CFR 200.414 — a meaningful shift for organizations that have never negotiated a rate with a cognizant federal agency.

Organizations near the threshold should track cumulative federal expenditures by award, not just by program budget. Crossing the threshold mid-year triggers the Single Audit obligation for the entire fiscal year, not just the portion spent after crossing.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: entering the grant award amount in your system means the award is set up.

What most organizations actually enter: a grant name, a dollar total, and a due date. That is not a grant setup — it is a note.

A complete grant record has the project period start and end dates from the Notice of Award (not the application), the approved budget broken down by category, the restriction type, every reporting deadline for the full performance period, and the program officer contact. It also needs the accounting system grant code that links expenditures to this award in the GL.

The most common error in setup is using the application budget instead of the approved budget. Funders modify budgets at award time. An application that asked for $50,000 in personnel may be awarded with $42,000 in personnel and the remainder cut entirely. If the system holds the application budget, every budget-vs-actual report for the life of the award is wrong.

The hour invested in a complete setup at week one eliminates a recurring compliance debt that compounds across every expenditure, every report, and every audit.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Program income Step 3 under 2 CFR 200.307: establish the tracking ledger before any income-generating activity begins.

Program income is gross income earned by the grantee that is directly generated by activities supported by the federal award. Patient fees at a federally-funded clinic. Admission to a grant-funded training program. Rental of a federally-funded facility. The revenue is federal money earned under the award's authority — not unrestricted income, not a donation.

The ledger tracks the award number, income source description, date earned, amount, and cumulative total by period. Each of those fields is there because the Federal Financial Report SF-425 asks for cumulative program income earned and expended on Lines 10 and 10a — and auditors testing Single Audit compliance cross-reference those lines against the accounting records.

Program income deposited into the general operating account without a separate tracking mechanism becomes unrecoverable within 30 days. The accrual basis applies: record when earned, not when collected. A patient visit on September 30 generates program income on September 30 regardless of when the insurance payment arrives.

Under 2 CFR 200.307(e)(1), deductive treatment is the default when the award is silent on disposition. Plan the election at award time — changing it mid-award requires written federal agency prior approval.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Net assets without donor restrictions is not the same as cash available.

Under FASB ASC 958-210-45, every nonprofit's net assets fall into one of two classes: with-donor-restrictions or without-donor-restrictions. The without-restrictions class includes unrestricted contributions, program service revenue, government contract revenue, investment returns on unrestricted funds, and board-designated amounts. It is the default — funds land here unless a donor has imposed explicit conditions.

The term replaced "unrestricted net assets" through FASB ASU 2016-14, effective for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2017. The substance is identical; only the label changed. Using "unrestricted net assets" in an audited financial statement is now an error.

Board designations are the classification that creates the most confusion. A board that votes to designate $250,000 as an operating reserve has created an internal allocation — not a donor restriction. The board can rescind it by resolution at any time. That $250,000 remains within net assets without donor restrictions, disclosed in the footnotes, not moved to a separate net asset class.

FASB ASU 2016-14 added a required liquidity and availability footnote precisely because a $2 million without-restrictions balance may consist largely of fixed assets, vehicles, and long-term receivables. The footnote surfaces what is genuinely accessible within 12 months. High unrestricted net assets without the liquidity footnote tells auditors and board members almost nothing.

**Post 5 — TOOL_TEARDOWN**

Network for Good was acquired by Bonterra in 2022, alongside EveryAction, CommunityForce, and Social Solutions.

Before the acquisition, pricing was published in tiers. After the acquisition, pricing moved to a custom contract model requiring a demo. Reviews written before 2022 describe a different product structure than what current prospects encounter in a sales conversation.

What the platform does: fundraising pages, peer-to-peer campaigns, donor email tools. What it has never done: grant prospect tracking, restricted fund management, grant budget-versus-expenditure tracking, or compliance report generation. These were not features removed at acquisition — they were never part of the product.

Transaction fees may apply on processed donations in addition to the subscription cost. The specific fee structure is part of the negotiated contract, not disclosed publicly.

For nonprofits whose primary activity is campaign-based fundraising with no grant compliance requirements, the product is coherent. For organizations receiving restricted foundation or government grants, grant compliance still lives elsewhere — usually in a spreadsheet.

The evaluation question is not whether the platform works for fundraising. It is whether your organization's workflow requires a system that handles restricted grant compliance alongside donor management, or two separate systems with manual reconciliation between them.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

VAWA grantees cannot maintain grant files that link identifiable survivor information to service records accessible to outside reviewers.

That requirement is not a suggestion from a program officer. The confidentiality provisions of the Violence Against Women Act are statutory — they prohibit shelters from disclosing personally identifying survivor information without informed, written, reasonably time-limited consent. Critically, confidentiality extends into the grant record itself.

This creates a specific data architecture requirement for domestic violence shelters: program tracking systems must report aggregate service data to funders without exposing individual case records. VOCA performance reporting requires aggregate counts of victims served by crime type, services provided by category, and demographic data. OVW semiannual progress reports through GMS require similar aggregate documentation. Neither accepts individual survivor records.

Most shelters operate with VAWA, VOCA, and FVPSA grants simultaneously. Each has different period of performance dates, different allowable cost categories, and different reporting timelines. VOCA funds are typically capped at 10% for administrative costs. Time for a shelter director whose salary is split across VAWA, VOCA, and operating funds must be documented separately for each funding source.

Using a single case management database for both service delivery and grant compliance tracking creates the linkage that the VAWA confidentiality provisions prohibit. The compliance architecture and the service delivery architecture must be designed separately from the start.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

Grant compliance failures happen after the award, not during the application.

The obligations begin the moment the grant agreement is signed. Spending restrictions, reporting cadences, documentation standards, and time tracking requirements are all active before the first dollar is spent. Most mid-sized nonprofits discover the scope of those obligations when the first report is due, not when the award letter arrives.

The Nonprofit Grant Compliance Checklist covers the post-award sequence: reading the award letter before any spending begins, setting up restricted fund tracking in the accounting system, calendaring every reporting deadline immediately, implementing time documentation for grant-funded staff, and running budget-vs-actual comparisons monthly. It also covers the warning signs that the current system is approaching its limits — spreadsheets typically break at three to five active grants with different fiscal years and staff splitting time across programs.

The threshold at which a dedicated system replaces the spreadsheet is around $500,000 in grant revenue or three active grants managed simultaneously.

**Post 8 — COMPARISON_INSIGHT**

Keela ($99–$299/month) and Bloomerang ($125–$249/month) compete directly in the mid-market nonprofit CRM space. Both are cloud-native. Both position as simpler alternatives to Salesforce and Blackbaud.

Keela's differentiator is a modern interface and marketing automation included at base pricing. Bloomerang's differentiator is a donor retention analytics dashboard that tracks retention rates, identifies at-risk donors, and surfaces giving frequency patterns. Per the Fifty & Fifty 2025 Nonprofit Peer Report, only 33.3% of nonprofits rate their CRM as effective — 20.1% find them outright ineffective.

The shared gap between Keela and Bloomerang: neither provides grant lifecycle management or restricted fund compliance tracking. Keela has basic grant recording — deadline tracking, not compliance. Bloomerang has minimal grant features. Organizations that receive restricted foundation or government grants still need a separate system for grant compliance regardless of which of these two they choose.

Choosing between them based on donor management features is a coherent evaluation for fundraising-primary organizations. Adding the grant compliance layer changes the comparison entirely — because neither platform eliminates the need for a separate compliance workflow.

**Post 9 — QUESTION_POST**

Development directors at $2M–$10M nonprofits typically carry 40 to 120 major donor prospects in active moves management alongside 8 to 25 active grants at any given time.

The Fundraising Effectiveness Project has reported sector-wide donor retention between 40 and 45 percent every year for over a decade. The cause is rarely appeal quality. It is the failure to follow up the second gift, track the lapsed-donor list, or notice when a multi-year pledge is 90 days overdue. These are operational failures — ones that fragmented tooling makes routine.

Most development directors run four systems that do not talk to each other: a donor CRM for gift history, a spreadsheet for the grant pipeline, QuickBooks for restricted fund balances, and a slide deck for the board that requires manually joining all three. Reconciling across those systems to build a board packet consumes 4 to 8 hours per cycle. If the CFO's numbers and the development report disagree, the development director explains it at a board meeting.

The job that needs doing is not complicated: moves management, grant pipeline, restricted fund visibility, and board-ready reporting from one source of truth.
