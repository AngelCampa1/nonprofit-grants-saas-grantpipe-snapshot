# Net Asset Releases: The Accounting Entry Nonprofits Most Often Get Wrong

A finance director at a mid-sized nonprofit receives a $150,000 restricted grant in October. By November, the check has cleared and the grant agreement is signed. By December, the full $150,000 has been recorded as unrestricted revenue in the Statement of Activities. The organization looks financially stronger. The board is pleased. The auditor is not.

Recording the entire grant award as unrestricted income at receipt is the most consequential timing error in nonprofit accounting. It misrepresents the financial position, overstates operational flexibility, and creates audit exposure that follows the organization through the annual audit cycle and potentially longer. The correct treatment — a net asset release triggered only as qualifying expenditures are incurred — is what FASB ASC 958 requires and what every auditor of a grant-reliant nonprofit is specifically trained to test.

## What a Net Asset Release Actually Is

A net asset release is a reclassification entry, not new revenue. When a restricted grant is received, the funds enter the with-donor-restrictions net asset class. They sit there, committed but not yet deployed. As qualifying program expenditures are made against the grant, the restrictions are released — the funds transfer from with-donor-restrictions to without-donor-restrictions in the Statement of Activities. The total net assets of the organization do not change with the release entry; the composition does.

This matters because the two net asset classes carry different information for financial statement users. With-donor-restrictions shows what the organization has been entrusted to deliver. Without-donor-restrictions shows what the organization can deploy at its own discretion. A board member reading a Statement of Financial Position where all grant funds have been prematurely released into the unrestricted class is reading an inaccurate picture of organizational flexibility — and of obligation.

Under FASB ASC 958-210-45, there are two net asset classes. The old "unrestricted" and "temporarily restricted" and "permanently restricted" three-class model was replaced by FASB ASU 2016-14, effective for fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2017. Organizations still using the old terminology in audited statements are presenting an accounting error, not just a style choice.

## Three Triggers for Release

Restrictions are released when one of three conditions is satisfied.

**Purpose restriction met.** The funds were spent on the activity specified in the grant agreement or gift letter. A grant restricted to "after-school tutoring for middle school students" releases its restriction dollar-for-dollar as qualifying tutoring program costs are incurred and documented. The release tracks expenditure activity in real time — it does not wait for the grant period to end, and it cannot outrun actual spending.

**Time restriction expired.** Some contributions specify a time period rather than a purpose: "these funds are for fiscal year 2026." When that period arrives, the time restriction is satisfied and the funds transfer to without-donor-restrictions — even if they have not been spent on any program. This is distinct from a purpose restriction. A contribution that is both time-restricted and purpose-restricted requires both conditions to be met before release.

**Combined restrictions both satisfied.** Grants that carry time and purpose restrictions simultaneously — federal grants are a common example — are released only when the grant period is active and qualifying expenditures are made. There is no shortcut: satisfying one condition does not trigger partial release on the other.

## The Journal Entry

The accounting entries for a net asset release appear in two coordinated parts.

The release entry moves the funds between net asset classes: debit Net Assets Released from Restrictions — With Donor Restrictions, and credit Net Assets Released from Restrictions — Without Donor Restrictions, for the amount released. This entry has no cash impact. It is a reclassification.

The expense entry records the program cost that triggered the release: debit the appropriate program expense account, and credit Cash or Accounts Payable. These two entries appear simultaneously in a well-structured accounting system. When an expense is coded to a restricted grant in the general ledger, the system should generate the release entry alongside the expense entry. Organizations using accounting systems that do not automate this must generate the release entries manually — but the entries must appear in the same period as the qualifying expenditure.

In the Statement of Activities, the release line appears in both columns. The with-donor-restrictions column shows a deduction — funds leaving that class. The without-donor-restrictions column shows an addition — funds entering the class — offset by the program expense incurred. The net change to without-donor-restrictions from the release and the related expense is zero. The Statement of Activities accurately reflects that grant funds were deployed toward the restricted purpose.

## The Four Timing Errors

**Releasing the full grant at receipt.** The most common and most damaging error. The entire award is recorded as unrestricted revenue when the check arrives. This inflates without-donor-restrictions net assets, makes the funds appear available for any use, and creates an audit finding when the grant is subsequently tested. Correcting it requires reversing the entry and restating prior-period financials if the error crosses a reporting period — a significant operational burden.

**Not releasing as expenditures are made.** The opposite problem: qualifying expenditures are coded and paid, but the release entries are never generated. The result is a growing gap between the restricted net asset balance and the actual unspent grant funds. At year-end, the accounting records show more in with-donor-restrictions than actually remains. Reconciliation at audit time requires tracing every qualifying expenditure back through the year to generate the missing entries — work that compounds in complexity with each passing month.

**Releasing based on budget rather than actual expenditures.** Some finance teams release restricted net assets based on the grant's budget schedule — releasing $25,000 per quarter on a $100,000 annual grant regardless of actual spending. This is incorrect. Releases must be driven by actual qualifying expenditures posted in the accounting system, not by the budgeted timeline. A quarter in which the program is under-spent should show a smaller release than a quarter in which spending ran ahead of schedule.

**Releasing before the grant period begins.** A federal grant covering fiscal year 2026, received and signed in November 2025, carries a time restriction that is not yet satisfied. The funds should remain in with-donor-restrictions until the grant period start date arrives, even though the award has been made and the check has cleared. Recording the income in the period of receipt is correct; releasing the restriction before the period opens is not.

## Documentation That Makes Audit Straightforward

Every net asset release should be traceable through a four-document chain.

The original grant agreement or gift letter establishes what the restriction requires — what activities qualify, what time period applies, what documentation the grantor expects. This document is the baseline against which every release entry is evaluated by an auditor.

The expenditure documentation for the costs that triggered the release — invoices, payroll records, time sheets — demonstrates that qualifying program work was done. For personnel costs charged to a federal grant, 2 CFR 200.430 requires that the documentation reflect actual time worked on grant-funded activities, not estimated or budgeted percentages. Reconstructed time records are a compliance risk.

The accounting entries showing the release and the related expense confirm that the reclassification was recorded correctly and in the right period.

A period-end reconciliation of the restricted fund balance before and after the release surfaces any discrepancy between what was spent and what was released. For organizations managing multiple restricted grants simultaneously, this reconciliation is the control that prevents the with-donor-restrictions balance from drifting out of alignment with actual unspent obligations.

## The Specific Risk for Grant-Reliant Organizations

Mid-sized nonprofits managing four to ten active restricted grants simultaneously face a specific compounding risk. Each grant has a different restriction, a different period, and a different release trigger. Personnel whose salaries are split across multiple grant-funded programs must have time documentation that allocates hours to the correct grant — and each allocation generates a separate release event when the expense is posted.

The common failure mode is annual: all of the year's qualifying expenditures are posted correctly, but the release entries are generated once at year-end rather than concurrent with the expenses. The Statement of Activities for the first three quarters of the year understates program activity and overstates the restricted net asset balance. When the auditor tests period-end balances against transaction-level records, the timing mismatch surfaces as a control finding.

The mechanics are not complicated. The discipline is. Each qualifying expenditure generates a release entry in the same period. The restricted fund balance declines in real time as program work is delivered. The Statement of Activities reflects, month by month, exactly what grant obligations have been satisfied and what remains.

## What to Do This Week

Pull the balance in net assets with donor restrictions. For each grant comprising that balance, confirm the unspent grant amount in the accounting system matches the restricted net asset balance attributed to that grant. A discrepancy is a missing release entry — and every missing entry is a prior-period adjustment waiting to happen.

If the reconciliation reveals a gap, generate the missing release entries for the relevant periods before the audit cycle begins. Document what expenditures triggered each release and attach that documentation to the journal entries.
