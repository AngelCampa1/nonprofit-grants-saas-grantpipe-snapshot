# Restricted Fund Tracking: Where Nonprofits Lose Control and What to Do About It

A program coordinator who works 60% on one grant and 40% on another has a compliance obligation most organizations do not have a reliable system to fulfill. The time split must be documented. The allocation must be updated if responsibilities shift. The payroll charges must map to the correct fund code, pay period by pay period, for the full grant term. When an auditor samples personnel costs three years after the fact, the organization either produces that documentation or explains a finding.

This is not a software problem or a staffing problem, though both are factors. It is an operational problem that most nonprofits underestimate until it surfaces in an audit finding, a disallowed cost, or a funder relationship that does not survive the compliance review.

## What Restricted Funds Actually Require

Restricted funds are assets whose use is limited by an external donor or funder condition. The accounting standard is FASB ASC 958, which separates nonprofit net assets into unrestricted, temporarily restricted, and permanently restricted categories. For most mid-sized nonprofits managing active grant portfolios, the day-to-day challenge is temporary restrictions: a federal workforce development grant limited to specific job training activities, a foundation health grant limited to a defined service population, a state housing grant with approved budget categories and allowable cost definitions that differ from the organization's own cost structure.

The compliance obligation is not ambiguous. Restricted funds must be spent on what the funder approved. They must be tracked separately from unrestricted operating funds. They cannot cross-subsidize each other without documented allocation methodology. And they must generate the documentation that supports funder financial reports and withstands audit testing.

The failure mode is not usually deliberate misuse. It is a system that cannot enforce the rules automatically, combined with staff who are working too fast to catch the errors before auditors do.

## The Three Operational Breakdowns

**Personnel cost allocation.** Staff costs present the largest restricted fund tracking risk. A program coordinator whose time is split across two grants cannot simply appear on one grant's payroll. Monthly personnel activity reports — not semi-annual certifications, which apply only to single-program employees — must document 100% of that person's compensated time, allocated to the correct funding source for each pay period. If responsibilities shift mid-year, the allocation percentages must be updated prospectively and documented. Payroll systems do not track grant allocations by default. Someone must create, maintain, and update that tracking system. When the person who owned the spreadsheet leaves, the system often goes with them.

**Shared cost allocation.** Office supplies, software subscriptions, telephone, facility costs — these benefit multiple programs simultaneously. Organizations charge these to grants using an indirect cost rate or a cost allocation methodology. The methodology must be documented, consistently applied, and defensible under audit. Federal grantees without a negotiated indirect cost rate agreement (NICRA) may use the de minimis rate of 15% of modified total direct costs under 2 CFR 200, but choosing the de minimis rate requires a documented election and consistent application. Organizations that use different methodologies in different years, or that cannot produce the documentation underlying their allocation, create audit exposure on what should be a routine compliance item.

**Timing gaps between spending and reconciliation.** A restricted grant is received in month one. Personnel expenses accumulate in months two through twelve. Vendor invoices for approved program activities arrive throughout the year. By the time someone reconciles the grant budget in month six — or month twelve, if reconciliation only happens at report time — connecting each expenditure to the correct fund requires reconstructing decisions made months earlier. The longer the gap between spending and reconciliation, the higher the error rate. The MacArthur Foundation's indirect cost study found that the minimum indirect cost rate associated with financially healthy organizations is 29%, yet most funders reimburse far less. Organizations managing grants with inadequate tracking tools end up absorbing costs that were legitimately chargeable to grants — not because the costs were ineligible, but because the documentation did not exist to claim them.

## What Audit Failure Costs

The Nonprofit Finance Fund's 2025 State of the Nonprofit Sector Survey found that 36% of nonprofits ended FY2024 with an operating deficit — the highest rate in a decade. Cash constraints make the cost of audit findings particularly acute.

For federal grants, a finding that restricted funds were used outside their approved purpose can result in questioned costs: the funder refuses to reimburse those expenses and may demand repayment of amounts already disbursed. Significant findings can result in suspension of future funding, additional monitoring requirements, or debarment from federal programs.

For foundation grants, the consequence is almost always relational: required repayment and loss of the grantor relationship. Foundation program officers communicate with peer funders. A compliance failure that becomes part of the organization's reputation in a funding community affects grant prospects beyond the single funder involved.

The consistent pattern: the cost of correcting a compliance failure after audit is higher than the cost of tracking correctly throughout the grant period. Organizations that run the math on staff time spent on post-audit remediation, required repayments, and lost funding relationships consistently find the upstream investment in systems would have been cheaper.

## The Three-System Problem

The root cause of most restricted fund tracking failures is architectural. Accurate fund tracking requires connecting three systems that typically do not communicate: the accounting system, which holds the general ledger and fund codes; the grant management system, which tracks award terms, budget categories, and funder requirements; and the HR or payroll system, which processes personnel costs without grant allocation logic.

In the absence of a connected system, organizations build manual bridges: the spreadsheet that translates payroll to grant allocations, the shared drive where invoices are filed without a consistent naming convention, the monthly email from the grants manager that produces the reconciliation the finance director needs for SF-425 preparation. Each bridge is a failure point. When the grants manager is out or transitions roles, the bridge fails. When a budget modification is approved by the funder but not propagated to the tracking spreadsheet, the bridge fails. When the accounting system year-end close happens before the grant reconciliation is complete, the bridge fails.

Purpose-built grant compliance software creates the connection architecture directly. Each grant award links to its approved budget categories. Expenditures are recorded against the correct grant as they occur rather than being allocated retroactively. Personnel activity reports tie personnel costs to fund codes at the time payroll is processed. The fund balance visible to the development director matches the fund balance in the accounting system because they are drawing from the same data source.

## The Monthly Reconciliation Practice

The operational fix is not primarily a software purchase. It is a practice change that software can support. The practice: reconcile every active restricted fund monthly, not quarterly and not at report time.

A monthly reconciliation does four things. It catches misallocated expenditures while the transactions are recent and correctable, before they compound over multiple months. It produces the running budget-to-actual comparison that makes SF-425 preparation a confirmation exercise rather than a reconstruction project. It flags pending expenses that need to be reviewed for allowability before they are processed rather than after. And it creates the documentation record that demonstrates the monitoring occurred — which auditors treat as evidence of a functioning compliance system, not just data in a ledger.

The specific steps of a defensible monthly reconciliation:

1. Pull a fund-specific expenditure report from the accounting system for the period.
2. Compare line by line against the approved budget, by category.
3. Identify any expenditures that appear in the wrong budget category or that exceed approved category amounts.
4. Document corrections needed and the date corrections were submitted.
5. Confirm that personnel allocations for the period match approved effort percentages.
6. Update the remaining-balance calculation for each budget category.
7. File the reconciliation as a dated document with the grant file.

Organizations that complete this practice consistently find that grant closeout becomes a verification step rather than a month-long reconstruction.

## The Most Common Single Failure

Across audit findings involving restricted funds, the most common single failure is not misuse of funds. It is missing documentation of allowable expenditures. The expense was legitimate. The cost category was correct. The fund had budget remaining. The organization simply cannot produce the invoice, the time record, or the approval evidence that connects the expenditure to the grant.

The consequence is the same as actual misuse from the auditor's perspective: a questioned cost. The organization must either produce the missing documentation — if it exists somewhere — or accept the disallowance.

The fix is documentation discipline at the point of expenditure, not at audit time. Every charge to a restricted fund should have a source document that travels with it into the grant file: the invoice for vendor costs, the payroll register excerpt for personnel costs, the business purpose justification for travel. When the document is filed at the time the expense occurs, the audit response is retrievable in minutes. When it is not filed, the response requires weeks.

## What to Do This Week

If your organization manages active restricted grants, three actions create immediate risk reduction.

Pull the current budget-to-actual for each active grant today, from the accounting system, not from a spreadsheet. If the two sources do not match, the reconciliation gap is the first priority.

Verify that personnel allocation percentages in payroll match what is documented in each grant's file. A discrepancy means either the payroll allocation is wrong or the documented allocation is out of date — either way, it is an audit exposure that is better corrected now than explained later.

Confirm that the grant file for each active award contains the pre-award risk assessments, sub-award agreements, and SAM.gov debarment checks required under 2 CFR 200.332 for any subrecipients. These are among the most frequently requested documents in single audit testing, and they must have existed at the time the sub-award was made, not be assembled retroactively.
