# Grant Compliance for Environmental Nonprofits: What the Uniform Guidance Doesn't Cover

A Clean Water Act Section 319 grant closes in 90 days. The project logged 480 acres of stream buffer restored across 14 sites over three years. Field monitoring records exist — but they are in three different staff members' hard drives, two of whom no longer work at the organization.

That is an EPA compliance failure waiting to materialize. Not a bookkeeping error. Not a missed deadline. A documentation gap that will surface the moment a program officer asks to see the underlying data for the outcome numbers in the final report.

Environmental nonprofit grant compliance sits at a harder intersection than most federal grantees face. The financial side follows standard Uniform Guidance rules — allowable costs, restricted fund tracking, SF-425 submissions, single audit obligations. The programmatic side requires technically defensible environmental outcome documentation, and that documentation must be organized in a way that survives staff turnover, multi-year project timelines, and monitoring visits.

Most organizations manage the financial compliance reasonably well. The programmatic documentation side is where findings actually happen.

## The Two-Layer Compliance Framework

EPA grants operate under two layers of requirements that are easy to conflate.

The first layer is the Uniform Guidance (2 CFR Part 200), which applies to all federal awards. It governs cost principles — the three-part test of allowable, allocable, and reasonable under 2 CFR 200.405 — procurement standards, equipment management, subrecipient monitoring under 2 CFR 200.332, and single audit obligations when federal expenditures reach $1,000,000 in a fiscal year (updated in the 2024 revisions, effective for fiscal years ending September 30, 2025 or later).

The second layer is EPA-specific: 2 CFR Part 1500 (EPA's implementation of Uniform Guidance), program-specific requirements in the notice of award, and for cooperative agreements, the substantial involvement requirements that give EPA active oversight of project decisions.

The distinction between a grant and a cooperative agreement matters operationally. Under a cooperative agreement, EPA has an active role — approving significant project decisions, participating in activities, or redirecting work. Grant recipients expect less program officer involvement. Cooperative agreement recipients should build EPA check-in requirements into their project management workflow from day one, not treat program officer contact as an exception.

## Grant Prospecting and Restricted Fund Complexity

Environmental nonprofits commonly receive funding through several EPA mechanisms: Environmental Justice Collaborative Problem-Solving (EJCPS) cooperative agreements, Clean Water Act Section 319 nonpoint source grants distributed by states, State and Tribal Assistance Grants (STAG), and — increasingly — Inflation Reduction Act-funded environmental justice and climate resilience programs.

IRA-funded programs deserve special attention. These are relatively new funding streams, and program officer interpretations of allowable activities are still developing. Organizations receiving IRA funds should maintain close communication with their program officers about allowable use questions, document those conversations, and not rely on informal guidance that was not put in writing.

For state pass-through grants, the compliance obligations flow through regardless of the intermediary. A nonprofit receiving Clean Water Act 319 funds from a state environmental agency is a subgrantee of the state — but 2 CFR Part 200 applies end-to-end. The state is the pass-through entity responsible for communicating applicable requirements, but that does not transfer the compliance obligation away from the nonprofit.

## The Restricted Fund Accounting Problem in Multi-Year Projects

A three-year watershed restoration grant of $600,000 creates a specific accounting challenge that does not exist for a single-year award: the restricted balance must carry forward correctly across fiscal years.

If year one expenditures total $185,000, the remaining $415,000 is still restricted and must be tracked as net assets with donor restrictions (or in the case of conditional government grants, as refundable advances until conditions are met). A year-end accountant who fails to carry forward the restricted balance accurately, or who releases the restriction before qualifying expenditures are documented, produces financial statements that misrepresent the organization's financial position.

Match tracking over multiple years creates a parallel problem. Clean Water Act 319 and Brownfields grants typically carry matching requirements. Match contributions must be documented as they are contributed — not front-loaded in year one to meet the requirement early, and not deferred to year three. A running match ledger maintained across the full project period is the only documentation approach that survives a closeout review.

Under FASB ASC 958-205, a purpose restriction releases when qualifying expenditures have been incurred and documented — not when the project period ends, and not when the board approves a journal entry. For environmental grants, that means the restricted fund balance should decrease as program costs are coded to the grant throughout the project period, not in a single year-end entry.

## The Four-Part Environmental Outcome Documentation Framework

Environmental outcome claims are what differentiate an EPA grant monitoring visit from a standard federal grant review. The financial compliance is reviewable from standard accounting documentation. The outcome compliance requires project-specific evidence.

**Field monitoring records.** Water quality improvements reported in a Clean Water Act 319 final report must be backed by water quality sampling records: date, location, sampling methodology, laboratory results. Stream miles addressed must be traceable to field activity logs showing which sites were visited, by whom, on what dates, and what practices were implemented. Outcome numbers that cannot be traced to contemporaneous field documentation are not accepted.

**Approved methodologies.** EPA programs specify which estimation tools or measurement methods are acceptable for each outcome measure. Pounds of pollutant reduced must be calculated using an EPA-approved estimation methodology. Using an unapproved methodology produces outcome numbers that may not be accepted even if the field data is complete. Before the first reporting period, identify and document the approved methodology for each outcome measure specified in the notice of award.

**Scope alignment documentation.** Every project activity should be traceable to a specific task in the approved work plan. A project log connecting each activity to the work plan task it supports is not bureaucratic overhead — it is the primary evidence that the organization did what it was funded to do. When project conditions require adapting from the approved approach, written approval from the program officer must be obtained before proceeding, not after.

**Subrecipient monitoring records.** Environmental organizations that fund community groups, local governments, or research partners for project activities are pass-through entities for those dollars. Subrecipient monitoring under 2 CFR 200.332 requires documenting the subrecipient's compliance, reviewing their financial reports, and maintaining records of monitoring activities. Subrecipient monitoring failures are among the most frequent EPA grant findings — and they receive heightened scrutiny from EPA's Office of Inspector General.

## The Most Common EPA Compliance Failure

The single most serious EPA grant compliance failure is reporting environmental outcomes that are not supported by field monitoring data.

A project that claims 500 acres of stream buffer restored must produce documentation showing that specific sites were treated, by whom, on what dates, using what conservation practices. The documentation must exist before the claim appears in a report — not as a reconstruction assembled during a monitoring visit.

This failure pattern follows a predictable sequence: project staff track outcomes informally throughout the project, annual reports are compiled from staff memory and rough estimates, final reports aggregate those estimates without connecting them to site-level documentation, and then a program officer or auditor asks for the underlying data.

The gap between the reported number and the documentable number is the compliance finding. The consequence ranges from requiring a corrected report to repayment of disallowed costs if the project outcomes do not justify the expenditures charged to the grant.

The preventive approach is not complicated: require site visit logs at the activity level, store them in a shared project file accessible to all project staff, and conduct a documentation review before each reporting period — not only at year-end.

## Staff Turnover and Project File Continuity

A three-year environmental project will almost certainly see staff turnover. The development director who wrote the grant may not still be at the organization when the final report is due. The field coordinator who ran year-one site visits may have left by year three.

The project file must be structured so that a new staff member can understand the project history, outstanding obligations, and compliance status from the documentation alone — without institutional memory from staff who are no longer there.

That means the project file must contain: the notice of award and all amendments, the approved work plan and any approved modifications, a running match ledger, site visit logs organized by activity, outcome calculation documentation with the approved methodology, all correspondence with the program officer (particularly any informal guidance on allowable activities), and all submitted reports.

Organizations with cloud-based project file management — not shared drives or individual desktops — maintain this continuity more reliably. When a staff member departs, the documentation should not depart with them.

## Indirect Cost Rate Consistency

Environmental organizations frequently have above-average indirect cost rates. Facility costs for labs, field equipment, and monitoring infrastructure are high. Indirect cost recovery under EPA grants follows the rules in 2 CFR 200.414: organizations with a negotiated indirect cost rate agreement (NICRA) should use that rate; organizations without a NICRA may use the 15% de minimis rate.

The compliance issue is inconsistency. Using the de minimis rate on some awards and the NICRA on others — or changing the allocation base mid-project — is a finding. The approved rate must be applied consistently across reporting periods for a given award, and the base to which it is applied must be the base defined in the award terms, not the organization's standard calculation.

If your actual indirect cost rate is materially higher than what your awards permit you to recover, that gap is not a compliance problem — but it is an organizational sustainability problem worth addressing through the NICRA negotiation process.

## What to Do This Week

Audit the project documentation for every active environmental grant. For each grant, answer three questions: Can you trace every outcome claimed in the most recent report to field documentation? Is the match ledger current? Are all project files accessible by someone other than the primary staff member?

If the answer to any of these is uncertain, address it now — not at the next reporting deadline. Gaps that are discoverable during a proactive review are manageable. Gaps discovered during a monitoring visit are findings.
