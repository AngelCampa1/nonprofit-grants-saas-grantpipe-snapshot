**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

After-school programs receiving 21st CCLC funds face a single audit trigger at $1,000,000 in federal expenditures — raised to $1,000,000 in the 2024 Uniform Guidance revision.

The 21st Century Community Learning Centers program is authorized under ESEA Title IV Part B (20 USC 7171 et seq.) and flows from the US Department of Education through state educational agencies to local operators.

That subaward chain matters for compliance: 2 CFR Part 200 applies end-to-end, including allowable costs, procurement standards, and equipment rules.

GPRA performance measures cover three domains — academic proficiency in reading and math, school behavior, and school engagement.

Annual Performance Reports are submitted through the 21APR system, which aggregates grantee data at the state level before it reaches the federal level.

And grantees must conduct a validated program quality assessment — most commonly the Youth Program Quality Assessment from the Weikart Center.

Most after-school programs run their GPRA tracking in one system and their finances in another. That split is where documentation gaps happen.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Releasing restricted net assets at year-end is not an accounting choice — it is an accounting event that requires specific evidence.

Under FASB ASC 958-205, a release from restriction is a reclassification on the Statement of Activities, not new revenue.

The restriction releases when the donor-imposed stipulation expires or is fulfilled. Not when the board approves the journal entry. Not when year-end close arrives.

For purpose-restricted funds, the release amount equals qualified expenditures incurred — not the total grant balance available.

If $80,000 was received and $65,000 was spent, $65,000 releases. The remaining $15,000 carries forward as restricted.

The release journal entry should be dated at the date the restriction was satisfied — not at the close date unless year-end is when the restriction was actually fulfilled.

One of the most common audit adjustments in this area: a release that exceeds the documented qualifying expenditures. Auditors trace the release amount back to expenditure records. If the backup is not there, it is an adjustment.

FASB ASU 2016-14 also requires footnote disclosure of the nature and amounts of donor-imposed restrictions and expected timing of their release.

Run the full restricted fund roll-forward monthly on active grants — year-end should be a calculation, not a three-day reconciliation.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

2 CFR Part 200 is not a suggestion. If your organization receives federal awards — directly or through a state pass-through — the Uniform Guidance governs what costs are allowable, how your accounting systems must work, and when you need to be audited.

The Uniform Guidance consolidated three prior OMB circulars when it took effect in December 2014: A-133 (single audit), A-122 (cost principles for nonprofits), and A-110 (administrative requirements).

That consolidation matters because it created one consistent framework across federal agencies. A nonprofit with grants from HHS, EPA, and the Department of Education now operates under the same cost principle framework for all three.

The three-part test for allowable costs under 2 CFR 200.405: allowable (not prohibited under the cost principles), allocable (benefits the award in reasonable proportion), and reasonable (a prudent person would not pay more for the same goods under similar circumstances).

The 2024 revisions raised the single audit threshold to $1,000,000 in federal expenditures, effective for fiscal years ending September 30, 2025 or later.

Organizations tracking against the $1,000,000 threshold should update their monitoring now — particularly if federal expenditures are in that range and the audit trigger matters for planning.

For day-to-day compliance, Subpart D (post-award requirements) and Subpart E (cost principles, especially 200.430 on personnel costs) are where most findings originate.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

A $50,000 grant is not worth $50,000 after compliance costs. Most finance teams have never added up the actual number.

Grant compliance has four cost categories that rarely get totaled together: staff hours on setup, coding, reconciliation, and reporting; software gaps and the manual work required to compensate for them; error recovery when transactions are miscoded or reports are re-issued; and risk exposure from potential audit findings or disallowed costs.

The Center for Effective Philanthropy and GrantStation research consistently finds that mid-sized nonprofits spend 30 to 87 staff hours per grant per year on compliance activities. At a $45/hour blended rate, that is $1,350 to $3,900 per grant — before software costs or error recovery.

GrantStation's 2024 survey of 2,306 nonprofits found that 42% of funders cap indirect costs at 10% or less, while actual nonprofit overhead typically runs 25-35%. That gap is absorbed from other funds — usually unrestricted ones.

If your smallest grant generates a net value of less than 60% of the award amount after compliance costs, that grant costs more than the headline figure suggests.

**Post 8 — COMPARISON_INSIGHT**

Instrumentl and Foundant GrantHub are not competing tools. They address different phases of the grant lifecycle — and most nonprofits end up buying both.

Instrumentl is a grant prospecting platform: finding opportunities, managing applications, and tracking the pre-award pipeline. It starts at $179/month (annual billing).

Foundant GrantHub is a post-award compliance tracker: deliverables, reporting deadlines, and documentation for active grants. It starts at $960/year ($80/month).

The practical consequence: a nonprofit using both for grant discovery and compliance, plus a separate donor CRM like Bloomerang, pays $400-$700/month — and still maintains three separate data stores with three vendor relationships.

The development director becomes the human bridge connecting funder relationships tracked in the CRM, applications tracked in Instrumentl, and active compliance tracked in GrantHub.

Grant compliance failures are cited as the primary reason for funder relationship damage by 44% of development directors, per the AFP Fundraising Effectiveness Project 2024.

Neither Instrumentl nor Foundant includes restricted fund tracking or donor relationship management. The three-tool model creates the coordination problem that good software is supposed to eliminate.

Before evaluating which prospecting tool or compliance tool to buy, identify where your organization's compliance friction actually lives: finding grants, managing active compliance, or keeping donor and grant data connected.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

The SF-425 Federal Financial Report is required for virtually every federal grant award — and Box 10e is where most errors originate.

Box 10e, Total Federal Share of Expenditures, is the cumulative total of allowable costs charged to the award from the start of the grant period. It must reconcile exactly with the grant fund balance in your accounting system.

The most common error: reporting period figures instead of cumulative totals on the final SF-425. The final report requires cumulative totals covering the entire period of performance — not just the last quarter or year.

Submission schedules vary by program and are specified in the notice of award: quarterly (due 30 days after quarter end), semi-annual (due 30 days after June 30 and December 31), annual (due 90 days after grant year end), or final only (due 120 days after period-of-performance end date).

Missing interim SF-425 deadlines generates delinquency notices in federal grant management systems and can suspend draw-down access until reports are current.

For indirect costs, apply the approved rate to the base defined in the award — not your organization's standard base. Some awards cap indirect costs or define the calculation base differently from your NICRA.

Program income on Box 10l is frequently omitted: revenue generated as a result of federally funded activities must be reported, even if amounts are small.
