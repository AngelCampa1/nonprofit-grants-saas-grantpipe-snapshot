# How to Negotiate a Negotiated Indirect Cost Rate Agreement (NICRA): A Practical Guide for Nonprofit Finance Teams

A nonprofit receiving $2 million in federal awards and charging indirect costs at the 15% de minimis rate recovers $200,000. The same organization with a negotiated indirect cost rate of 18% recovers $360,000. That $160,000 difference funds staff time, facilities, and administration that would otherwise come out of unrestricted reserves — or not be funded at all.

The gap between those two outcomes is a NICRA: a Negotiated Indirect Cost Rate Agreement issued by a cognizant federal agency. The decision to pursue one — or to stick with the 15% de minimis rate under 2 CFR 200.414(f) — is one of the most consequential financial decisions a federally funded nonprofit makes. Most organizations make it by default rather than by calculation.

## The First Decision: NICRA or De Minimis

Before preparing any proposal, the calculation comes first. Under 2 CFR 200.414(f), an organization that has never had a negotiated indirect cost rate may elect the de minimis rate of 15% of Modified Total Direct Costs (MTDC). No proposal, no cognizant agency review, no annual renewal required. The election can be used indefinitely.

The de minimis rate wins when actual indirect costs are close to or below 15% of MTDC. For organizations with modest administrative structures relative to their direct program spending, chasing a negotiated rate produces marginal additional recovery that does not justify 40–80 hours of initial proposal work.

The NICRA wins when actual indirect costs run materially higher. A practical threshold: if the organization's actual indirect rate is 13–15% or above, the NICRA pays for itself within two to three years. Below that, the de minimis is often the more pragmatic path.

The 2024 revision to 2 CFR 200 also extended de minimis eligibility significantly. Some organizations that previously had to submit ICR proposals can now elect de minimis instead. It is worth confirming current eligibility before starting a proposal.

## Identifying the Cognizant Agency

The cognizant agency is the federal entity responsible for reviewing and issuing the rate agreement. For most nonprofits, it is determined by a single rule: the federal agency providing the largest direct dollar amount of funding.

In practice, that means HHS handles most nonprofits. The Division of Cost Allocation (DCA) within HHS's Program Support Center is the functional office. The Department of Education handles educational organizations with significant federal education funding. USDA handles organizations with agricultural or rural program focus.

If an organization receives only pass-through federal funding — federal money flowing through a state agency or intermediary — HHS DCA typically serves as the cognizant agency by default, since there is no direct federal funder to assume the role.

This matters practically because different cognizant agencies have different submission portals, different documentation checklists, and different response timelines. HHS DCA accepts electronic submissions and has published checklists. First contact with the relevant agency should happen before the proposal is assembled.

## The Three-Part Framework: Pool, Base, and Documentation

Every ICR proposal rests on three technical elements. Getting these right determines whether the proposal moves or gets returned.

**The Cost Pool**

The indirect cost pool contains all organizational costs that benefit multiple programs and cannot be directly assigned to a specific federal award. Common indirect costs include general administration (executive leadership, finance, HR, legal), facilities costs (rent, utilities, depreciation on shared space), IT and communications infrastructure, and board-related expenses.

What does not belong in the indirect pool: any cost that can be specifically identified with a particular award. A program coordinator whose time is tracked to specific grants is a direct cost, not an indirect one. Facilities costs for a space used exclusively by a single program belong direct. Organizations that commingle these categories — or that charge as indirect any cost that is inconvenient to track directly — will face challenges during review.

**The Allocation Base**

The allocation base is the denominator against which indirect costs are spread. The standard base is Modified Total Direct Costs. MTDC includes direct salaries, fringe benefits, materials and supplies, services, travel, and the first $50,000 of each subaward. MTDC excludes equipment, capital expenditures, rental of real property, student aid, and the portion of each subaward exceeding $50,000.

The base must be complete and consistent. Organizations that include some direct salaries but exclude others without documented justification will face scrutiny. The cognizant agency verifies that the base captures all relevant direct costs and that exclusions are defensible.

**The Documentation Package**

A complete submission includes: audited financial statements for the base year, IRS Form 990, personnel time allocation records, a written indirect cost allocation methodology, rate calculation worksheets that tie to the audit, a complete list of federal awards with amounts by agency, and a Certificate of Indirect Costs signed by an authorized official (typically the CEO or CFO).

The reconciliation between the proposal and the audited financial statements is the most critical element. Every number in the cost pool and base must trace to a specific line or schedule in the audit. Unreconciled submissions are returned without negotiation. This is not a technicality — the purpose is to ensure the proposal reflects actual costs, not estimated ones.

## Choosing the Rate Type

Four rate types exist, and the choice affects administrative burden for years after the NICRA is issued.

**Provisional rates** are temporary, used until a final rate is negotiated. They are common for first-time submissions while the initial review is pending. Provisional rates create uncertainty because retroactive adjustment is possible once the final rate is set.

**Predetermined rates** are fixed and not retroactively adjusted. They provide budget certainty for both the organization and its federal awarding agencies. The downside: if actual indirect costs exceed the predetermined rate, the difference is not recovered.

**Fixed-with-carryforward rates** are set in advance, but variances between the negotiated rate and actual costs carry into the next period's rate calculation rather than requiring retroactive adjustment. For most mid-sized nonprofits, this is the practical sweet spot: rate certainty in the current period, with over- or under-recovery settling out over time.

**Final rates** are established retroactively once actual costs are known. They provide precision but require keeping awards open until the final rate is resolved — administratively heavy for organizations managing multiple overlapping grant periods.

## The Submission and Negotiation Process

Submission deadlines are typically 180 days after fiscal year-end. Extensions are available if requested in advance from the cognizant agency.

After submission, expect the agency to ask clarifying questions and request additional documentation. Common focus areas: justification for specific cost allocations, support for personnel time records, and verification that excluded costs were correctly treated. Responses should be prompt and documented in writing.

First-time negotiations typically run 90–180 days from submission to rate issuance. Renewals of established NICRAs are faster — often 30–60 days. Organizations with clean audits, well-documented cost allocation methodology, and complete submissions move through the process fastest. Every response to an agency question that requires back-and-forth adds weeks.

Once the rate letter is issued, it specifies the rate, the allocation base, the rate type, and the effective period. Pass-through subrecipients of the organization's federal awards may use the NICRA for their own indirect recovery if subaward terms permit.

## The Most Common Failure: Mixing Direct and Indirect Costs

The single most frequent reason ICR proposals are returned or challenged during negotiation is mixed classification: costs that belong in the direct category appear in the indirect pool, or vice versa, without time-study support or documented methodology.

The most common specific version: a program director whose time benefits multiple grants and organizational activities is charged entirely to indirect costs for administrative convenience, rather than being tracked to specific awards through documented time allocation. The cognizant agency treats undocumented mixed-function salaries charged to indirect costs as a red flag.

The remedy is a written indirect cost allocation methodology — not a theoretical policy document, but an operational one that specifies how each expense category is classified, how staff time allocation is determined (time study, periodic allocation, after-the-fact certification), and how shared costs like IT and facilities are handled. This document is reviewed annually and during every ICR negotiation. Organizations that cannot produce it face extended review cycles.

## What to Do This Week

Organizations currently using the de minimis rate should run the calculation: what is the actual indirect cost rate based on the most recent audited financials? If it is at or below 15%, de minimis is likely correct. If it is 15% or higher and federal funding is $1M or more, a NICRA negotiation is worth modeling.

Organizations with an existing NICRA should confirm the rate letter expiration date. If renewal is due within the next 90 days, the submission should already be in progress. If it lapses without renewal, the organization falls back to de minimis — which may be acceptable, but should be a deliberate choice rather than an oversight.
