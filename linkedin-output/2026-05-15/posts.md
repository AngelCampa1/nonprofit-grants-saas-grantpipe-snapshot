**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Bloomerang is not a grant compliance tool. That distinction matters more than most organizations realize until a federal audit arrives.

Bloomerang tracks grants as fundraising relationship records. It shows the funder, the amount, the date. It does not track spending against the award. There is no mechanism for seeing "Grant X: $100,000 awarded, $67,340 spent, $32,660 remaining."

There is no SF-425 generation capability because Bloomerang does not hold the financial data an SF-425 requires.

There is no time and effort tracking for personnel charged to federal awards — a requirement under 2 CFR 200 that cannot be satisfied with a CRM relationship record.

There is no non-editable audit trail for grant expenditure changes.

Organizations managing federal grants or five or more simultaneous restricted awards from Bloomerang are relying on a system that was genuinely not designed for those functions. The spreadsheets that fill the gap create the documentation holes that show up as audit findings.

The question is not whether Bloomerang is a good product. For individual donor management, it is well-regarded. The question is whether it matches the job.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

A December gift refunded in January is not a simple reversal. The donor's prior-year charitable deduction has been affected.

IRS Publication 1771 requires written acknowledgment for contributions of $250 or more. When a gift is refunded — especially across a tax year — that original acknowledgment letter is no longer accurate. A correction letter must be issued stating the original gift date and amount, the refund date and amount, and the net contribution of zero for a full refund.

The letter should explicitly note that the refund occurred in a different tax year and recommend the donor consult their tax advisor about any prior deduction they claimed.

Three other things that go wrong when this process is handled manually: the CRM record is not updated, leaving giving totals inflated and LYBUNT/SYBUNT metrics overstated; the restricted fund balance is not reversed, which distorts net asset reporting under FASB ASC 958; and the Form 990 Part VIII contribution total is wrong because the refund happened in a prior fiscal year and was not tracked at the transaction level.

Same-year refunds are straightforward. Cross-year refunds require communication, documentation, and careful accounting treatment. Organizations that process one refund per quarter rarely have a consistent workflow for this — until an audit asks for the file.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

MTDC is not total direct costs. That distinction costs organizations money on federal awards every year.

Modified Total Direct Costs, defined at 2 CFR 200.1, is the cost base you multiply by your indirect cost rate — whether that is the 15% de minimis rate or a negotiated NICRA. It begins with total direct costs and removes eight mandatory categories before the rate is applied.

The exclusions: equipment at or above your capitalization threshold (generally $5,000), capital expenditures, patient care charges, tuition remission, rental costs for off-site facilities, scholarships and fellowships, participant support costs, and sub-award amounts above the first $25,000 per subcontract.

That last one is where organizations consistently leave recovery on the table or overclaim. If a grant includes a $75,000 sub-award, only $25,000 of it counts toward MTDC. The remaining $50,000 is excluded. Applying the indirect rate to the full $75,000 overclaims indirect costs and produces an audit finding.

The GAO reports that approximately 30% of single audits identify at least one cost-claim finding. Indirect cost base errors are among the most frequently cited categories.

2 CFR 200.414(f) specifies MTDC as the base for the 15% de minimis rate. Applying 15% to total direct costs is wrong and it is a routine finding.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

A $300,000 animal rescue managing six simultaneous grants is doing compliance work built for a development staff it cannot afford.

ASPCA awarded more than $10 million to animal welfare organizations in 2022. PetSmart Charities granted more than $60 million in fiscal year 2023. Each of those grants carries different outcome metrics — animals placed, spay/neuter counts, adoption rates, length-of-stay data — in addition to financial expenditure reports.

Standard fund accounting software tracks money. It does not track animals placed or procedures completed. Organizations managing ASPCA and PetSmart Charities grants simultaneously are managing two different outcome reporting frameworks with different metrics, different terminology, and different report formats.

Restricted fund tracking across 4–6 small grants in the $5,000–$50,000 range requires knowing exactly how much of each award has been spent, how much remains, and whether current expenditures are coded correctly. When a spay/neuter clinic draws supplies from inventory, that cost must hit the right grant, not a general operating account.

Organizations that pool restricted funds — even unintentionally — create audit exposure and risk losing renewal eligibility with major funders.

The institutional knowledge problem compounds this when the staff member who understood the grant coding leaves.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five things worth knowing before evaluating grant management software:

Most software in this category was built for grantmakers, not grantseeker nonprofits. Submittable, Fluxx, and similar platforms manage the process of reviewing applications and awarding grants — from the funder's side. Nonprofits that receive grants need post-award tools, not grantmaker portals.

Pre-award tools (Instrumentl, Foundant GrantHub, ZoomGrants) cover grant discovery, application tracking, and deadline management. They do not cover what happens after the award: restricted fund balances, expenditure documentation, funder-specific financial reports, or audit preparation.

Salesforce with grant management configuration is not a low-cost option. Implementation runs $30,000–$100,000 before the first user logs in. The discounted license through TechSoup does not change the implementation cost.

The accounting integration question is non-negotiable. If gift records in the CRM do not flow to the accounting system without manual reentry, someone is doing double data entry at 2,000+ transactions per year. That is an error source and a time cost that compounds.

Spreadsheets are the default post-award compliance tool at most nonprofits — and the primary reason compliance failures occur. There is no audit trail for expenditure changes, no version control, and the institutional knowledge lives in the spreadsheet owner's head.

The relevant shortlisting question is not feature count. It is whether the system covers the handoff between development, finance, and executive reporting without requiring another spreadsheet layer.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

Processing a nonprofit donor refund involves eight distinct steps. Here is how to do it without creating tax problems for the donor or compliance problems for the organization.

Step 1: Confirm the request in writing and verify the original gift details — amount, date, fund designation, payment method.

Step 2: Determine whether the refund crosses a tax year. A December gift refunded in January affects the donor's prior-year return. Document this before anything else.

Step 3: Refund through the original payment method. Credit card to the same card. ACH to the originating account. Retain the transaction confirmation as primary documentation.

Step 4: Void or revise the tax acknowledgment. Under IRS Publication 1771, the original letter must be corrected. Issue a revised letter stating the original amount, refund amount and date, and net contribution. For cross-year refunds, explicitly note that the donor should consult their tax advisor.

Step 5: Book the general ledger reversal — credit cash, debit contribution revenue — dated as of the actual refund date, not the original gift date.

Step 6: Update the CRM record. An unaddressed refund inflates giving totals and corrupts LYBUNT/SYBUNT metrics.

Step 7: Address restricted fund implications. If the gift was restricted and program costs were already incurred, the fund may have a deficit. Management decides how to cover it. Do not leave a restricted fund with a negative balance.

Step 8: Assess Form 990 impact. Same-year refunds reduce Part VIII gross contributions. Cross-year refunds reduce net assets in the year processed.
