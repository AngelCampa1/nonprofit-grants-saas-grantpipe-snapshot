# The Eight-Step Donor Refund Workflow: What Nonprofit Finance Staff Get Wrong

A $500 gift refunded in January for a December donation is not a simple transaction reversal. It is a prior-year tax event for the donor, a net asset adjustment under FASB ASC 958, and a Form 990 reporting matter that will not resolve itself without deliberate action. Most organizations process the payment refund correctly and then stop — leaving three other obligations unmet.

This article covers the full eight-step donor refund workflow, with specific attention to the steps that generate compliance problems when skipped.

## Why Donor Refunds Are More Complex Than They Look

Donor refunds are infrequent enough that most organizations lack a consistent written procedure. A development coordinator handles a duplicate charge, issues a check, and moves on. The CRM record stays unchanged. The tax acknowledgment remains "sent." The general ledger entry never gets made.

When that same situation involves a cross-year refund, a restricted gift, or an amount large enough to affect Form 990 reporting, the incomplete workflow creates real consequences: a donor who files taxes based on a charitable deduction they no longer have, a restricted fund with a phantom balance, and a 990 that overstates gross contributions.

IRS Publication 1771 requires written acknowledgment for all charitable contributions of $250 or more. When a gift at that threshold is refunded, the acknowledgment is no longer accurate. Issuing a correction letter is not optional — it is what allows the donor to accurately represent their giving on a tax return.

## The Cross-Year Problem

The most consequential version of a donor refund crosses a calendar year boundary: a gift received in November or December, refunded in January or later. This is the scenario most likely to cause problems, and the one for which most organizations have no specific procedure.

Under FASB ASC 958, refunds of restricted contributions require elimination of the associated donor restriction from net assets with donor restrictions. The restricted net asset cannot remain on the books once the cash has been returned. But the accounting treatment depends on when the refund is processed, not when the gift was received. A cross-year refund reduces net assets in the year the refund is processed, not in the year the gift was received. The Form 990 treatment follows the same logic.

For the donor, the situation is more complicated. If they claimed a charitable deduction on their prior-year return for a gift that was subsequently refunded, that deduction is no longer valid. The organization's obligation is to document the refund clearly and promptly — not to advise on the donor's tax situation, but to provide the paperwork they need to address it.

## The Eight-Step Workflow

**Step 1: Confirm the request and eligibility**

Receive the refund request in writing. Email is sufficient documentation. Verify the original gift details: amount, date, fund designation, and payment method. Check whether your organization's refund policy covers the situation. Most policies address duplicate charges and processing errors without ambiguity. A donor who changed their mind on a restricted gift may require management or board involvement before proceeding.

**Step 2: Determine the tax-year treatment**

Document whether the refund crosses a tax year before taking any other action. Same-year refunds follow a simpler path. Cross-year refunds require the additional communication steps described below. This determination drives everything that follows.

**Step 3: Process the payment refund**

Refund through the original payment method where possible: credit card to the same card, ACH to the originating account, check reversal via organization check. Retain the transaction confirmation — refund receipt, bank transaction ID, or check copy — as the primary documentation that funds were returned. For credit cards that are no longer valid, coordinate with the payment processor on alternative return methods.

Do not issue a check for a credit card refund without confirming this is the only option. Mismatched payment methods create reconciliation complexity and may confuse the donor's bank records.

**Step 4: Void or revise the tax acknowledgment**

This step is the one most frequently skipped. Under IRS Publication 1771, the original acknowledgment letter must be corrected. Issue a revised letter stating the original gift amount, the refund amount and date, and the net contribution received (zero for a full refund).

For cross-year refunds: the revised letter must explicitly state that the refund was processed in a different tax year than the original contribution. The donor needs this documentation to assess whether their prior-year return requires amendment. The letter should recommend they consult their tax advisor — not because the organization is responsible for their tax situation, but because a prompt, clear correction letter prevents a worse situation later.

**Step 5: Book the general ledger reversal**

Enter a journal entry reversing the original contribution: credit cash (or accounts payable for pending refunds), debit contribution revenue. If the original gift was donor-restricted, also reverse the net asset restriction. The entry is dated as the actual refund date — not the original gift date. This matters for fiscal year reporting.

If the refund crosses a fiscal year boundary and the organization's books for the prior year are already closed, work with the CFO to determine the correct treatment. A same-fiscal-year refund reduces current-year contribution revenue. A cross-year refund reduces current-year net assets.

**Step 6: Update the CRM record**

Flag or reverse the gift in the donor database. A refund that is processed in the payment system but not reflected in the CRM creates a permanent discrepancy. Development staff will see giving totals that include the refunded amount. LYBUNT/SYBUNT retention reports will be overstated. Year-end giving summaries sent to donors will be wrong.

Every refund requires a CRM update. This should be treated as a required step, not a follow-up task.

**Step 7: Address restricted fund implications**

If the refunded gift was restricted to a specific program or fund, management must address any expenditures already made against that restriction. If program costs were incurred in reliance on the gift before the refund was received, the restricted fund may carry a deficit.

Do not leave a restricted fund with a negative balance. Management must decide whether to cover the deficit from operating reserves, a funding reallocation, or a program modification. Document the decision. Under FASB ASC 958, restricted net assets with a negative balance are a reporting error, not an acceptable temporary state.

**Step 8: Assess Form 990 reporting impact**

Form 990 Part VIII reports gross contributions received. A same-year refund reduces the gross total on Part VIII. A cross-year refund appears as a reduction in net assets in the year the refund is processed, not in the year the original gift was received. This appears in the reconciliation of beginning and ending net asset balances.

The CFO and gift officer should both be aware of significant refund amounts. For organizations with tight contribution totals relative to budget assumptions, a large cross-year refund can affect both the current-year 990 and the narrative explanation in Schedule O.

## The Most Common Failure

The single most common mistake in this workflow is failing to void or revise the tax acknowledgment. Organizations process the payment refund, consider the matter closed, and never issue the correction letter.

For same-year refunds, this means the donor is holding a receipt that overstates their charitable giving for the year. For cross-year refunds — particularly refunds processed in January or February, when donors may already be filing taxes — the original acknowledgment may already have been submitted to a tax preparer.

The correction letter is the organization's documentation that it handled the refund correctly. It is also the donor's evidence that the situation occurred. Skipping it exposes the donor to a tax error they cannot correct without the organization's assistance, and creates a record gap if the refund is ever audited.

## A Complete Refund File

For audit purposes, the complete documentation set for any refund should contain: the written refund request from the donor; the original gift record with date, amount, method, and fund designation; payment processor confirmation of the refund with transaction ID and timestamp; the revised or voided tax acknowledgment letter; the general ledger journal entry; the updated CRM record; and management or board approval documentation if the amount exceeds the organization's approval threshold.

All seven elements should be stored together — in a compliance folder, a document management system, or a linked record in the donor platform. Any missing link weakens the audit evidence and creates reconstruction work when the question comes up months or years later.

## What to Do This Week

Organizations without a written donor refund policy should draft one. It does not need to be long. It needs to specify: who can approve a refund at what dollar threshold, which payment methods are acceptable for returns, and what documentation must be assembled before the refund is processed.

Organizations with an existing policy should review whether cross-year refund treatment and restricted fund implications are covered explicitly. Those two scenarios are the ones most likely to generate compliance problems, and they are the scenarios most likely to be handled by staff who have never seen them before.
