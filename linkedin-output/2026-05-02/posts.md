**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

The micro-purchase threshold under 2 CFR 200.320(a)(1) is $10,000. The simplified acquisition threshold is $250,000.

Purchases between $10,001 and $250,000 require price or rate quotations from an adequate number of qualified sources — generally two to three, documented and retained.

Purchases above $250,000 require sealed bids or competitive proposals depending on contract type. Construction and commodities use sealed bids; professional services use competitive proposals.

The anti-fragmentation rule in 2 CFR 200.323 prohibits splitting a $500,000 project into five $100,000 purchases to dodge the threshold.

Procurement noncompliance is one of the most cited categories in the OMB 2024 Compliance Supplement. Purchases above $25,000 also require a SAM.gov debarment check before award.

Organizations whose written procurement policies were last updated before the 2024 Uniform Guidance revision are likely citing stale figures. GrantPipe maintains the procurement audit trail at transaction level so testing is a query, not a reconstruction.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: DonorPerfect handles grant compliance because it has a grant tracking module.

DonorPerfect's grant features cover deadline reminders, fund records, and grant status fields. That is calendar tracking, not compliance.

What it does not cover: tracking actual expenditures against approved grant budget lines, flagging over-expenditure on restricted funds, or producing audit-ready reports showing funders how restricted dollars were spent.

Organizations receiving federal grants or multi-year restricted foundation grants typically run compliance documentation in a second system — usually a spreadsheet or the accounting platform — alongside DonorPerfect.

DonorPerfect reports 93% customer retention and a 13-year average tenure. Those numbers reflect mature donor management, not grant compliance depth.

The myth comes from conflating "grant tracking" (knowing when a report is due) with "grant compliance" (proving how every dollar was spent against an approved budget). They are different jobs.

Audit your grant tools by asking: can it produce a funder financial report from system data alone, or does staff rebuild it in Excel each cycle?

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Address loan and loan guarantee programs separately on the SEFA.

Federal loans get specialized SEFA treatment under 2 CFR 200.502. The outstanding balance at year-end plus new loans received during the year is typically reportable, not just current-year cash receipts.

This applies to PPP loans, SBA programs, USDA rural development loans, and similar federal lending. Organizations treating these as cash transactions on the SEFA understate program expenditures.

The reportable figure has three components in most cases: prior-year outstanding loan balance carried forward, new principal disbursed during the fiscal year, and any forgiven amounts recognized in the current period.

Consult 2 CFR 200.502 and the auditor before finalizing the schedule. Loan reporting rules vary by program type, and the OMB Compliance Supplement clarifies treatment for the largest federal lending programs each year.

Organizations with any federal loan activity should confirm treatment before SEFA delivery to the auditor — getting it wrong on a loan line can shift major-program determination.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Schedule A is the Form 990 attachment where 501(c)(3) organizations prove they qualify as a public charity under IRC Section 509(a).

The most common path is Section 509(a)(1) combined with Section 170(b)(1)(A)(vi): at least 33.33% of total support over a rolling five-year window must come from public sources.

The 2% cap is where concentrated donor bases get hurt. Contributions from any single source that exceed 2% of total five-year support are capped at the 2% level in the public support numerator. Excess counts in the denominator only.

Program service revenue and investment income count in the denominator (total support) but not in the numerator (public support).

Fail both the one-third test and the 10% facts-and-circumstances test for two consecutive years and the IRS reclassifies the organization as a private foundation by operation of law — triggering the 1.39% excise tax under IRC Section 4940, the 5% minimum distribution requirement, and Form 990-PF filing.

About 100,000 private foundations file Form 990-PF annually compared to 1.5 million public charities. The math behind that ratio runs through Schedule A.

**Post 5 — TOOL_TEARDOWN**

Donorbox at $300,000/year in donations costs $5,250 in platform fees alone. At $400,000/year it costs $7,000.

The free tier is genuinely free — no monthly subscription. The 1.75% platform fee is deducted from each donation. Donorbox Pro at $199/month waives the fee on the first $1,000/month of donations.

What Donorbox does well: embeddable donation forms deploy in hours, recurring gift management with a donor-facing portal for payment updates, and zero setup friction for organizations without a software budget.

What it does not do at any tier: full donor CRM with moves management, major gift officer assignment, restricted fund tracking, or grant lifecycle management. Donorbox Pro adds basic CRM features, but they remain shallow relative to purpose-built CRMs.

Donorbox has processed over $2 billion across 50,000+ nonprofits. The product is good at what it does. The problem is total cost of ownership shifts as donation volume grows past roughly $200,000 annually, where the fee model starts crossing the cost of a dedicated CRM with grant compliance built in.

The break-even calculation is rarely run until the organization is already past it.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

The National Endowment for the Arts awarded $147 million in fiscal year 2023. State arts agencies collectively distribute around $500 million annually through state appropriations and NEA grants-to-states.

Arts organizations face a compliance challenge that most nonprofit categories do not: they generate earned revenue. Ticket sales, tuition, facility rentals, and merchandise mix with grant revenue in the operating budget, creating restricted fund separation problems that general-purpose accounting tools handle poorly.

NEA Art Works grants require a final performance report capturing total attendance, demographic data on audiences and artists served, educational programming hours, and geographic reach — separate from the financial report.

State arts agency grants run on each state's fiscal calendar. An organization with a calendar fiscal year, an NEA direct grant on January-December, and a state grant on July-June is managing three different fiscal periods at year-end.

The result: attendance data sits in a program system, financial data sits in accounting software, and connecting them at year-end is a manual exercise that introduces errors and consumes staff time.

36% of nonprofits ended FY2024 with an operating deficit. Arts organizations cannot afford reporting overhead that does not produce funder-ready output.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

If that coordinator spends 10 hours a week on grant reconciliation, funder report assembly, and audit prep, the annual cost is about $19,500. **Post 8 — COMPARISON_INSIGHT**

Bloomerang starts at $125/month. Little Green Light starts at $39/month. Neither manages grants.

Little Green Light is the budget choice for small nonprofits that need a basic donor database — contacts, gifts, pledges, and basic reports. Setup runs 1-3 days. Reporting depth is shallow.

Bloomerang is the mid-tier upgrade. The retention dashboard surfaces at-risk donors, which most nonprofit CRMs ignore entirely. Pricing scales with record count to $249/month. Setup runs 1-2 weeks.

Only 33.3% of nonprofits rate their CRM as effective per the 2025 Fifty & Fifty Nonprofit Peer Report. 46.5% call it neutral. 20.1% call it ineffective.

The shared limitation: neither platform tracks restricted fund expenditures against grant budgets, produces audit-ready compliance reports, or handles the post-award grant lifecycle. Organizations receiving grants run grant management in spreadsheets alongside the CRM and reconcile manually each month.

Choose Little Green Light if budget dominates and the work is donor-only. Choose Bloomerang if retention analytics drive strategy. Choose neither if grants represent more than 20% of revenue — the spreadsheet workaround compounds quickly.

**Post 9 — QUESTION_POST**

A question for executive directors at $500K-$10M nonprofits: how long does board prep currently take, and how many systems does the staff touch to assemble the packet?

The pattern is consistent. Revenue year-to-date comes from QuickBooks. Pipeline status comes from a CRM or a spreadsheet. Retention metrics come from a different report. **Post 10 — PRODUCT_INSIGHT**

Approximately 30% of nonprofits with revenues over $1M operate more than one related legal entity per IRS Statistics of Income data.

The single-database problem: running a 501(c)(3), a 501(c)(4), and a related private foundation in one CRM workspace forces all entity data into a shared environment with manual tags as the only separation. A staff member with access to charity data can see advocacy data because they share a workspace. Compliance calendars merge into one list.

The legal separation exists for regulatory reasons. The data separation should match.

Multi-entity consolidation in GrantPipe gives each legal entity an isolated workspace with its own donor records, grant portfolio, restricted fund ledgers, and compliance calendar. Roles are assigned independently per entity — a user can be Editor on the 501(c)(3) and have no access to the 501(c)(4).

Rollup reporting spans authorized entities with intercompany elimination. When the foundation grants to the operating charity, that transaction appears as revenue and expense in separate workspaces and is netted in the consolidated view, matching FASB ASC 958-810 treatment.

Multi-entity nonprofit structures incur an estimated 40% higher compliance administration cost than single-entity peers per the Nonprofit Finance Fund 2024 State of the Sector. The tooling should reduce that gap, not widen it.
