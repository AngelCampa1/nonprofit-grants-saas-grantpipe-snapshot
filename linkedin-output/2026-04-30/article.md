# Time and Effort Certification: The Federal Grant Compliance Step Most Nonprofits Get Wrong

A nonprofit that charges $400,000 of staff salary to a federal award over twelve months and cannot produce after-the-fact time records owes the federal awarding agency $400,000. Not a portion. The full amount. Personnel costs without supporting time and effort documentation are disallowed in full under 2 CFR 200.430, and the repayment obligation does not scale down for partial documentation.

This is the compliance area that creates more dollar-for-dollar exposure than any other in federal grant management. Personnel costs are 65 to 75 percent of total expenditures on most human services, health, and victim services awards. The HHS Office of Inspector General lists inadequate time and effort documentation as a top-three cause of disallowed costs across federal grant single audits.

The rule is short. The execution is where organizations get into trouble.

## What 2 CFR 200.430 Actually Requires

The Uniform Guidance section governing personnel cost documentation says that records must "accurately reflect the work performed" and be "supported by a system of internal controls which provides reasonable assurance that the charges are accurate, allowable, and properly allocated."

Three principles flow out of that language.

First, the after-the-fact rule. The record must be created based on what happened, not what was planned. The certification covers a period that has already ended. The certified allocation may differ from the budget — and that is acceptable, even required, when actual time differed from budget.

Second, the certifier qualification. The employee can certify their own record. A supervisor with first-hand knowledge of the work performed can certify on behalf of the employee. A finance officer signing timesheets for program staff she does not directly oversee does not have first-hand knowledge.

Third, the documentation must be retained. Under 2 CFR 200.334, time records are kept for 3 years from the date of the final expenditure report for the award. Lost or disorganized records are treated the same as missing records.

The 2024 Uniform Guidance revision confirmed that electronic time records are fully acceptable. A paper timesheet, a spreadsheet, a project tracking tool, or a payroll system with project coding are all acceptable formats — provided the after-the-fact rule and the certifier qualification are satisfied.

## The Most Common Failure Pattern

The most common compliance failure is signing a timesheet that simply copies the budget percentages.

If an employee was budgeted 60 percent to one grant and 40 percent to another, and the timesheet shows exactly 60/40 every month for three years without variation, auditors treat this as presumptive evidence of a budget-based allocation rather than an after-the-fact record. Real work does not produce flat percentages over multiple years. Programs ramp, staff cover for absences, priorities shift across reporting periods. A record that never moves is a record that was never actually filled out from observation.

The second pattern is the payroll system that auto-allocates without employee confirmation. ADP, Paychex, and QuickBooks Payroll can be configured to automatically code an employee's pay to a specific grant each period. The configuration is convenient. It is also a budget-based allocation unless the employee or supervisor confirms the allocation at period end. Auto-allocation without confirmation fails the after-the-fact rule.

The third pattern is the inappropriate certifier. The CFO or finance director signs the timesheet because she controls payroll. She does not have first-hand knowledge of what the program staff actually did. A program director who works alongside or directly oversees the staff member is the right certifier. A supervisor two levels removed who only sees the budget is not.

## The Five-Step Certification Workflow

Organizations that get this right run a standard cycle. The cycle is not complicated. It is the discipline of running it consistently that separates clean audits from disallowed costs.

**Step 1 — Identify all employees with grant-funded time.** Build the list at the start of each project year. Include executives if any portion of their time is budgeted to an award. Update when staff change or budget allocations shift. Missing one employee on this list is the easiest way to end up with no documentation for that employee at audit.

**Step 2 — Choose a record format and stick with it.** Paper timesheets, a spreadsheet template, a time-tracking tool like Harvest or Toggl, or a payroll system with project coding plus end-of-period confirmation. The format matters less than the consistency. Over-engineered systems generate poor compliance because staff avoid them. Under-engineered systems lose data.

**Step 3 — Establish a certification cycle.** Semi-monthly or monthly is the practical standard. The regulation does not specify a frequency, but records certified less frequently than monthly create gaps between actual work and certification. Memory degrades. Contemporaneous records are the goal.

**Step 4 — Separate federal award time from non-award time, and split between awards.** Employees splitting time across awards must show how each period was distributed. When an employee splits between federal awards and non-federal activities, both sides must be documented. Organizations sometimes certify the federal portion and leave the non-federal portion blank — that gap fails the standard.

**Step 5 — Store certified records with audit access.** Three years from final expenditure report submission, organized by award and period, in a location the audit team can pull on request. The grant might have closed in 2025. The records still need to be findable in 2028.

## The Special Rule for 100-Percent Time Employees

Under 2 CFR 200.430(i)(1), employees who work solely on a single federal award may use semi-annual certifications rather than detailed period-by-period timesheets. The conditions are specific.

The employee's effort must be confirmed as 100 percent to a single award for the entire semi-annual period. The certification must be signed by the employee or a supervisor with direct knowledge. The organization must maintain supporting documentation that the employee's duties during the period were consistent with the award's scope.

This exception applies only when there is genuinely no split. If the employee did any work on other awards or non-award activities during the period, even briefly, semi-annual certification fails and period-level records are required. Auditors test this. The exception is narrower than it looks.

## Cost Transfers and Time Records

Time and effort documentation interacts directly with cost transfers — moving previously posted salary from one award to another.

A cost transfer that moves a salary charge from Grant A to Grant B must be supported by time records showing the employee actually worked on Grant B during the period being adjusted. Without those records, the transfer fails the benefit test regardless of whether the timing was within the allowed window.

The right workflow checks time records before initiating personnel cost transfers, not after. Organizations that discover at the point of the transfer that records do not support the reallocation have two options — abandon the transfer or reconstruct records. Retroactive reconstruction is not acceptable. The first option is the only legitimate one.

This is a frequent finding pattern in fiscal years that include grant restructuring. Cost transfers proliferate during budget changes, and time records do not always keep up.

## The Three Findings Auditors Find Most Often

Single audit findings on time and effort cluster into three categories.

Budget-based certifications — timesheets that mirror budget percentages without variation — are the most frequent. The fix is reviewing certified percentages period-over-period before submission and asking the employee or supervisor whether they actually reflect what happened.

Missing records — time records not collected for all covered periods or all covered employees — are second. The fix is the master list at the start of the project year, plus a tracking mechanism for late or missing certifications. Letting a single period slide creates a gap that does not fill itself in.

Inappropriate certifier — certifications signed by finance staff without direct knowledge of the employee's work — are third. The fix is identifying the right certifier when the employee is added to the master list, not when the timesheet is being signed.

A pattern of any of these across the audit sample produces a significant audit finding. In major programs, this can escalate to a material weakness, which carries different consequences for the organization's audit risk rating in subsequent years.

## What This Costs When It Goes Wrong

The dollar exposure is direct. A salary charge without supporting time records is an unallowable cost. Unallowable costs are repaid to the awarding agency in full, not at a partial rate. A single year of a $50,000 program staffer charged to a federal award without proper records is a $50,000 repayment obligation, plus any associated fringe.

The indirect exposure is the audit rating downgrade. Repeat findings under 2 CFR 200.208 can trigger specific conditions — prior approval requirements, enhanced reporting, high-risk auditee designation. These conditions persist until the underlying issue is resolved, and they affect every federal award the organization holds.

The opportunity cost is staff time spent on remediation. Reconstructing missing records, responding to questioned costs, and revising the corrective action plan often consume more staff hours than running the certification cycle correctly would have required in the first place.

## What to Do This Week

Pull the list of employees whose pay is charged to any federal award. For each one, identify who has first-hand knowledge of their work and who currently signs the certification. Where those names do not match, fix it.

Pull the last twelve months of certifications for any employee splitting time across awards. Look at the percentages. If they are flat — exactly 60/40 every month, or exactly 50/50 every month — the next certification needs to reflect actual variation. Continued flat percentages will read as budget-based.

Set the certification cadence at monthly if it is not already there, and put the due date on a calendar with reminders. Late certifications signed more than 30 days after period end are a deficiency, and the calendar discipline is the cheapest insurance available.
