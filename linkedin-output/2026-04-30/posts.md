**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: software marketed as "grant tracking" handles post-award compliance.

Reality: most of it covers the pre-award pipeline only. Pre-award is grant discovery, application stages, deadline reminders, award decisions. Post-award is restricted fund balances by budget category, expenditures linked to grant lines, and SF-425-ready compliance reports pulled from real posted transactions.

The two are different problems with different data models. A tool that tracks "applied → awarded → reporting" without ever touching the GL is a calendar with a logo. It cannot answer the only question that matters at audit: did the dollars spent against this grant match the approved budget categories.

Three capabilities separate compliance tools from pipeline trackers — restricted fund balance tracking by budget category, expenditure documentation linked to individual grant budget lines, and compliance report generation from actual expenditure data. If a tool cannot do those three, it tracks grants informally.

Foundant GrantHub Pro was sunset January 31, 2026, and never covered restricted fund tracking in the first place. Buyers searching for a GrantHub replacement should be very clear about which problem they are actually solving.

The mistake most buyers make is searching for "grant tracking software" when what they actually need is post-award compliance.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Step 4 of responding to a Single Audit finding: draft the Corrective Action Plan with all five required elements under 2 CFR 200.511(c).

The five elements are the finding reference number, the auditee's position (agree or disagree), the specific corrective action, the named responsible party with title, and the target completion date. Vague language like "management will strengthen controls" is not acceptable and produces a follow-up letter from the awarding agency.

Step 5 is committing realistic timelines. Aggressive but achievable — typically 30 to 180 days depending on complexity. A policy update closes in 30. A system implementation may need 180. Missed CAP deadlines become a finding in the next year's audit, which is how organizations end up in repeat-finding territory.

The package is due the earlier of 30 days after the auditor's report or nine months after fiscal year-end, per 2 CFR 200.512(a)(1). The CAP is part of that package and travels with the report to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse at harvester.census.gov.

A strong CAP reads like a project plan, not a compliance document. "The CFO will update the procurement policy by May 31, circulate it with attestations, and present completed attestations to the audit committee at the June meeting" is what auditors and program officers want to see.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

The Unique Entity Identifier is a 12-character alphanumeric code assigned by SAM.gov. It replaced DUNS effective April 4, 2022.

Without an active UEI and current SAM.gov registration, a federal grant recipient cannot draw funds — even on an award already in flight. SAM.gov registration is valid for 12 months. There is no grace period. A registration that lapses on Tuesday halts payments on Wednesday.

The UEI is not the EIN. The EIN is the IRS tax identifier. The UEI is the SAM.gov procurement and grants identifier. Both are required, both go on federal applications, and they are different numbers stored in different systems.

Domestic activation runs 7 to 14 business days, with IRS verification typically the bottleneck. Renewal reminders go out 60 days before expiration. The recommended practice is to start renewal on the first reminder, not the last one.

Pass-through entities have a separate obligation under 2 CFR 200.332: collect the subrecipient's UEI before executing any sub-award and check the SAM.gov exclusions list. If the subrecipient is not registered, the pass-through cannot legally execute the agreement.

Registration lapses are among the top five administrative reasons OMB cites for delayed grant drawdowns. The fix is calendar discipline, not a software purchase.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

A church that receives a USDA Community Food Projects grant cannot spend a single grant dollar on worship services, religious instruction, or staff time supporting religious activities — even if the food pantry runs out of the same building.

Federal funding requires documented separation between grant-funded secular programs and religious activity. That means tracking personnel time by grant-funded versus religious duties, allocating facility costs proportionally, and documenting that grant participants were not required to participate in religious activities. Reconstructed records at audit time do not satisfy the standard.

The data structure most congregations already use makes this harder than it looks. Building Fund, Missions Fund, General Fund, and youth program designations are restricted in the church's internal sense. They are not the same as grant restriction. Commingling a federal award into the General Fund creates Form 990 reporting errors and audit exposure.

There are roughly 380,000 religious congregations in the U.S. with combined annual revenue of $100-$120 billion (Duke National Congregations Study). Faith-based organizations account for about 32% of all U.S. charitable giving (Giving USA 2023). FEMA Public Assistance, USDA Community Food Projects, and historic preservation grants are the most common federal entry points.

The compliance fix is fund separation enforced at the transaction level — not a tag retroactively applied to a journal entry someone already posted to the wrong account.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

Federal grant closeout is not a single event. It starts 90 days before the period of performance ends and continues for 3 to 7 years after the grant formally closes.

The 90-day mark is for budget-versus-actual review, no-cost extension decisions, and subrecipient closeout planning. The 30-day mark is for equipment review (capitalization threshold $10,000 under 2 CFR 200.313), unspent funds calculation, and the final obligation lock. The final week is for expenditure lock and reconciliation to the dollar against the accounting system.

Post-period, the final SF-425 is due within 120 calendar days for most agencies, 90 days for some HUD programs. The cumulative federal expenditures on that report must match the GL exactly. A discrepancy is an audit finding.

Record retention under 2 CFR 200.334 is 3 years from the date of final expenditure report submission — not from the period end date. USDA and HUD programs commonly require 7 years. Records involved in pending audits, litigation, or claims must be retained until the matter resolves.

**Post 9 — QUESTION_POST**

Program directors own program outcomes and the budget that funds them — usually with no formal finance training and no purpose-built tool for the budget-to-delivery connection.

The standard setup at mid-sized nonprofits: program budget in Excel built from the award template, actuals in QuickBooks, reconciliation once a month if someone has time. When a program director approves a subrecipient invoice or authorizes a venue deposit, there is no live check against the remaining budget line. The check happens 30 days later, by which point the overage is booked.

Cost disallowance was named one of the five most common Single Audit findings in the GAO's April 2024 analysis (GAO-24-106173), contributing to the 17% of federal spending linked to severe findings. The Center for Effective Philanthropy puts reporting alone at 6 to 8 hours per grant per year. Multiply by 10 to 20 active grants and a program director loses one to two weeks annually to report assembly.

Question for the program directors and grants managers reading this: when you approve a vendor invoice, can you see the remaining balance on that grant's budget line right then, or do you find out at month-end. Does the disconnect between program tracking and the GL ever surface a 70%-consumed warning early enough to actually file a budget modification.

**Post 10 — PRODUCT_INSIGHT**

Under 2 CFR 200.430, payroll charges to federal awards must be supported by after-the-fact records reflecting actual time worked — not budget percentages. Personnel costs are 65-75% of expenditures on most human services awards and the largest single category of disallowed costs in federal Single Audits.

The two-system problem is where this falls apart. Payroll runs in ADP or QuickBooks. The grants manager keeps a salary allocation spreadsheet. Finance posts journal entries to move costs from a general payroll account to grant-specific accounts. The time-and-effort certifications are PDFs in a shared folder. At audit time, the auditor wants three things lined up — the certification, the allocation entry matching the certification, and the payroll register confirming gross pay. Reconciling three sources for every employee, every month, every award, is the manual work that consumes days and still leaves gaps.

The most common finding is allocation percentages that do not match the certified time — usually because the allocation was entered at budget percentages instead of certified ones. The second most common is late certifications signed more than 30 days after period end. Both create dollar-for-dollar repayment exposure.

GrantPipe records salary and fringe allocations directly to grant ledgers per pay period, links each entry to the corresponding time-and-effort certification, flags percentage mismatches before audit, and supports CSV imports from ADP, Paychex, and QuickBooks Payroll.
