# What a Grant Report Actually Needs to Say

A program officer at a mid-sized community foundation reviews 40 to 60 grant reports per quarter. She is not reading for narrative quality. She is scanning for three data points: are you on track to meet your performance targets, is the money being spent as planned, and are there any signals that warrant a follow-up conversation?

Most reports she reads fail that scan — not because the programs are off track, but because the reports cannot be read quickly. They describe activity without outcome data. They show total expenditures without line-item breakdowns. They include no variance explanations. The program officer marks them for follow-up, which costs her time and signals organizational weakness to the funder.

This is a correctable problem. Understanding what funders actually need in a grant report — and what the two most common report types require — eliminates most of the compliance risk that reporting creates.

## Why Grant Reporting Is a Compliance Function

Grant reporting is commonly treated as a development task: write something compelling, submit it on time, move on. That framing misses the compliance dimension.

For federal grants, the SF-425 Federal Financial Report is a legal document. Missing the submission deadline is a compliance finding under 2 CFR Part 200, independent of whether the underlying spending was compliant. A late report can trigger a hold on future drawdowns from the Payment Management System, creating an organizational cash flow problem. Federal audit findings are public record — they appear on the Federal Audit Clearinghouse and affect future funding eligibility.

For foundation grants, the stakes are relational rather than regulatory, but the consequences are similarly serious. Foundations share information about grantees within their networks. A program officer who needed to send three follow-up emails to get a clear answer from a prior grantee will note that when the organization applies to a different program at the same foundation.

The median nonprofit spends 6 to 8 hours per grant per year on reporting, with cumulative lifetime compliance effort reaching 30 or more hours per grant (Center for Effective Philanthropy Grantee Perception Reports, 2021–2023). That time investment only pays off if the report actually closes the funder's information loop.

## The Two Sections Every Report Must Have

Funder reporting formats vary widely, but the underlying content requirements are consistent across federal agencies, community foundations, and private funders. Every report needs a financial section and a narrative section.

**The financial section** answers the question: was the money spent as planned? The minimum content is a budget-vs-actual table showing the approved budget and actual expenditures for each line item, year-to-date totals, remaining balances, and variance explanations for any category that is more than 10 to 15 percent over or under budget. Funders do not expect perfect alignment to the budget — they expect that variances are visible and explainable.

**The narrative section** answers the question: is the program delivering what was committed? The minimum content is actual outcome data matched against the targets from the original grant application, a description of the activities implemented, the population served, and an explanation of any significant gaps between planned and actual performance.

Neither section is optional. A report with strong narrative but no financial detail leaves the funder unable to verify restricted fund compliance. A report with a clean budget-vs-actual table and no outcome data leaves the funder unable to evaluate programmatic progress.

## The Financial Reconciliation: A Concrete Example

Consider a $50,000 workforce training grant from a state workforce agency, six months into a 12-month performance period.

At the midpoint, a well-formatted report shows each budget category with the approved amount, period expenditures, year-to-date total, remaining balance, and percentage of budget used. If total year-to-date expenditures are approximately $25,000 — 50% of budget at the midpoint — the table communicates alignment without requiring narrative explanation.

Variances that require explanation: a participant stipends line that is 48% of budget at mid-year rather than 50% because a January enrollment delay pushed two cohorts to early February. The variance is minor and explainable. The report notes it and states that the program is on track to meet enrollment targets by Q3.

What is not acceptable: reporting a $25,035 total expenditure figure without the line-item breakdown. The funder cannot determine from the total whether restricted funds were spent in approved categories. Personnel costs at 50% of budget tell a different story than participant stipends at 48% and training materials at 58%. The line-item detail is not optional for any federal report and is standard practice for foundation reports.

## The Narrative Report: Outcomes, Not Activities

This is the section where most reports fail. The pattern is predictable: the report describes what the organization did rather than what the program produced.

Activities are inputs. "The program conducted 14 community health screenings at 8 partner sites" is an activity description. It tells the funder the organization was operating, but not whether the program is succeeding.

Outcomes are what changed because of those activities. "Of 342 unduplicated participants screened, 67 — 20% — received referrals for follow-up care. The program has served 58% of the annual target of 590 participants through the first six months" is an outcome report. It answers the funder's questions directly: are you reaching the people you committed to reach, and is the program having the effect you said it would?

The practical rule: report against the performance targets from the original grant application. If the application committed to serving 200 participants with a 75% employment placement rate, the interim report should show actual enrollment against the 200 target and actual placement rates as they develop. Funders evaluate reports against commitments, not against retrospective assessments of what you should have committed to.

When the program is behind on a target, the report should say so explicitly and explain what corrective action is underway. Funders can work with problems they know about. A program officer who learns about a significant performance gap at the final report — rather than at the midpoint — loses confidence in the organization's monitoring capacity.

## The Three-Part Framework for Any Grant Report

Regardless of funder type, a report that answers these three questions clearly will pass review with minimal follow-up:

**Financial alignment.** Is spending proportional to the grant period elapsed? Are any categories significantly over or under, and if so, why?

**Program progress.** Are activities being implemented as planned? Are the people being served? Are the early outcome indicators moving in the right direction?

**Risk flags.** Is there anything that warrants a conversation with the program officer — a staffing change, an external disruption, a shift in program design, or an anticipated deadline challenge?

The third point is where organizations create unnecessary risk. Withholding information about a program challenge until it becomes a crisis is the most reliable way to damage a funder relationship. Program officers generally respond well to early, honest disclosure of challenges paired with a mitigation plan. They respond poorly to surprises in final reports.

## The Most Common Reporting Mistake

The single most common compliance failure in grant reporting is missing expenditure detail in the financial section. Organizations submit total expenditure figures or cash drawdown amounts without the line-item breakdown that demonstrates restricted fund compliance. This is especially common in reports generated from accounting software that was not set up for grant-level reporting.

The consequence is not always visible immediately. A program officer may accept an incomplete report the first time. An auditor reviewing the same grant months later will not. Under 2 CFR Part 200, expenditure documentation must be contemporaneous — assembled at the time of the transaction, not reconstructed at audit request. A report that does not include line-item expenditure detail signals that the underlying documentation may not exist in audit-ready form.

The fix is structural: a reporting workflow that generates line-item expenditure data from the same system used to track grant transactions, rather than assembling the financial section separately from the accounting data. When the report is produced by manually reconciling two or more systems, each reconciliation introduces error potential and creates the documentation gap that auditors find.

## What to Do This Week

Review the last report you submitted for any active grant. Does the financial section show line-item budget vs. actual? Does the narrative section cite specific outcome data against the targets from the grant application? Does the report explain any variances above 10 to 15 percent?

If the answer to any of those questions is no, the report did not fully close the compliance loop — even if the funder accepted it without follow-up.

Set up a reporting template for each active grant that captures the three required elements before each submission deadline: the budget-vs-actual table pulled from grant-level expenditure data, the outcome data against application targets, and any risk flags worth disclosing. Build the template now, not the week a report is due.
