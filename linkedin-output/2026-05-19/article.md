# The Grant Compliance Calendar Most Nonprofits Build Is Missing Half the Work

A grants manager at a $3M nonprofit sits down in late October to pull together the quarterly SF-425 for a federal workforce development award. The report is due October 30. She has eight days.

The expenditure data is in the accounting system. The time records for split-funded staff are scattered across email threads from the past three months. The drawdown reconciliation for August was never completed. The budget-vs-actual review that should have flagged an overspent travel line in September did not happen because there was no process for it.

This is not a staffing problem. It is a calendar problem — specifically, the kind of compliance calendar that only tracks report due dates and not the recurring operational work that makes accurate reports possible.

## The Reporting Calendar Versus the Compliance Calendar

Most grant calendars at small and mid-sized nonprofits look like reporting calendars: a list of submission deadlines organized by date.

The SF-425 for the workforce development grant is due October 30. The foundation annual report is due December 1. The final programmatic narrative is due 120 days after the period of performance ends, which is February 28.

These deadlines matter. They are the visible surface of the organization's compliance obligations. But a calendar that only shows due dates is a calendar that tells you when you are already behind — not one that helps you arrive prepared.

A compliance calendar includes report submission deadlines. It also includes the recurring operational tasks that generate the data those reports require. Without the underlying work scheduled and owned, the due dates are reminders to scramble rather than prompts for an orderly process.

## What the Recurring Compliance Work Actually Looks Like

For a single federal award with an annual period of performance, the compliance task list is approximately 30 to 40 entries. That count surprises organizations that have been treating federal grants as a report-twice-a-year obligation.

**Drawdown reconciliation.** If the organization draws federal funds from the Payment Management System or a similar federal portal, every drawdown must be reconciled against the accounting system monthly. An unreconciled drawdown in March compounds. A single coding error in month three creates a reconciliation discrepancy that grows every month it goes unresolved. By month nine, the correction requires reconstructing six months of transactions.

**Time and effort documentation.** Under 2 CFR 200.430, personnel costs charged to federal awards must be supported by records that accurately reflect actual work performed. Split-funded staff need monthly effort reports. Staff fully funded by a single award may use semi-annual certifications, but only if they worked genuinely on no other activities during that period. These records are among the most scrutinized items in a single audit. Personnel costs represent 65 to 75 percent of total expenditures on human services and health grants. Missing time records do not produce partial disallowances — they produce dollar-for-dollar disallowed costs and repayment demands.

**Budget-vs-actual review.** Monthly review of expenditures against approved budget categories catches problems early. Is the travel line 85 percent spent at month four of a twelve-month period? Is an indirect cost rate calculation off? Is a program running under-budget in a way that suggests the award's scope is behind schedule? Monthly review means monthly course correction. The alternative is discovering at month ten that the organization has a problem that requires a budget modification, a no-cost extension, or a conversation with the program officer.

**Subrecipient monitoring.** Organizations that pass federal funds to subrecipient organizations carry monitoring obligations under 2 CFR 200.332. Quarterly desk reviews of subrecipient expenditure reports, invoice review before payment, and documented site visits per the monitoring plan. These are compliance tasks that must be scheduled and completed — not reminders that the subrecipient exists.

## The Five-Task Framework for Building the Calendar

A compliance calendar built around five task categories covers the recurring work for most federal and foundation awards.

**Reporting deadlines.** Enter every submission deadline from every award agreement using the actual dates from the document — not approximate dates from memory. Federal interim reports are typically due 30 days after the close of each reporting period. Final financial reports are due 120 days after the period of performance ends. Foundation due dates are in the grant agreement; they vary and cannot be approximated from memory.

**Preparation start dates.** For each report due date, back-calculate a preparation start date. Quarterly SF-425 reports need 10 to 14 business days of preparation. Annual foundation reports typically need 15 to 20 business days, accounting for narrative drafting, financial reconciliation, and internal review. Set calendar reminders at the preparation start date, not just the submission deadline. A report due October 30 needs a preparation start date of October 14 or earlier.

**Monthly reconciliation tasks.** For each federal award, schedule a monthly drawdown reconciliation on a fixed date — the last business day of the month works for most organizations. Schedule budget-vs-actual reviews for the fifth business day after month end, when the prior month's accounting is typically closed. These tasks are monthly, not quarterly. Organizations that only do budget reviews at report time are operating without course-correction capability for most of the grant period.

**Time and effort collection.** Schedule effort report collection from split-funded staff on a fixed day each month — the last Friday of the month is a common standard. Schedule semi-annual certification reminders for staff fully funded by a single award. These tasks exist independently of reporting cycles. They generate the documentation that makes personnel costs allowable.

**Subrecipient monitoring checkpoints.** If the organization passes federal funds to subrecipients, schedule quarterly desk reviews 15 days before the close of each quarter. That lead time allows the review to be completed and documented before the period closes. Site visit scheduling, invoice review requirements, and monitoring report due dates are layered on top of the quarterly cycle based on the terms of each subaward.

## The Most Common Failure: Ownership Without Accountability

The most predictable way a compliance calendar fails is not that the tasks are missing. It is that the tasks are listed without a named owner.

A calendar entry that says "SF-425 due October 30 — Finance" does not create accountability. One that says "SF-425 for Workforce Development Award due October 30 — Maria Rivera" does.

Every item on a compliance calendar must have a specific person's name attached. Not a department. Not a role. A person. When the owner is on planned leave during a deadline period, the backup must be named in advance — not figured out when the owner is already out.

This is especially important for organizations where compliance responsibilities span two functions. Development staff manage funder relationships and narrative components. Finance staff manage expenditure tracking, drawdown reconciliation, and financial report sections. The compliance calendar is where these two sets of tasks are coordinated. If it is maintained only by one function, the other function's tasks accumulate as blind spots.

## Scale Changes the Infrastructure Requirement

Five active grants — a mix of two federal awards and three foundation grants — generate roughly 80 to 120 compliance calendar entries per year. That volume is manageable in a shared calendar application with discipline and consistent naming conventions.

Fifteen active grants, with five federal and ten foundation, generate 250 to 400 compliance calendar entries per year. At this scale, the compliance calendar becomes its own maintenance burden. Monthly reconciliation tasks for five federal awards alone total 60 entries per year. The probability that one gets missed increases with every entry added. Manual calendar maintenance becomes itself a risk.

Purpose-built grant management software addresses this by generating compliance tasks automatically from each grant's award terms. The monthly drawdown reconciliation appears because the award is a federal award. The subrecipient monitoring tasks appear because the award includes pass-through funding. The task schedule is a byproduct of the grant record — not a separate document that must be maintained in sync with the record and updated every time an award is amended or a no-cost extension is granted.

## The Single Audit Dimension

Organizations approaching or exceeding $1,000,000 in federal expenditures in a fiscal year trigger single audit requirements. The compliance calendar has specific single audit obligations that must be added separately.

The Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards (SEFA) preparation should begin 60 to 90 days before fiscal year end, not two weeks before the audit fieldwork starts. The SEFA lists every federal award by Assistance Listing Number (ALN); ALN errors on the SEFA are a compliance finding in their own right. SEFA cluster aggregation — combining related ALNs for major-program threshold calculation — requires accurate expenditure data by award throughout the year, not just at year end.

The single audit submission deadline is nine months after fiscal year end for organizations that exceed the threshold. That nine-month window covers audit fieldwork, management letter response, and FAC (Federal Audit Clearinghouse) submission. Building the SEFA preparation start date, the audit fieldwork period, and the submission deadline into the compliance calendar — rather than treating single audit as a separate process — prevents the common situation where compliance tasks during the year are not documented in a way that supports the audit.

## The Common Mistake and Its Consequence

The single most common compliance calendar failure is treating the reporting calendar and the compliance calendar as the same thing.

Organizations that only calendar report due dates arrive at each deadline with the data-assembly problem concentrated in the two weeks before submission. Finance staff pull three months of transactions from the accounting system. Development staff reconstruct narrative metrics from program notes. Time records are collected from staff who certified them in name but not in substance.

The funder report gets submitted. But the underlying compliance documentation — the drawdown reconciliation, the certified time records, the documented budget-vs-actual review — reflects two weeks of scramble rather than twelve months of consistent practice. In a year where a funder conducts a desk review or a federal auditor requests documentation supporting a specific expenditure, that difference is material.

The organizations that arrive at report deadlines with confidence have done the same work — they have just distributed it across the twelve months of the grant period rather than compressing it into the two weeks before each due date.

## What to Do This Week

Pull the award documents for your three most active grants. List every compliance requirement explicitly stated in each document — not just the report due dates, but the monitoring obligations, drawdown requirements, time and effort requirements, and budget modification thresholds. Then check which of those requirements are currently on your calendar as scheduled, owned tasks versus which exist only as institutional knowledge.

That gap — between what the award agreement requires and what is actively scheduled and owned — is the compliance calendar you need to build.
