# New York CHAR500: What Nonprofit Finance Staff Need to Know Before May 15

A New York nonprofit receives a deficiency notice from the Charities Bureau in June. The filing went in on time, the fee was paid, and the Form 990 was attached. The notice says the audit is missing.

The organization's gross revenue plus support crossed $1 million two years ago. Nobody updated the CHAR500 preparation process when that happened. The audit requirement under NY Executive Law 172-b had been triggered for two consecutive years. Both filings were incomplete.

This is not a rare scenario. The threshold that triggers a compliance obligation is easy to miss when financial thresholds shift during a growth period and the annual filing checklist doesn't get updated accordingly.

## What CHAR500 Is

CHAR500 is the Annual Filing for Charitable Organizations administered by the New York Attorney General's Charities Bureau. It is separate from federal Form 990, separate from state corporate filings, and separate from any federal grant reporting obligations.

New York regulates charities under two statutes. Article 7-A of the Executive Law governs charitable solicitation. The Estates, Powers and Trusts Law (EPTL) governs organizations holding charitable assets in New York. Many organizations are dual-registered under both, which affects both the fee calculation and the compliance scope.

Every charitable organization registered with the Charities Bureau must file CHAR500 annually. That includes New York-incorporated 501(c)(3) public charities, out-of-state charities that solicit contributions from New York residents, charitable trusts holding New York assets, and private foundations with New York nexus. Religious organizations and certain educational institutions have specific exemptions. Most conventional 501(c)(3) nonprofits do not qualify for exemption.

## The Due Date and Extension Window

CHAR500 is due four months and fifteen days after the end of the organization's fiscal year. For calendar-year filers, that is May 15.

One six-month extension is available, but it must be requested before the original due date. An approved federal Form 990 extension extends CHAR500 automatically when documented on the filing.

Missing the May 15 deadline without an approved extension is not a minor administrative issue. Late filings without an extension can result in deficiency notices, fees, and — if unresolved — suspension of solicitation authority in New York. Organizations that lose solicitation authority in New York cannot legally fundraise from New York residents until the status is restored.

## The Fee Calculation Problem

The Charities Bureau rejects a significant number of filings for incorrect fee amounts. Understanding why requires understanding the two separate fee structures.

Article 7-A fees are based on gross contributions, under tiers established by NY Executive Law 172-b. Organizations raising less than $25,000 pay $10. Organizations in the $25,000 to $100,000 range pay $50. The $100,001 to $250,000 band is $100. The $250,001 to $1,000,000 band is $150. Above $1,000,000, the fee is $250 (with higher tiers available for very large organizations).

EPTL fees are calculated separately based on net worth, starting at $25 and scaling upward for large endowments.

Dual registrants pay both fees. An organization registered under both Article 7-A and EPTL that calculates only the Article 7-A fee and submits that amount will receive a deficiency notice. The cure window is typically 30-60 days, but the delay pushes the acceptance date and creates administrative follow-up that compounds during busy filing seasons.

The practical error most often seen: organizations that moved from one gross contributions tier to another during the year — due to a successful fundraising campaign, a major gift, or a new grant — and prepared the CHAR500 using the prior year's fee tier.

## The Three-Tier Financial Attestation Framework

This is where the most consequential errors occur.

Under NY Executive Law 172-b, Article 7-A registrants face financial attestation requirements based on gross revenue plus support — not gross contributions, not net revenue, not total expenses.

Above $1,000,000 in gross revenue plus support: an independent audit by a CPA is required. The audited financial statements must be attached to the CHAR500 filing.

Above $250,000 and at or below $1,000,000: a CPA review is required. Reviewed financials must be attached.

At or below $250,000: neither is required.

These thresholds are independent of the federal Single Audit threshold, which is now $1 million in federal expenditures under the 2024 revision to 2 CFR 200.501. Organizations above both thresholds face parallel attestation obligations. In practice, the single CPA engagement typically coordinates both, but the legal authority is separate and the triggering calculation differs.

The most common error here is not the calculation — most finance staff know their revenue figures. The error is failing to update the CHAR500 preparation process when gross revenue plus support crosses a threshold for the first time. An organization that crossed $250,000 in Year 3 and never engaged a CPA for Years 3, 4, or 5 has filed three deficient returns.

## The Three-Step CHAR500 Readiness Process

**Step 1: Calculate both figures before doing anything else.**

Gross contributions (for the fee) and gross revenue plus support (for the attestation requirement) are not the same number and do not come from the same line of the financial statements. Compute both explicitly during the fiscal year-end close process, before the CPA engagement conversation begins. If gross revenue plus support is near a threshold, confirm with the organization's CPA whether the current year will cross it.

**Step 2: Engage the CPA with adequate lead time.**

The May 15 due date is not the date to begin auditor conversations. For organizations requiring an audit, CPA engagement must happen early enough for fieldwork to be complete, management response periods to be completed, and final financial statements to be ready before the filing deadline.

A practical working calendar for calendar-year filers: CPA engagement confirmed by December, fieldwork January through February, draft financials reviewed by April, final statements available for the CHAR500 by early May.

Organizations that wait until April to engage a CPA for an audit will miss May 15. CPA capacity during the spring filing season is finite.

**Step 3: Verify every attachment before submission.**

The online Charities Bureau filing system checks for attachment presence and signature requirements before accepting the submission. The most common reasons for rejection: missing Form 990 (the complete federal return, not a summary), missing audit or review when the threshold is met, and missing the second officer signature.

CHAR500 requires two officer signatures — typically the president or authorized officer and the chief financial officer. A single signature is insufficient regardless of organizational structure.

## The Single Failure That Creates Cascading Problems

The most common single failure in CHAR500 compliance is not a calculation error or an incorrect fee tier. It is failing to update the organization's annual compliance process when a financial threshold changes.

Growth is typically what triggers this. An organization that spent several years below $250,000 in gross revenue plus support crosses the threshold during a strong fundraising year or after winning a significant grant. The same person who prepared the CHAR500 in prior years prepares it again using the same checklist. The CPA review engagement line is not on that checklist because it was never needed before.

The consequence is a deficient filing. If the deficiency is caught quickly through a Charities Bureau notice and cured within the window, the practical impact is a few weeks of administrative work. If the deficiency goes unaddressed — which happens when organizations move to new finance staff who do not know the filing history — the organization accumulates years of incomplete compliance records.

The cure is structural: build the threshold calculation explicitly into the year-end financial close checklist, not into the CHAR500 preparation checklist. By the time the CHAR500 is being prepared, the CPA engagement should already be contracted.

## What to Do This Week

For calendar-year New York nonprofits, May 15 is close. Three things to verify before submitting:

First, pull the gross revenue plus support figure from the current-year financials and confirm whether a CPA audit or review is required. If it is required and not yet contracted, contact a CPA firm this week.

Second, confirm the correct Article 7-A and EPTL fee calculation. Do not assume the same fee as last year if revenue changed materially.

Third, verify that the complete federal Form 990 is ready to attach. CHAR500 requires the full Form 990, not a summary. If the 990 is still in draft, coordinate with the preparer on the timeline.
