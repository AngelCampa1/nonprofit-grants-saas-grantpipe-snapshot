**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

HUD awarded approximately $3.3 billion in CDBG formula funds in FY2024, and a significant share flowed through nonprofit subrecipients who are now running four separate compliance systems simultaneously.

CDBG (24 CFR 570), HOME (24 CFR 92), ESG (24 CFR 576), and CoC (24 CFR 578) share IDIS as the reporting backbone, but diverge on almost every operational rule.
CDBG demands income documentation for every LMI benefit activity claimed under 24 CFR 570.208.
HOME triggers affordability covenants that can run 20 years for new rental construction.
ESG requires 100 percent match on all expenditures outside HMIS and administration.
CoC chronic homelessness documentation must be in the participant file at program entry — not reconstructed later.
The OIG findings that appear most often across HUD monitoring are the places where these four programs were treated as one.

If your organization manages two or more CPD programs on overlapping populations, the compliance framework separating them deserves the same attention as the program work itself.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

North Carolina has approximately 50,000 registered nonprofits — and a Charlotte-area organization managing a DHHS behavioral health contract, a Bank of America foundation grant, and a federal HUD community development award is tracking three separate fiscal calendars at once.

NC DHHS contracts align with the state's July 1 through June 30 fiscal year.
Federal research and HUD grants run on the federal October 1 through September 30 calendar.
Charlotte banking-sector foundations — Bank of America, Wells Fargo — operate on corporate fiscal years tied to internal giving cycles.
None of these align with each other.
The NC Department of Justice also requires a Charitable Solicitation License before the first solicitation, renewed annually and tied to the organization's own fiscal year.

The myth that state compliance is simpler than federal compliance disappears quickly when you map what each funder actually requires and when each deadline actually falls.

Tracking multiple fiscal calendars in a shared spreadsheet is where reporting errors first appear.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

A cost transfer discovered after federal grant closeout is not an administrative correction — it is a compliance event that requires written federal prior approval before a single journal entry is booked.

Under 2 CFR 200.344, grantees have 120 calendar days after the period of performance ends to liquidate obligations and submit final reports.
An award past that window is administratively closed. The financial records were certified as correct.
Any correction now requires contacting the federal program officer and grants management specialist in writing, with documentation of the original expenditure, proof the cost was incurred during the period of performance, and a narrative explaining the error.
Average federal response time: 30 to 90 days.
Do not process the journal entry until written authorization arrives — an unauthorized correcting entry creates a second compliance problem on top of the original.
If the transfer changes figures on the final SF-425, an amended FFR must also be submitted.

Organizations that discover closeout errors during annual audit preparation — typically six to eight months post-fiscal-year — often find the 120-day window has already closed. Monthly reconciliation is what keeps this from becoming a federal correspondence project.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

The single audit threshold is now $1,000,000 in federal expenditures in a fiscal year — updated under the 2024 revisions to 2 CFR Part 200, effective for fiscal years ending September 30, 2025 or later.

The threshold applies to total federal expenditures across all awards, not to any single grant.
Four federal grants of $250,000 each, all active in the same year, cross the threshold.
So does $1.1 million in state pass-through funding where the underlying dollars originated from a federal program — because those are federal awards expended, even if the immediate funder is a state agency.
The single audit has two simultaneous components: a financial statement audit and a federal program compliance audit.
The compliance audit tests each major program against the OMB Compliance Supplement and produces additional reports on internal control.
Findings appear in the Federal Audit Clearinghouse — publicly visible to every federal funder and pass-through entity.

Organizations tracking their federal expenditures against the old threshold should update their monitoring and confirm whether their fiscal year end date means the new threshold applies to their next audit.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

Most major donor solicitations fail because the ask arrives before the relationship.

The 90-day cultivation timeline is a minimum framework, not a ceiling.
Days 1 through 14: discovery — first contact, prospect research, initial meeting focused entirely on listening, not pitching.
Days 15 through 30: engagement — a facility tour or program observation, a personalized impact story tied to what the prospect said they care about.
Days 31 through 60: deepening — a board member introduction with genuine relationship, not a transactional one; an insider experience before something is publicly announced.
Days 61 through 75: internal alignment on ask amount, who makes the ask, and what the specific funding opportunity is.
Days 76 through 90: solicitation — in person, with a clear and specific ask, followed by silence.

A donor who has been thoughtfully cultivated is not deciding whether to respond to a solicitation. They are deciding whether the amount you are asking for matches what they were already considering.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

68 percent of nonprofits using QuickBooks for fund tracking maintain at least one parallel spreadsheet for restricted balance management, averaging 8.5 hours per month in maintenance.

The core issue: QuickBooks Classes are cost categories, not fund accounts.
A Class report shows what you spent. A fund account shows what you have left. Auditors testing restricted fund balances want the fund account — not the Class report.
FASB ASC 958 requires nonprofits to track restricted and unrestricted net assets separately, document releases from restriction with qualifying expenditures, and maintain the transaction trail. Cost categories do not satisfy this.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

Illinois charities registered with the Attorney General must file Form AG990-IL within six months of fiscal year end. The filing fee is $15. The audit and review thresholds are easy to miss.

Gross contributions above $300,000: independent audit required as an attachment.
Gross contributions between $150,000 and $300,000: financial review by a licensed CPA required.
Below $150,000: neither required, but the full AG990-IL is still due.
A complete copy of the federal Form 990 is required as an attachment in every case — including organizations filing 990-N at the federal level, who still file full financial detail at the state level.
The most common rejection reasons: missing or unsigned form, missing audit or review attachment, missing Form 990, incorrect fiscal year, or filing after the due date without an approved extension.
Extensions extend only the filing deadline. They do not extend an audit engagement that has not yet started.

For calendar-year filers, the audit fieldwork needs to begin in January or February to have the attachment ready for a June 30 due date. Starting in May produces rush fees, not savings.
