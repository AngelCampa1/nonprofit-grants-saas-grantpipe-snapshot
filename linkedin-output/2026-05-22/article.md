# How to Prepare a Clean SEFA Before Your Single Audit Begins

The finance director at a $2.8 million nonprofit received the auditor's fieldwork schedule in October. The engagement would begin in three weeks. The SEFA — the Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards — was not prepared. It had never been prepared before. The prior year, an outside consultant had assembled it at the last minute from bank records and grant files, and the auditor had spent two days requesting clarifications before the document was accepted.

That scenario plays out every year at organizations that treat the SEFA as an audit deliverable rather than a year-round financial document. The cost is measurable: extended fieldwork, higher audit fees, and — in the worst cases — findings that appear in the Federal Audit Clearinghouse and require corrective action plans.

Incomplete SEFA preparation is among the five most common Single Audit findings identified by GAO in its April 2024 analysis (GAO-24-106173). The same analysis linked $1.17 trillion of federal award spending from 2017 through 2021 to severe and persistent Single Audit findings. This is not a paperwork problem. It is a financial controls problem with public consequences.

## What the SEFA Actually Does

The SEFA lists every federal award expended during the fiscal year. It is organized by federal agency and by Assistance Listing Number (ALN) — the same number previously called the CFDA number, now maintained in SAM.gov. Each line shows the pass-through entity if the award came through a state or local government, the total amount expended during the fiscal year, and any amounts passed through to subrecipients.

This document determines the scope of your Single Audit. The auditor uses it to identify Type A programs — those large enough to require testing under the dollar threshold in 2 CFR 200.518 — and to select major programs for compliance testing. A clean, accurate SEFA narrows audit scope. A SEFA with missing awards, misclassified pass-throughs, or totals that do not reconcile to the financial statements expands scope and generates questions that delay the audit.

The current Single Audit threshold is $1,000,000 in federal expenditures in a fiscal year under the 2024 revisions to 2 CFR Part 200, effective for fiscal years ending September 30, 2025 or later. Organizations should update their monitoring and confirm fiscal-year applicability with their auditor.

## The Four-Part SEFA Framework

### Part 1: Federal Award Inventory

The foundation of the SEFA is a complete list of every federal award active during the fiscal year. This means direct federal grants, cooperative agreements, and — critically — awards received as sub-awards from pass-through entities.

Pass-through awards are where organizations most often go wrong. A grant received from the North Carolina Department of Health and Human Services for a mental health program may be a pass-through of federal Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA) funds. The grant agreement from DHHS may not make this obvious. The SEFA must list this award under the prime federal agency (HHS/SAMHSA) and disclose NC DHHS as the pass-through entity — not list it as a state grant.

The practical step: capture pass-through status at award acceptance, not at SEFA preparation time. When a new grant agreement arrives, read it for any mention of the federal program, ALN number, or pass-through entity. Document that information in your grant record. Trying to reconstruct federal origin at year-end from a stack of grant agreements is how SEFA preparation takes 40 hours instead of 8.

### Part 2: General Ledger Grant Coding

The SEFA totals must be supported by general ledger detail. For every award on the SEFA, there must be a corresponding set of expense transactions in the accounting system showing what was charged against that award during the fiscal year.

This is where the difference between real fund accounting and a workaround becomes operational. An accounting system with grant-level coding — whether through restricted fund accounts, project codes, or dimensions — produces SEFA data with a single export. The totals per grant reconcile to the ledger automatically because the ledger was coded to grants throughout the year.

QuickBooks Classes are the most common workaround in this space. Classes track expenses by category but do not maintain fund balances. A Class report shows total spending under a label. It does not maintain the fund account, does not automatically reconcile to the Statement of Activities, and does not document the release-from-restriction event required under FASB ASC 958. For the SEFA specifically, Class data is usable if grant-level Classes were maintained. But when expenses were coded only to functions (Program, Management, Fundraising), the SEFA must be reconstructed transaction by transaction.

### Part 3: Pass-Through Identification and Subrecipient Disclosure

Two separate disclosures are required under 2 CFR 200.510:

For awards received as sub-awards, the SEFA must identify the pass-through entity by name and UEI, and show the prime federal agency and ALN.

For amounts your organization passed through to subrecipients, the SEFA must disclose the total per federal program. This is a required line item, not an optional footnote. If your organization acts as a pass-through entity — administering a CDBG subaward from a jurisdiction, for example, and regranting to a smaller nonprofit — the amounts passed through must appear on your SEFA.

These two disclosures are not symmetric. One documents where your award came from. The other documents where you sent federal dollars downstream.

### Part 4: Reconciliation to the Statement of Activities

The grand total of federal expenditures on the SEFA must reconcile to federal revenue recognized on the Statement of Activities. This reconciliation is prepared by the auditee, reviewed by the auditor during fieldwork, and expected to be documented before fieldwork begins.

Common reconciling items include unearned federal revenue (cash received but not yet expended), prior-year receivables collected and expended in the current year, and timing differences on multi-year awards. Each item must be documented — a line in a reconciliation schedule with a brief explanation.

Auditors will ask for this documentation. If it is not prepared, the auditor prepares it during fieldwork, at your expense.

## The Common Mistake That Generates Findings

The single most common SEFA error is pass-through misclassification. An organization lists a state agency grant under the state agency. The auditor identifies the federal program underlying the award. The SEFA is corrected, but the finding is documented: the organization lacked controls to identify federal pass-through awards at intake.

The consequence is not just a finding note. The finding appears in the Federal Audit Clearinghouse, visible to every federal funder and pass-through entity that reviews your organization's compliance history. It must be addressed in a corrective action plan. If the same error appears in subsequent years, it escalates from a deficiency to a material weakness.

The correction is straightforward but must happen upstream: at the point of award acceptance, not at year-end.

## What the Auditor Needs and When

Deliver the SEFA draft to your auditor at least 30 days before fieldwork begins. This timeline is not arbitrary. The auditor uses the SEFA to determine major programs, draft compliance testing procedures, and allocate fieldwork hours. A late SEFA compresses the auditor's preparation, increases the chance that the auditor identifies errors during fieldwork rather than in advance, and extends the engagement timeline.

The draft should be complete: all awards, all pass-through disclosures, all subrecipient pass-through amounts, and a documented reconciliation to the Statement of Activities. A SEFA delivered 30 days before fieldwork with one reconciling item still outstanding is better than a SEFA delivered one week before fieldwork with clean numbers.

## Building the Habit Before Year-End

The organizations that prepare clean SEFAs quickly are not doing more work at year-end. They are doing smaller, consistent work throughout the year.

Three practices drive clean SEFA preparation:

Capture ALN numbers at award acceptance. Every grant agreement should be reviewed for the federal program identifier before it is filed. This takes five minutes at intake and saves hours at year-end.

Maintain grant-level coding in the general ledger. Expense transactions should reference the specific grant throughout the year. Even in a simple system, a project code or restricted fund designation by grant produces the year-end extract without reconstruction.

Reconcile grant totals to the general ledger quarterly. A quarterly review of spending by grant versus the ledger catches coding errors — costs charged to the wrong grant, costs coded to function without grant attribution — while the year is still open. Correcting a misposted expense in August is a two-minute journal entry. Correcting it in November during SEFA preparation, when the award may have already entered closeout, is a compliance event.

## What to Do This Week

If your fiscal year ends in the next six months, pull your federal award inventory now. List every active award with its ALN number and pass-through entity. Check each one against your general ledger coding — can you trace every expense back to the specific award? If not, identify where the gaps are and close them before year-end.

If your fiscal year recently ended and SEFA preparation has not started, start with pass-through identification. Confirm which awards are federal and under which prime agency each one belongs. That determination drives everything else on the document.
