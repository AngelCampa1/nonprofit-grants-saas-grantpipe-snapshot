**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Under 2 CFR 200.430(i), employees working solely on a single federal award may certify time semi-annually. Employees split across multiple awards — or across an award and non-award duties — must certify more frequently.

Most organizations over-apply semi-annual certification. An employee working 80% on one federal grant and 20% on administrative duties is not a single-award employee. Monthly certification is required for that person.

Single Audit fieldwork tests this directly. Auditors look at employee classification, then at certification frequency. A mismatch is a finding.

The most defensible system: certify monthly for everyone on federal awards, distribute within two weeks of period end, require return within one week of distribution.

The certification itself must reflect an after-the-fact distribution of actual time — not a projection. A form that shows only the employee name and grant number without actual time percentages is not compliant.

The reconciliation step that most organizations skip: compare certification percentages to what was posted to payroll for the same period. If an employee certified 50% on Grant A and payroll shows 55%, the posting is wrong and needs a correcting entry.

Personnel cost findings are among the most frequent in federal single audits. Inadequate time documentation and misallocated charges appear in GAO reports every cycle.

The system itself is not complicated. Running it every month, without exception, is where most teams struggle.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

UBTI — Unrelated Business Taxable Income — applies when a tax-exempt organization earns money from a commercial activity that fails all three of these tests: it is a trade or business, it is regularly carried on, and it is not substantially related to the exempt purpose.

All three must apply. Failing any one of them means the income is not UBTI.

The word "substantially" in the third test is where most analysis concentrates. The question is not whether the profits fund the mission. The question is whether the activity itself contributes importantly to accomplishing the exempt purpose.

A nonprofit hospital's parking garage open to the general public: UBTI. A university bookstore selling required textbooks to enrolled students: program service revenue. The same bookstore selling branded merchandise to the general public: potentially UBTI on that portion.

Since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, each unrelated business activity is treated as a separate silo under IRC Section 512(a)(6). Losses from one activity cannot offset income from another. The strategy of using a rental property loss to shelter a profitable parking lot is no longer available.

The Form 990-T filing threshold is $1,000 in gross UBTI income. The tax rate is 21%, regardless of the organization's exempt status.

More than 30,000 tax-exempt organizations file Form 990-T annually. The distinction between program service revenue and UBTI is one of the most consequential — and most frequently miscategorized — lines in nonprofit tax compliance.

**Post 6 — HOW_TO**

Under 2 CFR 200.303, federal grantees must maintain effective internal controls over federal awards. "Effective" means written, operational, and demonstrable — not a reference to a general purchasing policy.

Most nonprofits have a purchasing policy. Grant spending controls are a specific layer on top of it.

The distinction: a purchasing policy governs how the organization buys things generally. Grant spending controls govern expenditures against specific restricted funds, within specific approved budgets, subject to specific funder requirements.

Organizations that point to their purchasing policy when asked about grant spending controls are describing a gap, not a system.

The authorization framework needs three defined levels: routine expenditures within approved categories, non-routine expenditures requiring finance or executive review, and items that require written funder prior approval before the expenditure is made.

Segregation of duties under 2 CFR 200.303 requires distributing the initiation, authorization, recording, and reconciliation functions across different individuals. For small teams where full segregation is not achievable, compensating controls substitute — typically a second-level review documented in writing and actually performed.

The test that matters most: before your auditor arrives, pull 10 recent grant expenditures and verify each one had a documented prior approval from the correct person under your authorization matrix. If any fail, that is a finding preview.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

A grant kickoff meeting done in 20 minutes without an agenda is not a kickoff. It is a formality that leaves the program lead, finance staff, and grants manager with different understandings of the same award.

A useful kickoff covers seven specific items before any spending begins: award overview, approved budget walkthrough line by line, fund restrictions and prior approval requirements, the complete reporting schedule with named owners, roles and responsibilities for each compliance function, grant management system setup confirmation, and an open items log.

The approved budget walkthrough is where most kickoffs fail. The finance director needs to know what each line covers and what documentation is required. The program lead needs to know what cannot be charged even if it seems reasonable. Running through the budget together, with both in the room, surfaces the interpretation gaps before they become misallocation findings.

The reporting schedule section should produce a documented calendar: every report type, reporting period, due date, prep start date, who prepares, who reviews, and who submits. Not a verbal agreement — a table in the meeting record.

The open items log matters because decisions made verbally in a kickoff meeting have a way of being remembered differently by different people six months later. Writing them down, assigning owners, and setting due dates converts a conversation into a compliance record.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

Federal grant draws are compliance events, not routine cash transfers. Each one must be supported by actual expenditures in the accounting system, reconciled monthly, and documented with enough detail that an auditor can trace every dollar from the draw request to the underlying expense.

The two payment methods — advance and reimbursement — carry different timing requirements. Under 2 CFR 200.305(b)(3), advance cash must be disbursed within 30 calendar days. Cash held beyond 30 days is potentially subject to interest calculation and remittance. Interest earned on federal cash above $500 per year must be returned to the federal government.

The reconciliation that prevents cash management single audit findings: monthly, cumulative draws to date should equal cumulative expenditures recorded in the general ledger for that award. If draws exceed expenditures, the organization is holding federal cash it has not yet spent — a compliance issue under 2 CFR 200.305 that requires the excess to be returned or offset against the next draw.

Most organizations discover these gaps at audit time. Gaps accumulate monthly and become much harder to trace and correct in bulk than they would have been at each month-end close.

Each draw should have a documented log entry: award number, payment system request number, date submitted, period covered, federal share claimed, the expenditure report that supported the draw, and the system confirmation. This log is the source data for the Federal Cash Transactions section of the SF-425.
