# Time-and-Effort Certification: The Monthly Control That Makes Payroll Auditable

A grants manager at a $3M workforce development nonprofit discovers during Single Audit fieldwork that three employees certified their time semi-annually — but two of them split time between a federal workforce grant and the organization's general fund. The auditor flags it as a compliance error under 2 CFR 200.430(i). Questioned costs on both employees' salaries for the full year are now on the table.

This is not an unusual scenario. It is one of the most consistent findings in federal single audits, and it stems from a rule that most finance staff understand in outline but misapply in practice: the semi-annual certification exception is narrower than organizations assume.

## What the Regulation Actually Requires

Under 2 CFR 200.430, payroll charges to federal awards must reflect actual time worked. The mechanism for demonstrating that is the time-and-effort certification — a signed document confirming how an employee's time was distributed across grants and other activities for a specific period.

The after-the-fact confirmation principle is the core requirement: charges must be based on records that reflect actual work, confirmed after the period ends. Budget-based allocations that are never reconciled to actual time violate this principle regardless of how carefully the budget was constructed.

The semi-annual option is real, but it is constrained. Employees working solely on a single federal award or cost objective may certify every six months. All other employees — those split across multiple awards, or whose time is divided between federal and non-federal activities — must certify more frequently. The regulation does not specify "monthly" for the latter group, but the after-the-fact principle is most reliably satisfied with monthly certification. Quarterly creates too much opportunity for recall that drifts from what actually happened.

The practical implication: an employee working 80% on one federal grant and 20% on administrative duties is not a single-award employee. The 20% is a separate cost objective. Monthly certification is required. This misclassification is exactly what auditors test for.

## The Three-Step Monthly Certification Cycle

Reliable time-and-effort compliance runs on a defined monthly cycle. The steps are straightforward; the discipline of executing them without exception is where organizations fail.

**Step 1: Identify and classify every employee with federal grant charges.** Pull a list of every employee whose salary or wages were charged to a federal award during the period. Include employees fully funded by a single award and employees splitting time across awards or between federal and non-federal activities. For each employee, confirm the correct certification frequency: semi-annual only if the employee works 100% on a single federal cost objective, monthly for everyone else.

**Step 2: Distribute certification forms within two weeks of period end.** The longer the gap between the period and the certification request, the less reliable the employee's recollection of time distribution. Distribute within two weeks; set a one-week return deadline. Certifications requested 90 days after the period are not credible after-the-fact records. They are reconstructions from memory, payroll reports, and email review. Auditors can identify the difference in the specificity of the responses and the consistency across employees.

**Step 3: Reconcile certification percentages to posted payroll before filing.** This is the step most organizations skip entirely. Collecting and filing certifications without comparing the stated percentages to what was posted to the general ledger provides no assurance — it is documentation theater. The comparison is the control. If an employee certified 50% on Grant A and payroll was posted at 55%, the posting is incorrect by 5%. A correcting journal entry is required, and the grants manager should be notified because the correction affects budget-versus-actual comparisons.

## What a Compliant Certification Must Contain

The format is flexible. A time sheet, a signed activity report, or a dedicated certification form are all acceptable under 2 CFR 200.430. The content requirements are not flexible.

A compliant certification must: reflect an after-the-fact distribution of the employee's actual activities across cost objectives, expressed as percentages that add to 100%. It must cover a specific period, identified by date. It must be signed by the employee or a supervisory official with direct, firsthand knowledge of the work performed. An unsigned certification is not compliant. A certification without a date is not compliant. A certification that lists only the employee name and grant number without any time distribution is not compliant.

Supervisor sign-off is permitted under 2 CFR 200.430(i), but the supervisor must have direct knowledge of the work — not just awareness of the assignment. A supervisor who managed the work and observed the time allocation can certify. A supervisor who approved the budget but was not present is not in a position to provide firsthand confirmation. Employee self-certification is more defensible and easier to explain to auditors.

## The Most Common Failure and Its Consequences

The single most common time-and-effort failure is applying semi-annual certification to employees who do not qualify. Organizations establish a certification schedule early in the grant period, assign employees to semi-annual or monthly cycles based on initial assessments, and then do not revisit the classification when circumstances change.

An employee who picks up a small assignment on a second federal award midway through the grant year is no longer a single-award employee. The classification must change. The organization should conduct an annual review of semi-annual certifiers — typically at fiscal year-end — to confirm that each person still meets the single-cost-objective criterion.

The consequence of misclassification is not administrative. Personnel costs are consistently among the largest findings categories in federal single audits. GAO's April 2024 analysis (GAO-24-106173) confirmed that time-and-effort documentation deficiencies — inadequate records, missing certifications, misallocated charges — appear across federal programs with regularity.

When an auditor identifies a missing or non-compliant certification for an employee with federal grant charges, the payroll charges for that employee for that period become questioned costs. The organization must demonstrate that the charges were reasonable and allocable to the specific award. If the certification cannot be produced and the allocability cannot be established from other records, the charges may need to be returned to the awarding agency.

## Audit-Ready Filing Structure

For each pay period or month, maintain a complete set of documentation: the list of employees with federal grant charges for the period, completed and signed certifications for each employee covering the correct period, the reconciliation worksheet comparing certification percentages to posted payroll, any correcting journal entries with the supporting certification basis, and notation if any certification was received late.

File by employee and by period. Auditors test time-and-effort compliance in two dimensions: they select a sample of employees and request all certifications for those employees across the audit year, and they select a sample of periods and request all certifications across all employees for those periods. Your filing structure must support both sampling approaches without requiring a search.

An auditor who cannot locate the certification for a sampled employee expands the sample. A larger sample means more potential findings. Organized filing prevents that cascading outcome.

## What to Do This Week

Review the list of employees currently assigned to semi-annual certification. For each one, confirm they work 100% on a single federal award or cost objective with no split to other programs or administrative duties. If any semi-annual certifiers are actually split, reclassify them to monthly certification effective immediately and note the correction in the compliance file.

If the organization is currently filing certifications without reconciling percentages to posted payroll, add the reconciliation as a mandatory step in the month-end close checklist. The comparison takes 15-30 minutes for a typical grant portfolio. The audit risk it eliminates is significant.
