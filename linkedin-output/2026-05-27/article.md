# 21st CCLC Compliance: What After-School Program Leaders Actually Owe the Federal Government

An after-school program in Ohio received a 21st Century Community Learning Centers subaward last fall. The program director had run enrichment programs for years. She understood the academic work. What she did not understand — until the first monitoring visit — was that the federal compliance obligations attached to the subaward were nearly as demanding as the program obligations.

That gap between program competence and compliance knowledge is common in 21st CCLC. The program model is familiar. The federal paperwork behind it is not.

This article is a practical map of what 21st CCLC grantees are actually required to do: the legal authority, the funding structure, the performance reporting system, and the Uniform Guidance overlay that applies to every dollar of federal money in the award.

## Authority and Money Flow

21st Century Community Learning Centers is authorized under Title IV Part B of the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (20 USC 7171 et seq.). That statutory basis matters because it means 21st CCLC is a federal formula grant — not a discretionary program — with a specific allocation methodology and a defined set of permissible activities.

The US Department of Education allocates funds to state educational agencies based on each state's Title I Part A share. The SEA — typically the state department of education — does not run the programs directly. It runs a competitive Request for Application process and subawards to eligible organizations: local educational agencies, community-based organizations, Indian tribes, faith-based organizations, and other public or private entities.

The subawardee operates the program, administers the funds, and reports through the state. The state aggregates and submits to USED. This pass-through structure has compliance implications at every level.

## What Subawardees Are Required to Deliver

21st CCLC programs must provide academic enrichment activities during non-school hours — after school, before school, weekends, and summer. The statute specifies that activities must align to state and local academic standards and serve students attending schools with high concentrations of students from low-income families or schools identified for comprehensive support and improvement.

Permissible activities include tutoring, homework help, STEM enrichment, arts programming, literacy instruction, physical activity, college and career readiness, and family engagement services. Activities are broad — the compliance discipline is in documenting that what the program actually did falls within the approved scope.

Family engagement is required, not optional. Programs must offer services that help families support student learning — literacy programs, parent involvement in academic events, activities that connect the school day to the home. This requirement appears in monitoring checklists and should appear in the program's service documentation.

An independent program evaluation is also required. The SEA sets the evaluation framework and schedule. Most SEAs require external evaluators. Evaluation findings feed program improvement cycles and are referenced in monitoring visits.

## GPRA Performance Measures

The federal Government Performance and Results Act requires 21st CCLC to demonstrate performance against specific measures. For after-school programs, those measures cover three domains:

**Academic achievement:** Increases in the share of regular participants reaching proficiency on state reading and math assessments. "Regular participants" is typically defined by the SEA as students attending the program a specified minimum number of hours. Programs must track participant attendance carefully to accurately identify who qualifies as a regular participant for reporting purposes.

**School behavior:** Improvement in indicators such as classroom behavior and school-day attendance. Programs collect teacher-reported data or use school records. The data collection methodology must be established early in the program year — retrofitting it at reporting time creates inconsistencies that monitoring visits flag.

**School engagement:** Improvement in indicators such as homework completion and classroom participation. Like behavior data, this requires a defined collection process tied to specific data sources that exist independently of the program's own records.

These three domains sound straightforward. The compliance work is in collecting the underlying data systematically throughout the program year, not reconstructing it when the Annual Performance Report window opens.

## The Annual Performance Report and 21APR System

The APR captures program operations data, participant demographics, attendance, and performance against GPRA measures. It is submitted through the 21APR reporting system — the federal data collection tool through which states collect grantee submissions and submit aggregated data to USED.

The SEA sets the submission timeline and requirements. Grantees submit to the SEA; the SEA reviews, aggregates, and submits to USED. The practical implication: the grantee's data must be complete and accurate before the SEA's internal deadline, which is earlier than the federal submission window.

Programs that treat APR completion as a once-a-year documentation event — rather than an ongoing data collection practice — consistently find themselves reconstructing attendance records and outcome data under time pressure. The APR is only as accurate as the program-year records that feed it.

## Program Quality Assessment

21st CCLC requires grantees to use a validated program quality assessment to evaluate point-of-service quality. The Youth Program Quality Assessment from the Weikart Center is the most commonly used tool. States may approve equivalent assessments.

The YPQA is an observational instrument — trained assessors observe program activities and score quality across multiple domains: safe environment, supportive environment, interaction, and engagement. The assessment produces a quality profile that programs use for continuous improvement planning.

Results from the program quality assessment feed into program evaluation and state monitoring. Programs that conduct the assessment perfunctorily — without using the results for actual improvement cycles — will find that monitoring visits ask about the improvement actions that should have followed assessment findings.

## The Uniform Guidance Overlay

Because 21st CCLC funds are federal, 2 CFR Part 200 (Uniform Guidance) applies end-to-end. This is the layer of compliance that surprises most program-side staff who have not worked with federal grants before.

The Uniform Guidance governs allowable costs, procurement standards, equipment management, record retention, and financial reporting. For subawardees, the specific obligations include:

**Allowable costs:** Expenditures charged to the 21st CCLC award must be allowable (permitted under 2 CFR Part 200 Subpart E and the grant agreement), allocable (benefiting the program being funded), and reasonable (consistent with market prices for equivalent goods or services). A program that charges an expense to the grant without confirming all three conditions is creating a potential questioned cost.

**Personnel costs:** Under 2 CFR 200.430, salary charges to federal awards must reflect actual time worked on the program, not budgeted percentages. After-school programs that split staff time between 21st CCLC and other programs need documented time allocation records. Staff who work 100% on 21st CCLC may use semi-annual certification; staff on multiple programs need monthly documentation.

**Procurement:** Goods and services above the micro-purchase threshold ($10,000 as a general rule) require documented competitive procurement. Many after-school programs use vendors for snacks, enrichment activities, or transportation. Procurement documentation must exist and must demonstrate that costs are reasonable.

**Single audit:** The October 2024 Uniform Guidance revision raised the single audit threshold to $1,000,000 in total federal expenditures in a fiscal year. Organizations crossing that threshold across all their federal awards — not just 21st CCLC — owe a single audit under 2 CFR Part 200 Subpart F. The audit covers all major federal programs, not just the one that pushed the organization over the threshold.

**Subrecipient monitoring:** If a 21st CCLC subawardee further subawards to partner organizations (a school operating out of a community-based organization's space, for example), 2 CFR 200.331 and 200.332 subrecipient monitoring requirements apply to the relationship.

## The Three Compliance Failures That Monitoring Visits Find

**Participation data not collected in real time.** GPRA reporting requires attendance and outcome data that must be collected through defined processes tied to school records, teacher reports, or program databases. Programs that intend to collect this data but have not established the collection process early in the program year find themselves unable to accurately populate the APR.

**Personnel cost documentation gaps.** Staff split across 21st CCLC and other activities without documented time allocation records. When monitoring visits request payroll and time records, the absence of contemporaneous time documentation creates a questioned cost — even if staff actually worked the hours they are claiming.

**Family engagement treated as optional.** The statute requires family engagement activities. When monitoring asks for documentation of family engagement programming, programs that treated this requirement informally have documentation gaps. Document every family engagement activity with attendance records, dates, and a brief description of the activity.

## What to Do This Month

If your organization is a 21st CCLC subawardee and has not reviewed compliance posture recently, three immediate actions reduce risk:

Review the APR data collection process for the current program year. Confirm that participation data — attendance, academic assessment data, behavior and engagement indicators — is being collected through a documented process, not informally.

Pull payroll records for any staff splitting time between 21st CCLC and other programs. Confirm that time allocation documentation exists for each pay period since the award year began. If gaps exist, address them before the monitoring visit calendar, not after.

Locate the program quality assessment schedule in the award agreement and confirm the assessment has been conducted or scheduled as required. Confirm that improvement action planning has followed the assessment results.
