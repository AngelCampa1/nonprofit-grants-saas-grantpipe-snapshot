**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Winning a grant is not the end of a process. It is the start of a multi-year compliance obligation.

A 24-month federal grant will typically require: grant agreement execution, an initial budget submission, quarterly financial reports, semi-annual programmatic reports, budget modification approvals, and a final report with full supporting documentation.
Each step has deadlines. Missing a single reporting deadline can trigger compliance findings that affect future funding eligibility.
The most common compliance failure is not fraud — it is fund commingling. Grant dollars tracked only in a spreadsheet, deposited into the general operating account, with no separate accounting code to prove the restricted funds were spent on approved programmatic activities.
When an auditor asks how $50,000 was spent, "we think it went to these things" is not an acceptable answer.
The second most common failure is personnel cost documentation. A time sheet that says "40 hours, program work" does not meet federal documentation standards. Time records must identify the grant or cost objective for each hour logged.
Audit readiness does not begin six months before the audit. It begins on the day of award.

Nonprofits that maintain organized grant files and current reconciliations throughout the grant period pass audits. Nonprofits that reconstruct documentation retroactively find the process much harder.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Every Single Audit finding requires a written Corrective Action Plan under 2 CFR 200.511(c).

The CAP is not optional, and "we will strengthen controls" is not a CAP. The regulation requires five specific elements for each finding: auditee position (agree or disagree), the specific corrective action being taken, the name and title of the responsible party, the completion date, and the finding reference number.
Vague responses invite the awarding agency to escalate — imposing specific conditions, requiring prior approval for draws, or designating the organization as high-risk in future years.
The deadline is strict: the CAP is included in the Single Audit reporting package due to the Federal Audit Clearinghouse at the earlier of 30 days after the auditor's report or nine months after fiscal year-end, per 2 CFR 200.512(a)(1).
One specific point that organizations frequently miss: corrective action is not finished when the CAP is filed. It is finished when the action is implemented and documented with dated evidence — signed policy, training attendance, reconciled records. Auditors test CAP completion in the following year's audit as prior-year follow-up.
Repeat findings in two consecutive audits trigger specific conditions under 2 CFR 200.208 — prior approval requirements, enhanced monitoring, or high-risk designation that persists until the pattern is broken.

A strong CAP reads like a project plan: specific actions, named owners, firm dates. That same document works for internal accountability.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Voluntary committed cost share is the cost share trap most nonprofits do not see until they are in an audit.

Under 2 CFR 200.306, cost share is the non-federal portion of total allowable project cost. It can be mandatory — required by the NOFO — or voluntary and committed, meaning the organization offered additional match in the application to improve its competitive score.
Here is the part that catches organizations off guard: the moment that voluntary cost share is written into a federal award agreement, it becomes legally binding. A nonprofit that pledges $50,000 in volunteer hours to strengthen a proposal must document and achieve that $50,000 in contributions during implementation. A shortfall is an audit finding with the same consequences as a mandatory match failure.
The documentation requirements for voluntary match are identical to mandatory match: verifiable from grantee records, necessary and reasonable for the project, allowable under applicable cost principles, and not sourced from another federal award.
In-kind match — volunteer labor, donated goods, facility use — is not a softer requirement than cash match. Auditors test in-kind the same way they test direct costs: they look for contemporaneous documentation, fair market valuations, and consistency with the approved budget.
The risk is concentrated in organizations that over-propose match to be competitive and then run short of documentation during implementation.

Federal pass-through funds cannot serve as match — even when the federal origin of the state dollars is not obvious in the funding flow.

**Post 5 — TOOL_TEARDOWN**

Qgiv was acquired by Bloomerang in 2024. If you are evaluating Qgiv today, you are evaluating Bloomerang's product direction, not an independent platform.

Qgiv built a real position in nonprofit fundraising: peer-to-peer campaign pages, event ticketing, auction management, and embedded donation forms at accessible price points. It served organizations that needed fundraising form infrastructure without a full CRM.
What Qgiv was not, and was never designed to be, is a donor CRM or a grant management system. Contact records in Qgiv were functional enough to track donors across transactions, but the platform had no relationship depth — no grant tracking, no restricted fund allocation, no compliance reporting.
The acquisition does not change Qgiv's scope. It changes who sets the roadmap. Features requested by existing Qgiv customers now compete with Bloomerang's own development priorities.
The practical question for a nonprofit evaluating its software stack is: what problem are you actually trying to solve? If the problem is fundraising form infrastructure and peer-to-peer events, Qgiv still covers that. If the problem is managing donor relationships and grant compliance in one place, the tool category is different.
Organizations using a fundraising-form-only platform alongside a separate CRM manage two sets of donor records in over 60% of cases.

Treating platform selection as a category question — not a feature comparison — usually produces a better outcome.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

Mental health nonprofits managing SAMHSA grants and CCBHC program funding face a compliance problem that most grant software does not model: the same service cost cannot be claimed to both federal grant funding and Medicaid billing simultaneously.

SAMHSA's FY2023 budget was approximately $7.5 billion, with the majority flowing through block grants to states and then to local mental health and substance use disorder service providers. The organizations receiving those funds are typically running MHBG, SABG, and CCBHC funding streams alongside Medicaid reimbursement for the same clinical services.
When the same staff member serves both Medicaid-eligible clients and clients covered under grant funding, time records must allocate labor costs to the correct funding source for every hour worked. A time sheet that says "program work" is not sufficient. Neither is a general allocation percentage applied at month-end without contemporaneous records.
Cost duplication between Medicaid billing and federal grants is a primary OIG audit focus in behavioral health organizations. The finding is often unintentional — it results from accounting systems that cannot track costs by payer at the service delivery level.
CCBHC programs add another layer: the prospective payment system rate is recalibrated based on actual cost reports by service type, so the documentation requirement is more detailed than standard grant financial reporting.
Organizations providing substance use disorder services also carry 42 CFR Part 2 confidentiality obligations that restrict what patient-level data can be shared in grant reports — requiring aggregate metrics that satisfy funder requirements without violating patient consent rules.

Multi-funder behavioral health compliance is not a spreadsheet problem. The required granularity of cost tracking by payer, service type, and funding source exceeds what manual processes can reliably sustain.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

Release-from-restriction is one of the most frequently mishandled events in nonprofit accounting — and the error is almost always about timing, not intent.

Under FASB ASC 958, a purpose restriction is released when the organization incurs qualifying expenditures for the specified use — not at year-end, not when the grant closes, not when the auditor asks. The release amount equals the qualifying expenses actually incurred in the period.
A common error: organizations hold the full restricted fund balance until year-end and release it in a single journal entry. If expenses were incurred throughout the year, the releases should have happened throughout the year. Recording them all in December creates a presentation that does not match when conditions were actually satisfied.
For auditors reviewing restricted fund management, the question is not whether the total release amount is correct. It is whether each release was recorded in the correct period, supported by qualifying expenditures, and documented with a journal entry that references the supporting GL detail.
The three release types under ASC 958 require different triggers: purpose restrictions release when qualifying expenses are incurred; time restrictions release when the required date or event occurs; perpetual restrictions on principal never release, only investment income may be released according to the organization's spending policy.
Endowment income release is a separate calculation from operating grant releases — they should not share the same journal entry or documentation file.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five things executive directors need from grant tracking software that development directors do not.

First: oversight-level compliance dashboards. The ED is accountable for whether restricted funds are being spent correctly, not just whether reports are submitted on time. A tool that only shows deadline status does not give the ED what they need to answer a board question about audit risk.
Second: audit trail at the transaction level. Government grant compliance requires a record of who approved expenditures and when. Spreadsheets do not log edits by default. This requirement eliminates spreadsheets from consideration for any federally-funded organization — not because of preference, but because the documentation standard cannot be met.
Third: restricted fund separation with expenditure monitoring. The ED needs to know, at any point, whether restricted dollars are being drawn down against approved budget categories. A grant balance number without expenditure category detail is not actionable oversight.
Fourth: budget-to-actual reporting by grant line item. Most donor CRMs report at the grant level. Federal compliance requires reporting at the budget category level — personnel, direct costs, indirect costs — against the approved budget for each award.
Fifth: clear handoff between development, finance, and executive reporting. The most expensive operational problem in this category is not the cost of the software. It is the time spent rebuilding the same funding picture for three different audiences every reporting cycle.

Only 33.3% of nonprofits rate their CRM as effective, according to the 2025 Nonprofit Peer Report from Fifty & Fifty. Reporting capability is the most cited gap.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

Michigan nonprofits soliciting contributions need two separate registrations current to stay compliant — and the agencies involved do not communicate with each other.

The Michigan Attorney General's Charitable Trust Section issues and maintains charitable solicitations licenses under MCL 400.271 et seq. The Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs (LARA) handles nonprofit corporate existence and processes the corporate annual report due every May 15.
An organization can have a fully current AG solicitation license while LARA has administratively dissolved the corporation. This combination is invisible in day-to-day operations and surfaces exactly when it causes the most damage: when a grant funder requests a Certificate of Good Standing.
The AG annual report is due within 7 months of fiscal year end. For December 31 filers, that is July 31. The late fee is $25 per day up to a maximum of $1,000 — a meaningful financial exposure for organizations that miss the deadline by even a few weeks.
Michigan's audit threshold for the AG annual report is $500,000 in gross revenue — separate from the federal Single Audit threshold. Organizations at $500,000 in gross revenue must attach CPA-audited financial statements, even if their federal expenditures fall below the federal audit threshold.
The fee schedule for the AG license ranges from $0 for organizations under $25,000 in revenue to $200 for organizations above $5 million.
To stay current: verify AG license status through the Charitable Trust Section's search tool, verify LARA corporate status separately, calculate the 7-month AG deadline from your fiscal year end, and calendar the May 15 LARA deadline as a fixed annual item.
