# Cash vs. Accrual Accounting for Nonprofit Grants: What Your Books Are Actually Telling the Auditors

A nonprofit runs its books on a cash basis. The development director submits an SF-425 Federal Financial Report each quarter. The numbers look reasonable. The auditors arrive for the single audit and ask for the reconciliation worksheet that ties the cash-basis ledger to the accrued expenditure figures on the FFR.

There is no worksheet.

This scenario plays out at mid-sized nonprofits with federal awards more often than the sector talks about publicly. The gap between how an organization manages its internal books and what federal grant reporting actually requires is one of the more reliable predictors of single audit findings. And it is almost entirely avoidable.

## The Requirement That Does Not Negotiate

Federal grant reporting is accrual-basis. This is not a preference or a recommendation. It is a requirement under 2 CFR 200.302(b)(3), which mandates that non-federal entity financial management systems permit the tracing of funds to a level of expenditure adequate to establish that funds have not been used in violation of the restrictions and conditions of the federal award.

Cash-basis records cannot meet that standard when there are unpaid vendor invoices, earned-but-unpaid wages, or incurred-but-unreimbursed costs sitting at any period end.

The SF-425 Federal Financial Report makes this concrete. Block 10e asks for total federal share of expenditures — which includes both cash disbursements and accrued expenditures. An organization that submits only its cash disbursements in Block 10e is underreporting federal expenditures, even if it does not realize it.

This matters beyond the quarterly paperwork. Auditors test the FFR against the general ledger. If cumulative FFR expenditures consistently match cash disbursements with no accruals ever appearing, that pattern is an audit signal — not because accruals must exist in every period, but because the absence of any accruals across multiple periods is statistically unlikely for most organizations.

## The GAAP Layer

FASB ASC 958, Not-for-Profit Entities, adds a second layer. All audited nonprofit financial statements must be prepared on an accrual basis. This means:

Net assets are classified into two categories: with and without donor restrictions. Revenue is recognized when earned, not when cash arrives. Expenses are recognized when incurred, not when paid.

An organization that maintains internal cash-basis books and produces audited financials is running two parallel records: the working cash books and the accrual conversion the CPA prepares at year-end. This dual-maintenance model is common and functional. The risk is that the accrual conversion happens once a year, is never tested during the year, and produces a version of the financials that may not reconcile cleanly to the quarterly FFRs submitted throughout the year.

The Form 990 adds a third layer. IRS instructions require accrual-basis financial data for 990 preparation. Organizations using cash-basis books must reconcile to accrual for 990 filing — another conversion step where errors accumulate if the process is not documented.

## The Grant Revenue Recognition Framework

Understanding when to recognize grant revenue is a separate question from understanding the accounting basis. Under FASB ASC 958-605, recognition depends on the nature of the grant.

**Unconditional, purpose-restricted grants** are recognized as restricted revenue when the grant agreement is executed. The cash may not arrive until months later — a grant payable by quarterly reimbursement may not result in a cash receipt for 90 days. Under accrual basis, the revenue accrues at the point of an enforceable right. The organization has an asset (grant receivable) and revenue (restricted contribution) before any money moves.

**Conditional grants** are recognized as revenue when conditions are substantially met. A grant advance received before conditions are overcome is deferred revenue — a liability, not income. The cash was received; the revenue has not been earned. When qualifying costs are incurred, the deferred revenue is released to grant revenue on the statement of activities.

**Government grants structured as exchange transactions** may follow ASC 606 rather than ASC 958-605, depending on whether the relationship is a contribution or a fee for services. This distinction affects timing and classification. Getting it wrong produces either revenue overstatement (recording a fee-for-service contract as a contribution) or understatement (deferring revenue that should be recognized at contract execution).

The grant agreement's terms govern the analysis — not the organization's preference or prior practice.

## The Cash-to-Accrual Conversion: Four Required Adjustments

An organization that maintains cash-basis books and must report on an accrual basis for each FFR needs to perform the following adjustments at every reporting date. This is not optional or theoretical. It is what federal auditors will test.

**Adjustment 1: Accounts payable and accrued liabilities.** Identify all vendor invoices received and approved but not yet paid. These are accrued expenditures under accrual basis. Add these to the expenditure figure for the reporting period.

**Adjustment 2: Accrued payroll.** If a pay period crosses the FFR reporting date, the earned but unpaid wages belong in accrued expenditures. Calculate the proration. For semi-monthly payroll on a period that ends on the 15th and 30th, a quarterly FFR due on the last day of the month will typically have a half-payroll accrual to include.

**Adjustment 3: Grant receivables.** Costs incurred and reportable to the funder but not yet submitted for reimbursement are grant receivables — an asset under accrual basis. These costs are expenditures in the current period even if the reimbursement request has not been submitted. Organizations on a cost-reimbursement award that draw funds monthly may have two weeks of unsubmitted costs at any reporting date.

**Adjustment 4: Deferred revenue.** Cash advances received for conditional grants should not be included in accrual-basis revenue until conditions are met. If a grant advance was received in the prior quarter for a conditional award where conditions have not been substantially met, that advance is still deferred revenue.

Each adjustment must be documented in a reconciliation worksheet that ties the cash-basis ledger total to the accrual-basis figure used in the FFR. The worksheet is audit evidence. Its absence is a finding.

## The Most Common Error in Practice

Organizations that manage their books in QuickBooks — or any general ledger configured to cash basis — often discover the gap during a single audit rather than during their normal reporting process.

QuickBooks set to cash basis does not capture accounts payable accruals or grant receivables correctly. Reports run from a cash-basis QuickBooks file show only cash disbursements. If those figures go onto an SF-425 without adjustment, the FFR is understated.

The fix is straightforward in principle: configure QuickBooks to accrual basis. This enables accounts payable tracking, accounts receivable tracking, and the ability to run both cash-basis and accrual-basis reports for comparison. An organization with federal awards that is running QuickBooks on cash basis is creating unnecessary reconciliation risk every reporting cycle.

Organizations that have been running cash-basis books for years often resist the configuration change because the existing reports are familiar and the prior-year financials were prepared on cash basis. The transition is manageable. It requires reconciling the opening balances at the transition date, training the bookkeeping staff on the accrual entries, and building the documentation habit before the next FFR is due.

## The Single Audit Connection

Discrepancies between cash-basis books and accrual-basis FFR submissions are one of the more reliable indicators of accounting control weaknesses in single audits. Not because auditors are looking for procedural failures — they are looking for evidence that the organization's financial management system can actually demonstrate that federal funds were spent in accordance with the award terms.

A clean reconciliation worksheet, produced at each reporting date, documented with source records, and retained in the grant file, is the evidence that the accrual-basis FFR was derived properly. It is also what makes the conversation with auditors short and uneventful rather than extended and documented in a finding.

The organizations that find themselves in single audit findings related to accounting basis are almost never committing intentional misrepresentation. They are organizations that did not build the conversion documentation habit, assumed the cash figures were close enough, or did not know the accrual requirement existed until an auditor asked about it.

## What to Do This Week

If your organization has federal awards and uses cash-basis books, the action items are clear.

First, identify the accounting basis in your general ledger and confirm whether your QuickBooks or equivalent system is configured to cash or accrual. Second, pull the most recent FFR submitted and compare the federal expenditures figure to the cash disbursements in your general ledger for the same period. If they match exactly — with no accruals ever appearing in Block 10e — you have a gap to close. Third, build the reconciliation worksheet for the current reporting period, even if a submission is not imminent. Document the four adjustments and retain the worksheet with the grant file.

If the reconciliation reveals a discrepancy with prior FFR submissions, discuss the scope of the issue with your auditor before self-reporting. Errors in prior FFR submissions are correctable through amended filings; the process is more manageable when it is documented and disclosed proactively.
