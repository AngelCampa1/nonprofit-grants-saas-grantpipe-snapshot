**Post 1 — STAT_BOMB**

The median nonprofit spends 6–8 hours per grant per year on reporting — 30+ cumulative hours over a grant's lifetime.

That is not time spent on program work. That is time spent assembling budget-vs-actual tables, hunting for expenditure documentation, and formatting numbers a funder already has in their system.

Most of the labor comes from two sources: the financial reconciliation by line item, and the narrative that ties outcomes to the targets written into the original application.

A federal grant report needs both. So does a foundation progress report. The format differs — SF-425 for federal awards, PDF or web form for most foundations — but the core content is identical.

Budget vs. actual by category. Actual participant counts versus committed targets. Explanation of any variance above 10%.

If those three elements are current and organized throughout the grant period, the 6–8 hours compresses significantly. If they are not, the report becomes the reconciliation.

**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

The myth: three donor segments is enough — everyone, major donors, lapsed.

The problem: three segments cannot drive differentiated action.

AFP Fundraising Effectiveness Project data shows first-year donor retention averaging 19–22%. Donors retained for three or more years retain at 60–65%. Those two populations need entirely different outreach. One list treats them the same.

Organizations with retention programs that actually work use fifteen or more saved segments, each connected to a specific workflow: LYBUNT re-activation, first-year second-gift cultivation, mid-level upgrade asks, lapsed sustainer re-engagement.

The cost difference matters here. Acquiring a new donor runs $50–$100. Re-activating a lapsed donor costs $15–$30. Segmentation that surfaces the right LYBUNT population before each appeal is among the highest-ROI activities in the development calendar.

A saved segment is not a static list. It is a live query that updates as records change. When a lapsed donor gives again, they leave the re-activation segment automatically. No manual refresh, no spreadsheet reconciliation.

The difference between three segments and fifteen is not complexity. It is whether the development team has actionable lists or a single undifferentiated pile.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

A release from restriction is not a revenue event. Under FASB ASC 958-205, it is a reclassification.

Debit net assets with donor restrictions. Credit net assets without donor restrictions. The total net asset number does not change. The columns shift.

That distinction matters because it determines when the entry is dated — and dating it incorrectly is one of the most common year-end close errors in nonprofit accounting.

The release should be dated at the moment the restriction was satisfied. For purpose restrictions, that is the date the last qualifying expenditure was incurred. For time restrictions, it is the date the time condition expired. Not the date the accountant posted the entry. Not December 31 by default.

A year-end procedure that batches all releases at fiscal close — regardless of when expenditures occurred — misrepresents when restricted funds became available. Auditors testing restricted net assets will trace back to expenditure evidence. If the entry date does not align with the documented fulfillment date, it is a finding.

The practical fix: run purpose-restriction releases monthly as program expenses post. By year-end, the calculation is already done. The close becomes a verification exercise, not a three-day reconciliation.

FASB ASU 2016-14 requires footnote disclosure of the nature, amounts, and expected timing of remaining restrictions.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Grant spend-down is the phrase that makes program directors nervous toward the end of a grant year.

The pressure is real. The framing matters.

"Spend the money before December 31" is a dangerous way to think about it. The correct framing is: incur allowable, documented expenses on approved activities before December 31. Those are different standards with different compliance consequences.

Under 2 CFR 200.309, all costs charged to a federal award must be incurred within the approved budget period. A grant that reaches zero balance through rushed, inadequately documented spending has a zero balance and a compliance problem. Questioned costs — expenses that fail the allowable, allocable, or reasonable tests — can require repayment even if the money was technically spent.

Federal grant closeout data shows approximately 15% of federal awards to nonprofits result in some funds returned at closeout, with the average returned balance representing 4–8% of the award.

Spend-down problems are best identified four to six months before the award end date. With that lead time, organizations can re-program budget, add allowable activities, or request a no-cost extension — all of which produce real program work with real documentation.

With four weeks of lead time, the only option is a spending rush that creates more risk than it resolves.

**Post 6 — HOW_TO**

Your board sees total gifts received. That number is not actionable.

It tells them what came in. It does not tell them whether the donor base is healthy or declining. Two organizations can show the same revenue total — one retained 70% of donors, the other retained 35% and replaced churn with expensive new acquisition. The revenue line hides that difference.

The four metrics that produce a useful board conversation:

Overall retention rate with year-over-year trend. Retention rate by giving segment — major ($1,000+), mid-level ($250–$999), general. LYBUNT count with estimated recapture revenue in dollars. Average gift size trend over four or more quarters.

Major donor retention should sit at 80% or higher. If it falls below that, the board needs to see it — major donors typically represent a disproportionate share of total revenue and falling retention is not a quarterly metric problem, it is a relationship problem.

Translate the retention rate into dollars before the board meeting: 260 lapsed donors at an average prior gift of $185 equals $48,100 in revenue that must be replaced through acquisition or recapture. That is a board-level decision, not a staff-level observation.

Report this quarterly. Annual retention reporting arrives too late to adjust strategy in the same fiscal year.

That gap is the most important number in the comparison, and it rarely appears in the demos.

Salesforce can be configured to track grants and related compliance, but that means scoping, building, and maintaining custom objects, fields, workflows, and reporting — by consultants, at $150–$250 per hour, with ongoing admin costs of $10,000–$25,000 per year.

The practical filter for the comparison: which product gives your team a trustworthy report faster with less manual reconstruction after the demo ends?

For organizations whose primary question is donor engagement, Bloomerang is usually the more practical choice. For organizations that also manage restricted grants and need finance-grade compliance, neither product gives you the answer without additional work.

The buying mistake that repeats: comparing both tools only at the CRM layer and finding out after implementation what the grant compliance workflow actually costs.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

Every cost charged to a federal grant must pass three tests simultaneously under 2 CFR Part 200.

Allowability — the cost is not prohibited by the terms of the award, by 2 CFR 200.420–200.475, or by any other applicable regulation. Explicit unallowable categories include alcoholic beverages (200.423), entertainment (200.438), fundraising costs (200.442), and lobbying (200.450). No exceptions on these.

Allocability — the grant actually benefited from the cost. A staff member who works 40% of their time on a federal grant can allocate 40% of their salary to that grant. A supply purchase used entirely on a different program cannot be allocated to the grant regardless of how it is coded.

Reasonableness — a prudent person would pay this amount for this good or service in the ordinary course of business. Costs established through competitive procurement are easier to defend. Costs from related parties without arm's-length pricing, or costs that significantly exceed market rates, may fail this test even if they are otherwise allowable.

A cost that passes two tests but fails one is disallowable and must be removed from the grant ledger — typically absorbed from unrestricted funds or repaid to the federal agency.

The documentation principle: if you had to explain the cost to a skeptical auditor who has never heard of your organization, write that explanation at the time of the transaction. Documentation assembled retroactively carries far less weight.
