**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: a nonprofit on cash-basis books can report to federal agencies the same way it reports internally.

Reality: federal grant reporting is accrual-only, under 2 CFR 200.302(b)(3).

The SF-425 Federal Financial Report is the clearest proof. Block 10e captures accrued expenditures — costs incurred but not yet paid. Block 10g captures unliquidated obligations. A cash-basis organization that enters only cash disbursements on Block 10e is underreporting federal expenditures, even if the books look correct internally.

Under FASB ASC 958, audited nonprofit financial statements must also be on accrual. Form 990 preparation follows the same standard.

The practical fix for a cash-basis organization: at each reporting date, identify unpaid vendor invoices, calculate any pay-period proration for accrued wages, isolate grant receivables not yet submitted for reimbursement, and document the cash-to-accrual reconciliation in a worksheet retained with the FFR.

Missing reconciliation worksheets are one of the more reliable indicators of accounting control weaknesses in single audits.

Cash books and federal reporting can coexist. The bridge is documented, repeatable, and auditable.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

A post-closeout cost transfer on a federal grant requires written authorization from the awarding agency before any journal entry is booked.

That is the step most organizations get wrong: they discover the misposting, assess it, and move the cost — then ask permission. That sequence creates a second compliance problem on top of the original one.

Under 2 CFR 200.344, grantees have 120 calendar days after the period of performance ends to liquidate obligations and submit final reports. A transfer discovered after that window closes is in different territory.

The correct sequence: document the specific cost and why it needs to transfer, confirm whether the 120-day window is still open, notify the federal program officer and grants management specialist in writing, submit a formal prior approval request package with original expenditure documentation, wait for written authorization (typically 30 to 90 days depending on the agency), then book the entry.

If the transfer changes figures on the final SF-425, an amended FFR must be submitted.

Program officers are helpful. Verbal concurrence from a program officer is not written authorization from a grants management specialist. The distinction matters.

Strong monthly reconciliation is the real answer: it catches mispostings while awards are still open.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

Post-award grant management is not the glamorous half of the grants function. Pre-award — prospecting, writing, submitting — has a clear narrative arc. Post-award is the operational discipline that runs quietly in the background for months or years.

It is also where most compliance failures happen.

Proposals are reviewed before any money moves. The funder evaluates the budget and work plan before making the award. Post-award management happens without that external review layer. Expenditures are approved internally. Documentation is maintained — or not — by staff with many other priorities.

The four phases are setup (entering the award, establishing restricted fund tracking, calendaring all obligations), active management (expenditure approvals, time records, drawdown reconciliation, interim reports), reporting (producing funder-required reports from accumulated data), and closeout (stopping expenditures, reconciling all transactions, submitting final reports, archiving the file).

Setup quality in the first two weeks of an award predicts compliance quality for the entire grant period.

Organizations with strong post-award systems have documented workflows that connect development, program, and finance staff. The typical audit finding is not a fraudulent application. It is a documentation gap that accumulated gradually — time records not collected, budget modifications without required prior approval.

What does your organization's post-award setup process actually look like in week one?

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

After-school nonprofits managing 21st Century Community Learning Centers grants face a quarterly reporting requirement that no standard donor CRM or general-purpose accounting system was built to handle.

The 21st CCLC program distributed approximately $1.3 billion in federal grants in fiscal year 2023, serving more than 1.7 million students at 6,600 centers operated primarily by nonprofits.

Quarterly attendance reporting requires documentation by school, grade level, and program type — data that lives in a student enrollment database, not a financial system. Annual academic outcome reporting requires teacher survey data, grade progression rates, and test score linkage coordinated with partner schools.

Title I pass-through funds add a second compliance layer. The funding chain runs from the U.S. Department of Education to the state to the local school district to the nonprofit. Each tier adds conditions: allowable use restrictions, district review and approval requirements, reimbursement billing rather than advance payments, and state monitoring visits.

Then there are split grant cycles. School-year programs often run on an academic calendar while summer programs run under a calendar-year or state fiscal-year grant. When those cycles do not align with the organization's fiscal year, every funder report requires manually reconstructing expenditures for a period that crosses fiscal year boundaries.

These are structural compliance challenges, not administrative problems a spreadsheet solves.

**Post 8 — COMPARISON_INSIGHT**

"Grant management software" describes two different products solving opposite problems, and buyers confuse them constantly.

Submittable is an applicant intake and review platform built for organizations that receive applications — foundations, corporate giving programs, scholarship administrators, regranting intermediaries. The product strength is form building, reviewer assignment, configurable scoring, and application pipeline management.

Most $500K-$10M nonprofits do not receive applications. They apply for grants. They are applicant-side, not grantmaker-side.

An applicant-side development director's problem is not intake workflow. It is: what is the restricted balance on grant #4, which SF-425 is due next quarter, what did the organization report to this funder last year, and how does this grant portfolio map against the donor relationships the major gift officer is managing.

Submittable pricing is quote-based and annually contracted — typically $5,000-$20,000 or more based on nonprofit procurement disclosures. It is a reasonable spend for a grantmaker running open calls. For a grant-receiving nonprofit, the product does not address the operating problem.

The October 2024 Uniform Guidance revision raised the single audit threshold to $1,000,000. That is more organizations managing compliance-intensive federal awards without necessarily knowing which software category addresses post-award obligations.

Buying the wrong category of tool is an expensive mistake that takes months to recognize.

**Post 9 — LIST_POST**

Five things to confirm before committing to a grant management platform — from a development director's daily workflow, not a feature checklist.

One: does it track restricted fund balances by budget category, not just total award amount? Knowing you have $47,000 remaining in a $200,000 award matters less than knowing how that balance breaks down across personnel, direct costs, and indirect.

Two: does it alert before reporting deadlines, not after? Calendaring SF-425 submissions and programmatic reports at award setup should be the system's job, not yours.

Three: does report generation pull from live transaction data or require manual formatting? If the funder-ready report requires an export, a spreadsheet step, and reformatting, the system is producing raw material, not a report.

Four: does it connect to your donor records, or does grant management require a separate system? Development directors managing both donors and grants who maintain parallel systems rebuild the same organizational story in two places every month.

Five: does it give finance the same visibility it gives development? Grant compliance is a shared function. A tool only the development team can use leaves finance reconciling from exports.

Only 33.3% of nonprofits rate their CRM systems as effective, per the Fifty & Fifty 2025 Nonprofit Peer Report. The gap between effective and merely present is usually one of the five issues above.

**Post 10 — PRODUCT_INSIGHT**

DAF giving now accounts for approximately 22% of all individual giving in the US, with Fidelity Charitable, Schwab Charitable, and Vanguard Charitable together distributing over $25 billion annually.

For most development teams, those gifts arrive with a note referencing the recommending donor's name — and then get recorded to the DAF sponsor. The recommending donor disappears from the CRM. Their giving history shows nothing. LYBUNT analysis excludes them. Stewardship stops.

Organizations that systematically steward DAF donors as individual donors report 30-40% higher DAF renewal rates in subsequent years, according to DAF Research Collaborative data from 2023.

Soft credit tracking is the mechanism that prevents that gap. The hard credit — the legally recognized donor of record, the entity whose gift appears in Form 990 Line 1 revenue — stays with the DAF sponsor. A soft credit links the attributed donor (the recommending donor) to the gift in their giving history without transferring revenue recognition or creating a duplicate acknowledgment.

The same logic applies to corporate matching gifts. Double the Donation estimates $4-7 billion in matching gift funds go unclaimed annually, partly because attribution tracking is inadequate and employees are never stewarded for their employer matches.
