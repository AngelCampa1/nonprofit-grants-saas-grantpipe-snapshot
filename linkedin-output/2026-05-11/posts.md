**Post 2 — MYTH_BUSTER**

Myth: if a CRM has a "grants" tab, it handles grant management.

Almost every nonprofit CRM uses that phrase. Almost none of them mean the same thing by it.

At the shallow end, "grant management" means a record type labeled grant — enough to store the funder name, the award amount, and a due date. That is useful for keeping notes, not for managing compliance.

What restricted fund management actually requires is different: tracking expenditures from award through closeout, generating compliance reports tied to specific grants, modeling program vs. administrative cost allocations, and producing output your funders will accept.

The 2025 Omatic Nonprofit Integration Report found that nearly half of nonprofits are considering switching CRMs in the next 12 months, up from just 10% the year prior. Feature gaps — not cost — are the primary driver.

Before evaluating any CRM on grant capability, run three scenarios in the trial: enter a grant award, record a restricted expenditure, generate a funder compliance report. If the vendor will not allow this during the sales process, that is diagnostic information.

A demo filtered through a sales rep tells you what the platform can do at its best. Scenario testing with your actual data tells you what it will do every month.

**Post 3 — WORKFLOW_STEP**

Under 2 CFR 200.430, posting payroll at the budgeted percentage and reconciling annually creates a full year of questioned costs if actual time differed from the budget.

This is the single most common payroll compliance error in federal grant audits. The rule is the after-the-fact confirmation principle: payroll charges to federal awards must reflect actual hours worked, not what the grant budget assumed.

Here is the step most organizations skip: reconciling allocation percentages to time-and-effort documentation at month-end, not at audit time.

An employee whose time sheet shows 55% on Grant A but whose payroll was posted at 60% requires a correcting journal entry — dated in the period the time occurred, supported by the certified time record.

Monthly reconciliation is not optional. Quarterly reconciliation is common but insufficient — it allows misallocations to compound over three months before correction. By the time an auditor samples your pay periods, you want those corrections already in the record.

Two regulation references to keep on file: 2 CFR 200.430 governs the after-the-fact requirement. 2 CFR 200.430(i) permits semi-annual certification only for employees working 100% on a single federal award — not employees split across multiple grants.

The 2024 Uniform Guidance revision also raised the de minimis indirect cost rate from 10% to 15% of Modified Total Direct Costs under 2 CFR 200.414(f), for organizations that have never negotiated a rate.

**Post 4 — TERM_EXPLAINED**

A subaward is not a contract. That distinction determines whether your organization has subrecipient monitoring obligations that go far beyond vendor management.

The definition comes from 2 CFR 200.1: a subaward is an award provided by a pass-through entity to a subrecipient for the subrecipient to carry out part of a federal award. The key phrase is "carry out part of a federal program" — not provide goods or services for the pass-through entity's own use.

The 2 CFR 200.331 five-factor test is what auditors apply to determine whether a payment is a subaward or a procurement contract. A subaward exists when the receiving entity determines who is eligible for federal assistance, has performance measured against federal program objectives, makes programmatic decisions, and is responsible for federal compliance.

When those factors are present, the pass-through entity's obligations under 2 CFR 200.332 are triggered: pre-award risk assessment, a written subaward document with required provisions including the FAIN and CFDA number, ongoing monitoring, single-audit verification, and finding resolution.

Characterizing a subaward as a procurement contract to avoid monitoring obligations is a common audit finding — and it generates two findings, not one: a missing written agreement and inadequate monitoring.

The subaward document must exist in writing. A verbal arrangement does not satisfy 2 CFR 200.332(a). The documentation failure is itself a finding, independent of whether the program operated well.

**Post 6 — VERTICAL_HOOK**

Grant-funded nonprofits answer to multiple audiences at the same time: funders want budget and outcome reports, boards want restricted vs. available resource visibility, and auditors need transaction-level support.

The hard part is not generating one report. It is keeping every version of the funding story consistent when each audience works from a different system.

Federal awards raise the stakes further. The Single Audit threshold increased to $1 million in federal expenditures effective October 1, 2024. Under the 2024 Uniform Guidance revision, nonprofits above that threshold face routine audit scrutiny — not occasional. And the GAO found that $1.17 trillion (16.7%) of federal awards were linked to severe audit findings over the 2017-2021 period.

The operational pressure in this segment is not about finding more features. It is about reducing coordination failure. Development tracks the funder relationship in one system. Finance tracks revenue and expenses in another. Program leads keep narrative deliverables in email and shared drives. By the time a board packet is due, staff are rebuilding the same story from scratch.

The software requirement is one system that keeps donors, grants, restricted funds, and reporting deadlines aligned before the month-end or audit pressure arrives — not one that creates more exports to manage.

Restricted fund tracking is the baseline. Every active grant behaves like its own operating constraint. Teams need to see what was awarded, what has been spent, what remains, and what reporting work is next.

**Post 7 — FREE_RESOURCE**

SEFA errors that appear at the start of a single audit — missing awards, misclassified cluster programs, period-of-performance mismatches — are almost always preventable.

They happen because organizations reconstruct the Schedule of Expenditures of Federal Awards during fieldwork instead of maintaining the data throughout the year.

The SEFA must list, per 2 CFR 200.510(b): the federal agency name, the Assistance Listing (CFDA) number, the FAIN, total federal expenditures for the fiscal year, and the aggregate amount passed through to subrecipients. One row per award — not per program.

Five errors account for most SEFA deficiencies: a missing award not tracked in the grants register, a wrong CFDA number copied from the application rather than the notice of award, a period mismatch where fiscal year totals include prior-year costs, a cluster misidentification using last year's OMB Compliance Supplement, and a pass-through amount omitted because subrecipient payments were not tagged to the federal award at origination.

**Post 10 — HOW_TO**

California nonprofits registered with the Attorney General's Registry of Charities and Fundraisers file Form RRF-1 every year. For calendar-year filers, the deadline is May 15 — four months and fifteen days after fiscal year end.

Here is how to complete the filing without getting a deficiency notice.

Step 1: Confirm Registry status. Use the Registry Search tool to verify current standing and your CT file number before doing anything else. A lapsed or suspended registration changes the filing path.

Step 2: Calculate gross annual revenue accurately. This figure determines your fee tier and whether the $2 million audit threshold applies. Fees range from $0 for organizations under $25,000 to $1,200 for those above $500 million. The most common fee mistake is using the wrong revenue tier.

Step 3: Engage an auditor now if at or above $2 million. The Nonprofit Integrity Act of 2004 (Government Code Section 12586(e)) requires an audited financial statement prepared by an independent CPA. The audit must be complete before you file. For calendar-year filers already at this point in April, the window is short.

Step 4: Prepare RRF-1 and attach Form 990. The Registry requires the complete federal Form 990, 990-EZ, or 990-PF. Organizations filing the 990-N postcard must still provide California-level detail on the RRF-1 itself.

Step 5: Submit with the correct fee. File online through the Registry portal — online filing reduces deficiency notices caused by attachment errors and missing signatures. Mail submissions with a check payable to "Department of Justice."

Note: California's $2 million audit threshold is separate from the federal Single Audit threshold of $1 million in federal expenditures under 2 CFR 200.501. If your organization sits above both, you may face parallel audit obligations — often handled in a single engagement by the same CPA firm.
