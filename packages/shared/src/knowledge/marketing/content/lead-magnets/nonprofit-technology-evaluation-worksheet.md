## Why Most Nonprofit Software Decisions Are Bad

The pattern repeats across mid-sized nonprofits: a decade-old donor database is creaking, a board member recommends Salesforce, a consultant proposes a $40,000 implementation, the team signs without a structured evaluation, and 18 months later the system is half-implemented, partially adopted, and twice the projected cost. The fault is rarely the vendor. It is the evaluation process - or the absence of one.

Mid-sized nonprofits face a structural disadvantage in technology purchases. Vendors target them with sophisticated sales motions. Boards want decisions fast. Staff lack time for deep evaluation. Decisions get made on demos and references hand-picked by the vendor. The result is software that does not fit, costs more than expected, and takes years to fully replace.

This worksheet imposes structure. It will not turn an inexperienced buyer into a procurement specialist, but it will surface the questions that have to be answered before signing a contract. Use it for any purchase above $5,000 annually.

## Stage 1: Needs Definition (Week 1)

Before evaluating any vendor, document what you actually need.

### Current state assessment

- What systems do you use today for this function?
- What works in the current state?
- What does not work?
- What workarounds have you built (spreadsheets, manual processes, duplicate data entry)?
- How much staff time does the current state cost per week?

Be specific. "Reporting is hard" is not actionable. "Pulling a year-over-year donor retention report by segment takes 4 hours of manual work in Excel each month" is.

### Required capabilities

List required capabilities, separated into:

- **Must-have.** The system fails without these.
- **Should-have.** Strong preference but not deal-breakers.
- **Nice-to-have.** Bonus features.

For a donor management evaluation, must-haves typically include: gift entry and acknowledgment, contact management, segmentation and queries, basic reporting, integration with payment processor, integration with email tool. Should-haves might include: campaign management, event registration, online giving forms, custom fields. Nice-to-haves: predictive analytics, AI assistants, mobile app.

For grants management, must-haves typically include: deadline tracking, document storage, restricted fund tracking integrated to the general ledger, reporting deadlines, budget vs. actual reporting. See the [grant management best practices guide](/resources/guides/grant-management-best-practices) for context.

### Volume and scale

- How many records (donors, grants, transactions) today?
- Projected growth over the next 3 years?
- Concurrent users?
- Peak load periods (year-end, audit season)?

Vendors price differently by scale. The same product can range 5x in cost depending on user count and record volume.

### Integration requirements

Map every system that needs to read or write data with the new system:

- Accounting (QuickBooks, Sage Intacct, Blackbaud Financial Edge)
- Email (Mailchimp, Constant Contact, HubSpot)
- Payment processor (Stripe, PayPal, Authorize.net)
- Online giving (Classy, Givebutter, Donorbox)
- Event registration
- Compliance and reporting tools

For each integration, specify direction (one-way, two-way), frequency (real-time, daily, manual), and data scope.

## Stage 2: Vendor Long List (Week 2)

Build a long list of 6 to 12 vendors that potentially fit. Sources:

- Idealware and TechSoup vendor directories
- Peer organizations of similar size and type
- Industry conferences (NTEN, AFP, NACUBO, depending on cause area)
- Vendor websites and reviews
- Capterra, G2, and Software Advice (with skepticism - pay-to-play dynamics)

For each vendor, capture:

- Product name
- Pricing model and approximate cost at your scale
- Target market (fits your size and type?)
- Key differentiators
- Initial fit score (1-5)

Filter to a short list of 3 to 5 vendors based on initial fit.

## Stage 3: RFP or Structured Inquiry (Week 3-4)

For purchases above $25,000 annually, issue a written RFP. For smaller purchases, send a structured email with your must-have list and ask for written responses.

### RFP structure

- Background on your organization
- Problem statement (what are you solving?)
- Required capabilities (must-haves and should-haves)
- Integration requirements
- Volume and scale data
- Implementation timeline expectations
- Vendor questions to answer
- Pricing breakdown requested (subscription, implementation, training, integrations, ongoing support)
- Reference requests (3 to 5 customers of similar size and type)
- Response deadline

### Vendor questions to answer

Beyond capabilities, ask:

- What is your typical implementation timeline for an organization our size?
- How is data migration handled, and what is the cost?
- What happens if we need to leave the system in 3 years?
- What integration approach do you use (native, API, middleware)?
- What is included in standard support? What costs extra?
- How are price increases handled at renewal?
- What is your customer retention rate?
- Who is your typical customer (size, type, vertical)?

## Stage 4: Demo and Reference Calls (Week 5-6)

### Structured demos

Do not let the vendor drive the demo. Provide a script of scenarios from your needs document:

- Walk us through entering a $500 gift from a new donor and triggering an acknowledgment
- Show us a year-over-year donor retention report by segment
- Walk us through closing out a multi-year grant with restricted fund balance reconciliation
- Show how a budget amendment moves through approval

If a vendor refuses or struggles to walk through your scenarios, that is a signal. Polished demos that avoid your specific use cases hide gaps.

### Reference checks

Reference customers picked by the vendor are filtered. Ask the vendor for references, then ask the references for additional contacts at peer organizations not on the official list.

Reference call questions:

- Why did you pick this vendor?
- What was the implementation experience?
- What surprised you (positively and negatively)?
- How is ongoing support?
- Would you pick this vendor again? If so, what would you do differently?
- Can you connect us with another customer?

A vendor whose references hesitate or hedge is showing you something the sales team will not.

## Stage 5: Total Cost of Ownership (Week 7)

Build a 3-year TCO model with all costs:

### Year 1

- Subscription fees (monthly - 12)
- Implementation services
- Data migration
- Integration setup
- Training
- Internal staff time during implementation (calculate at fully-loaded hourly rate)

### Year 2 and Year 3

- Subscription fees (with anticipated increase, often 3 to 8 percent per year)
- Ongoing support
- Additional integrations
- Custom development
- Internal admin time

The all-in TCO is often 1.5 to 3x the headline subscription cost in year one and 1.2 to 1.5x in subsequent years. Compare on TCO, not subscription price.

## Stage 6: Implementation Risk Assessment (Week 8)

Software fails when implementation fails. Score implementation risk on five dimensions:

- **Data quality.** How clean is your current data? Bad data going in is the most common implementation failure.
- **Internal capacity.** Who will own implementation internally? If no one, the vendor cannot succeed alone.
- **Timeline realism.** Has the vendor seen organizations like yours implement in the proposed timeline?
- **Change management.** How will staff be trained and adopted?
- **Integration complexity.** How many integrations are required, and how complex is each?

Each dimension scores 1-5. Total below 12 indicates high implementation risk. Slow down or restructure the project.

## Stage 7: Decision and Contract (Week 9-10)

### Final decision

Compare finalists on:

- Capability fit (1-5 by required capability)
- TCO over 3 years
- Implementation risk
- Reference quality
- Vendor stability (financial health, customer growth)
- Cultural fit (responsiveness, transparency)

Document the decision rationale in writing. Future evaluations benefit from the historical record.

### Contract review

Before signing:

- Term length and renewal terms
- Price increase caps
- SLA commitments and remedies
- Data ownership and export rights
- Termination terms and data return
- Indemnification and liability
- Data security and breach notification

Have legal counsel review contracts above $25,000 annually. Many nonprofit-friendly law firms offer pro bono review.

## Stage 8: Implementation Kickoff

Once signed, the work starts. Build an internal implementation plan covering:

- Data migration plan and acceptance criteria
- Integration build order
- Training schedule by role
- Parallel operation period
- Go-live date and rollback plan
- Post-implementation review at 30, 60, and 90 days

## Common Evaluation Failures

- **Skipping needs definition.** Buying based on vendor demos without a documented internal need.
- **Single-vendor evaluations.** No comparison set; cannot evaluate fit objectively.
- **Daily user not on evaluation team.** Decisions made by leadership; daily user works around bad fit for years.
- **TCO ignored.** Decision made on subscription price; total cost surprises year 1.
- **References from vendor only.** Hand-picked references give one-sided picture.
- **No exit plan.** Three years in, switching costs are higher than expected because data extraction was never tested.

The discipline this worksheet enforces is slow. That is the point. Mid-sized nonprofits cannot afford to get core technology decisions wrong. Six weeks of evaluation is cheap compared to three years of misfit software.
