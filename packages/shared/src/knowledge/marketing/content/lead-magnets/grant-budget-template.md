## What This Template Is For

A grant budget is the part of a proposal where good intentions meet arithmetic. Reviewers read the narrative for the story and the budget to find out whether you actually understand the work. A budget that does not tie to the project plan, that buries personnel in a single lump, or that applies an indirect rate nobody can explain signals an organization that will struggle to manage the award. This template exists to keep that from happening to you.

It is built around one idea: a grant budget is a defensible argument, not a guess. Every line should be allowable under the funder's rules, allocable to the funded project, and reasonable for what it buys. The structure here makes that argument legible. Costs are grouped by the standard categories funders expect, the math that should be a formula is a formula, and a paired narrative tab forces a one-sentence justification for every line so the numbers and the reasons never drift apart.

The template is most useful at the proposal stage and again at award setup, when the approved budget becomes the baseline you spend against. Use it to draft, to check your totals, and to hand a clean budget to whoever writes the final application. It is not accounting software and it does not replace your funder's required forms, but it gets the structure and the logic right before either of those matters.

## The Standard Federal Cost Categories

Federal budgets are organized into a fixed set of cost categories, and most foundations have quietly adopted the same vocabulary. Using them from the first draft is the single easiest way to make a budget read as competent. The categories are personnel, fringe benefits, travel, equipment, supplies, contractual, other direct costs, and indirect costs.

The order is not arbitrary. Direct costs come first because they are the costs you can tie to the project specifically. Indirect costs come last because they are computed from the direct costs once those are settled. The template lays the Budget tab out in this sequence, with subtotal rows that roll the line items up into a Total Direct Costs figure before indirect is ever calculated.

Getting the category right for each cost matters more than it seems. The same dollar can be allowable in one category and a problem in another, and reviewers notice when costs are parked in the wrong bucket to dodge a rule. The sections below walk through the categories where organizations most often go wrong: personnel and fringe, the equipment versus supplies line, and indirect costs.

## Personnel and Fringe by Percent Effort

Personnel is usually the largest category and the one reviewers scrutinize most, because it is where wishful budgeting hides. The template drives every personnel line from two honest inputs: an annual salary and a percent of effort. The budgeted cost is salary multiplied by effort, computed by formula, so you cannot accidentally request more than the math supports.

Percent effort is the discipline that keeps personnel real. If a program director earns 90,000 dollars and will spend a quarter of their time on this project, the grant can request 22,500 dollars of that salary, not a round number someone liked better. Effort across all of a person's funded projects cannot exceed 100 percent, and a budget that implies otherwise will not survive review. Stating effort explicitly also sets up the time and effort certification you will owe later on a federal award.

Fringe benefits are the employer's share of payroll taxes, health insurance, retirement, and similar costs. They are a rate applied to salary, not a separate guess. The template puts the fringe rate in a labeled input cell and computes the fringe line as a percentage of the personnel subtotal, so a single rate change flows through the whole budget. Use your organization's actual fringe rate; a rate that does not match your audited financials is an easy finding for a reviewer to flag.

## Other Direct Costs, Equipment, and the Capitalization Threshold

After personnel and fringe come the other direct costs: travel, equipment, supplies, contractual, and a catch-all other category. Most of these are straightforward, but one line trips up more budgets than any other, and that is the split between equipment and supplies.

Under the Uniform Guidance, equipment is tangible property with a useful life of more than one year and a per-unit acquisition cost at or above the applicable capitalization threshold, typically 10,000 dollars under current federal rules or a lower organizational threshold. Anything below that threshold is supplies. The distinction is not bookkeeping pedantry. Equipment is treated differently for indirect cost purposes, which is exactly why the line matters, and the template includes a sample equipment item above the threshold so the structure is visible.

Travel should be budgeted to a basis, not a lump sum: number of trips, travelers, nights, and a per-night or per-mile rate, ideally tied to a published rate you can defend. Contractual covers work performed by an outside vendor or subrecipient under the award. The general rule across all of these is the same one a reviewer applies to every line: show the basis. A cost with visible arithmetic behind it reads as allowable and reasonable; a round number with no derivation invites a question you would rather not answer.

## Indirect Costs and the 15 Percent De Minimis Rate

Indirect costs are the real expenses of running an organization that cannot be tied to a single project: accounting, the executive director's oversight, rent on shared space, insurance. They are legitimate, and the mistake nonprofits make most often is undercharging them, effectively subsidizing federal work out of unrestricted dollars.

There are two ways to claim indirect costs. If your organization has a negotiated indirect cost rate agreement with a federal cognizant agency, you use that rate and the base it specifies. If you do not, you may use the de minimis rate, which the 2024 revision of the Uniform Guidance raised to 15 percent of modified total direct costs, up from the long-standing 10 percent. That increase is real money on every federal award, and any organization without a negotiated rate should be using the full 15 percent.

The base matters as much as the rate. Modified total direct costs, or MTDC, are not simply your total direct costs. MTDC excludes equipment and excludes the portion of each subaward over 50,000 dollars, among a few other items. The template puts the indirect rate in a labeled input cell defaulting to 0.15, computes the MTDC base as total direct costs minus equipment, and calculates indirect as the base times the rate. Because the rate is a single editable cell, swapping in a negotiated rate later takes one keystroke and the whole budget updates.

## Cost Share, the Budget Narrative, and Allowable Costs

Some awards require cost share or match, meaning your organization commits resources alongside the funder's. Match can be cash or in-kind, such as donated professional services or volunteer time valued at a defensible rate. If a match is required, it is a condition of the award, not a nicety, and unmet match can mean returning funds. The template includes a Cost Share tab to record each source, whether it is cash or in-kind, the amount, and a note, so the commitment is documented before you make it.

The budget narrative is where the numbers earn their place. A budget tells a reviewer what you want; the narrative explains why each cost is necessary and how you derived it. The template pairs every sample budget line with a one-sentence justification on the Budget Narrative tab, so the habit is built in. "Program coordinator at 0.5 FTE to deliver weekly sessions to 40 participants" is a justification; a personnel number with no explanation is a gap.

Two rules sit underneath everything. First, costs must be allowable: the funder's terms and the cost principles in 2 CFR 200 Subpart E govern what you can charge, and certain costs such as lobbying, fundraising, and entertainment are generally unallowable on federal awards regardless of how reasonable they feel. Second, costs must fall within the period of performance: a cost incurred before the start date or after the end date is not chargeable to the award. Build the budget to the project period, not the calendar year, unless they happen to align.

## When to Graduate to Software

This template is honest about its limits. A spreadsheet is the right tool for drafting a budget and for managing the budget of a single, simple award. It begins to strain the moment the approved budget has to be tracked against actual spending across several grants with different periods and shared staff, because at that point the budget is no longer a document, it is a live control.

The signals are concrete. If you are rebuilding a budget-versus-actual comparison by hand at each reporting deadline, if percent effort has to be reconciled across multiple awards for time and effort certification, if a budget amendment in one grant ripples into allocations you track in another, or if more than one person edits the workbook, the spreadsheet has become the risk rather than the safeguard. Crossing the federal single audit threshold raises the documentation and internal-control bar past what manual discipline reliably meets.

Moving to dedicated grant and fund accounting software is not a failure of the spreadsheet; it is the spreadsheet succeeding at its job, which was to get you to the volume of activity that justifies a system enforcing the rules instead of trusting everyone to remember them. Draft your budgets here, keep the structure clean, and treat the move to software as the next stage of doing the work well.
