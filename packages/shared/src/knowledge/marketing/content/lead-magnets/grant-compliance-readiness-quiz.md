## Why a Maturity Quiz, Not a Checklist

Compliance checklists answer "did you do the thing." Maturity assessments answer "is the thing the way you operate." The difference matters because a one-time SEFA reconciliation, a one-time procurement memo, or a one-time policy update gets you through one audit cycle. Maturity is what survives staff turnover, board changes, and the next funder.

This quiz is a maturity assessment. It asks not just whether you have a written cost transfer policy - it asks whether your team references it, whether transfers are documented at the workpaper level, and whether the 90-day rule is enforced. The same applies to SF-425 process, drawdown reconciliation, and subaward reporting. The questions are designed to distinguish "we know we should" from "we have evidence we did."

## The Five Levels

**Level 1 - Ad hoc.** Compliance work happens reactively when a deadline arrives or a funder asks. There is no written framework. The highest-risk posture for federal grantees.

**Level 2 - Reactive.** Compliance work happens, but mostly under deadline pressure. Some processes are repeated each cycle but they aren't written down. Knowledge lives with one or two people.

**Level 3 - Defined.** Processes exist and are followed, but documentation is uneven. Most policies exist on paper somewhere. Staff have been trained but not recently. This is the modal posture for mid-sized nonprofits.

**Level 4 - Managed.** Processes are documented, training happens annually, and management reviews compliance metrics regularly. Audit findings are infrequent and resolved with corrective action plans. The first level at which compliance is institutionalized.

**Level 5 - Optimized.** Compliance is part of operating culture. Processes are continuously improved based on findings, training is role-specific, and the organization passes audits without remediation. Rare but achievable for organizations with mature finance leadership.

## How to Use Your Result

Your result places you on the scale and lists the compliance guides that map to your weakest areas. The guides are practical workpapers and templates: the Grant Compliance Checklist for the foundational scan, the 2 CFR 200 Audit Prep Checklist for federal grantees, the SF-425 Reporting Checklist for federal financial reporting, and the Single Audit Prep Timeline for organizations approaching the $1M expenditure threshold.

The realistic improvement target is one level per 12-18 months. Going from level 2 to level 3 typically requires writing your top three policies (procurement, allowability, cost transfer) and training staff on them. Going from level 3 to level 4 requires institutionalizing compliance metrics in monthly finance reports and tying corrective action plans to board oversight. Don't try to skip levels - the documentation and training work is cumulative.

Retake the quiz in six to twelve months to validate that the work translated into a higher score. If it didn't, the work was paper rather than practice.

## What the assessment measures

The assessment scores readiness across the control areas and operating practices that most reliably distinguish well-run mid-sized nonprofits from organizations operating in chronic catch-up mode. Each area is weighted by the operational and audit risk it carries, so the score reflects where remediation effort should go first.

## How to use your score

A score above 80 indicates strong fundamentals across the assessed dimensions. A score between 60 and 80 indicates one or two areas that need attention, usually addressable within a quarter of focused work. A score below 60 indicates structural gaps that benefit from a deliberate remediation calendar with named owners and dates. The questionnaire routes you to the specific remediation worksheet for each gap so you can act on the score immediately rather than scheduling a follow-up project.

## What separates strong programs from weak ones

The pattern across nonprofits that score well: they treat the relevant work as monthly discipline rather than annual scramble. They document as they go, they tie financial and program data together at the moment of entry rather than reconstructing it at report time, and they use systems that surface the relevant constraints and metrics on a regular cadence rather than only at deadline pressure. These are cultural and structural traits more than they are technology choices, but the technology either supports the discipline or fights it.

## After the assessment

Once you have your score, the next step is a remediation calendar with named owners and dates. The remediation worksheets the assessment routes you to are designed to be completed inside one or two work sessions, not as multi-month projects. The principle throughout: shift the work from annual to monthly, document as you go, and the underlying numbers improve on the same multi-year horizon as the underlying balance sheet and operating practices.
