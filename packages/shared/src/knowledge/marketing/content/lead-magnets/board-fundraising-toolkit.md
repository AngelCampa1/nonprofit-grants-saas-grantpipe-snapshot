## Why Boards Underperform on Fundraising

The most common pattern in mid-sized nonprofits: a 12-person board, two members who actively raise money, three who give but do not ask, and seven who attend meetings and contribute expertise but not dollars. Total board fundraising rarely exceeds 5 percent of revenue. Leadership accepts the dynamic because changing it feels confrontational.

This pattern is not a recruiting failure or a willingness failure. It is a structural failure. The board never agreed to a written fundraising expectation. New members were not told what they were signing up for. There is no quarterly mechanism to make commitments visible. The Executive Director carries the awkwardness of asking individual members to do more.

This toolkit fixes the structure. It will not turn an academic into a major gift closer. It will turn a willing-but-untrained board member into someone who consistently opens doors, makes thank-you calls, and contributes to fundraising results.

## Section 1: The Give/Get Policy

A give/get policy is a written expectation that every board member will personally give and personally raise (get) defined amounts each year. Without it, fundraising lives in the gray zone where everyone hopes someone else will step up.

### Sample policy language

> Each board member commits to a personally significant annual gift of at least $X, in addition to raising $Y through introductions, asks, sponsorships, or other fundraising activity. The combined give/get expectation is $Z annually. The policy applies to all board members and is reviewed annually by the Governance Committee.

### Setting the number

Three inputs:

1. **Organizational fundraising goal.** What is your annual private contributed revenue target?
2. **Board contribution share.** What share should the board be responsible for? Sector benchmarks suggest 10 to 25 percent of private revenue from the board and their networks.
3. **Board financial capacity.** What is the realistic distribution of giving capacity across current members?

Divide goal - share by board size to get the average per-member target. Round to a credible number. Adjust for variation: the policy is a floor, not a ceiling.

### Common levels

- Small organizations ($500K to $1M revenue): $1,000 to $2,500 give/get per member
- Mid-sized ($1M to $5M): $2,500 to $10,000 give/get per member
- Larger ($5M to $10M): $10,000 to $25,000 give/get per member

### Implementation

- Adopt the policy at a board meeting with a vote.
- Include it in the recruitment process for all new members.
- Build it into the annual self-assessment.
- Report progress quarterly. Public accountability moves the number more than any other intervention.

## Section 2: The Prospect List

Every board member should produce a personal prospect list of 10 to 25 names: people in their network who could give, give more, or be cultivated for future giving.

### Worksheet structure

For each prospect, capture:

- Name and relationship to the board member
- Estimated giving capacity ($, $$, $$$, $$$$)
- Current relationship with the organization (none, donor, lapsed, prospect)
- Inclination signals (mission interest, prior support of similar causes)
- Suggested next move (introduction to ED, invitation to event, ask for X)

The board member does not need to commit to ask each prospect. The list is a starting point for staff and the board member to triage together.

### Prospect identification prompts

If a board member draws a blank, walk through these prompts:

- Whose name is on a building, plaque, or program at another nonprofit you know?
- Who in your professional network has expressed interest in our cause area?
- Whose company has a foundation or corporate giving program?
- Whose family foundation has supported organizations like ours?
- Who attended your last birthday or work milestone event?
- Who do you serve on another board with that might be a prospect?

Most board members come up with 10 to 15 names with these prompts. Some come up with 50.

## Section 3: The Ask Script

Most reluctant board members can be coached through an ask if given a script. The script is not for reading aloud. It is for rehearsing.

### Five-part ask structure

1. **Open with relationship.** "I am asking because I trust you and because I think this fits something you care about."
2. **State the why.** "Here is what the organization is working on and why it matters." (60 seconds, specific.)
3. **State the ask.** "Would you consider a gift of $X to support this work?" (Specific number. Silence after.)
4. **Listen.** Do not fill the silence. The donor needs space to respond.
5. **Close with next step.** Yes, no, or maybe - each has a defined next move (acknowledgment, thank-you, follow-up date).

### Sample script

> "Thanks for meeting with me. I wanted to talk to you about [organization] because I have been on the board for [time] and I think the work fits something you care about.
>
> The organization runs [program]. This year, we are trying to do [specific goal]. To get there, we need to raise [campaign target] from people in our community.
>
> I am asking my closest connections, including you, whether you would consider a gift of [amount]. Your support would [specific impact].
>
> Would that be possible for you this year?"

Then stop talking.

### Handling the responses

- **Yes:** "Thank you. I will send you the information to make the gift. Can I introduce you to [ED]?"
- **No, with reason:** "I understand. Is there a different way you would like to support the work?"
- **Maybe:** "I appreciate you considering it. Can we follow up in two weeks?"
- **Counteroffer at lower amount:** "Thank you. That makes a real difference."

The board member's job is not to convert every ask. It is to make the ask cleanly and pass the prospect to staff for follow-through.

## Section 4: Warm Introduction Template

For board members who will not personally ask but will make introductions, the warm introduction is the highest-leverage activity.

### Email template

> Subject: Introduction - [Prospect] and [ED]
>
> Hi [Prospect],
>
> I want to introduce you to [ED], the Executive Director of [organization]. I serve on their board and have been impressed by [specific accomplishment]. They are working on [specific initiative] this year, and I thought of you because [reason].
>
> [ED], I am cc'ing you so you can reach out directly to [Prospect]. They are involved in [Prospect's relevant work or interest].
>
> Looking forward to seeing where this goes.
>
> Best, [Board Member]

This single email pattern accounts for the majority of major gift introductions in well-functioning boards. Most board members can write 5 to 15 of these per year without ever making an ask themselves.

## Section 5: Quarterly Accountability

Annual goals slip. Quarterly check-ins do not.

### Quarterly board fundraising scorecard

For each board member, track:

- Personal gift made (yes/no, amount)
- Prospects added to pipeline this quarter
- Introductions made
- Thank-you calls made
- Asks made (count)
- Revenue raised through their network this quarter

Review the scorecard at the quarterly board meeting. Public visibility is the accountability mechanism. Most members do not want to be the only zero on the scorecard.

### What to do with persistent under-performers

After two consecutive quarters of zero activity, the board chair should have a private conversation. Three options:

1. Renewed commitment with a defined plan
2. Shift to a non-board role (advisory, committee chair) where fundraising is not expected
3. Step off at the next term

Boards that allow persistent non-performance signal to active members that the policy is not real. This is corrosive over time.

## Section 6: Connecting Board Activity to the Fundraising Pipeline

Every prospect a board member identifies should enter the major gift pipeline. See the [major gift cultivation guide](/resources/guides/major-gift-cultivation-practical-guide) for the cultivation moves that follow.

The handoff from board member to staff is where most prospects disappear. The toolkit's recommended flow:

1. Board member identifies prospect on quarterly worksheet.
2. Board member and ED meet within two weeks to discuss.
3. ED or development staff researches and assigns a cultivation stage.
4. Staff drives next move; board member is looped in for personal touches.
5. Quarterly review of pipeline movement.

Without this flow, a prospect goes from a name on a worksheet to nothing. With it, the worksheet becomes the front of a pipeline that produces measurable revenue.

## Section 7: Onboarding New Board Members

Most fundraising failures start at recruitment. New members agreed to something they did not understand.

### Pre-vote disclosure

Before nomination, every prospective member should receive a written packet including:

- Mission and current programs
- Annual budget and revenue mix
- Current strategic priorities
- Time commitment expectation
- Give/get policy with the specific number
- Sample quarterly scorecard

Members who join with this information consistently outperform those recruited on vague pitches.

### First 90 days

- Tour and program experience in week 1
- Coffee with the ED in week 2
- Coffee with the development director in week 3
- First prospect list submitted by day 60
- First thank-you calls assigned by day 90

By day 90, the new member should have a small but real fundraising contribution behind them. Momentum is easier to build than to recover.

## Common Failure Modes

- **Policy without enforcement.** A give/get policy on paper that no one references. Worse than no policy because it signals leadership does not mean it.
- **Annual goals only.** Without quarterly checkpoints, the year ends with most members at zero.
- **No staff support.** Asking a board member to ask without giving them the tools and prep time.
- **Recruiting on social cachet only.** Adding a famous name without confirming time and giving capacity.
- **Avoiding the awkward conversation.** The chair will not address persistent non-performance. The policy collapses.

The shortest path to a fundraising board is structural: written policy, named prospects, scripted asks, quarterly accountability, and visible support from staff. None of those steps is novel. The reason most boards do not have them is not difficulty - it is that someone has to lead the change. This toolkit is the starting point.
