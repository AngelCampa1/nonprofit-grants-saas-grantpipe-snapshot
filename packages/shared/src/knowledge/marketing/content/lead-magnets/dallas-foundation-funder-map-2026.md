## Why This Map Exists

The DFW metroplex hosts roughly 27,000 registered nonprofits and a foundation community shaped by family wealth, corporate philanthropy, and a small number of large community foundations. The largest 25 funders shape most regional giving, and their priority areas overlap considerably - which makes precision targeting more important, not less.

This map shows what each foundation has actually funded over the last three years, paired with stated priorities, so LOI capacity goes where it has a real chance of converting.

## How to Use This Map

Pick three funders whose 24-month grant history matches your program area, budget tier, and DFW sub-region. Use the median-grant-size column to right-size every ask. Family-foundation funders in this region are particularly sensitive to over-asks.

## Common Failure Modes This Map Surfaces

The patterns most DFW nonprofits miss until the second or third declined LOI:

- Foundations whose website lists a program area they have not actually funded in three years.
- Foundations whose typical grant size is well below what your program needs.
- Foundations that only fund prior grantees but accept unsolicited LOIs anyway.
- Foundations where 24-month giving has shifted because of trustee transitions, program officer turnover, or new strategic plans not yet reflected publicly.

Each entry in the map flags these patterns where they appear so the prospect list you build is one you can act on.

## What to Do With This Map First

Open it next to your current prospect list. Cross out funders where the median grant size, geographic focus, or program area no longer matches your work. The remaining names get a 24-month-grant-history pull from ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer or Candid before any LOI is drafted.

Most DFW organizations cut their tracked prospect list by 30-50% after one pass and redirect that capacity to deeper stewardship of the funders that survive the cut.

## Family Foundations and the Over-Ask Problem

DFW's family-foundation community is one of the deepest in the country, and family foundations behave differently from community foundations or corporate funders. The trustees are often donors, the relationships are long, and the median grant size is smaller than the public pages suggest because the family is making many small bets rather than a few large ones.

The most common rejection pattern in this region is the over-ask: an organization with a $400K program need approaches a family foundation whose median grant is $35K and asks for the full $400K in one year. The application gets read, the trustees note that they cannot fund anywhere near that, and the relationship ends before it begins. The same organization could often have built a multi-year relationship by asking for $35K against a specific milestone, demonstrating fit, and growing the relationship over time.

The right-sized-ask column on this map is the single most important filter. Use it before drafting.
