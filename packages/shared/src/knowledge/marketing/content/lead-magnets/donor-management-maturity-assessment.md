## Why a Maturity Lens

Most development directors know what good fundraising looks like - they've read the books, attended the conferences, can describe what their operation should be doing. The gap between that knowledge and what's actually documented and operating in the database is where this assessment lives.

Maturity is a documentation-and-discipline measure, not a budget measure. A $1M nonprofit with a written stewardship plan, retention tracking, and a board doing portfolio work outscores a $5M nonprofit that runs on ad hoc appeals and a major gifts officer's memory. The score reflects practice, not scale.

## The Five Levels

**Level 1 - Reactive.** Fundraising happens when there's a need or a deadline. No retention measurement, no written plan, no segmentation beyond perhaps a major donor list. Acknowledgments are slow or inconsistent.

**Level 2 - Foundational.** Donors are tracked and acknowledged. There's an appeal calendar but it's not segment-aware. Retention is occasionally calculated but not used as a steering metric. The board reviews fundraising but doesn't engage donors.

**Level 3 - Developing.** Core fundamentals exist: retention is tracked, appeals are segmented at least by giving level, acknowledgments go out within a week, and there's a written annual development plan. Major donor work happens but isn't formalized into portfolios with documented moves.

**Level 4 - Strong.** Most disciplines are in place: retention by segment, RFM-based appeal targeting, documented stewardship plan with tier-specific touch counts, fast acknowledgments, formal major donor portfolios, monthly giving program. Gaps tend to cluster around board engagement and lapsed donor renewal.

**Level 5 - Best-in-class.** Documented systems run the operation. Stewardship is tier-specific and tracked. Major donor moves management is current. Monthly giving has dedicated stewardship. Board members have portfolios and are accountable. Planned giving is an active line of work.

## How to Use the Result

The realistic improvement target is one level per 12 to 18 months. The assessment links to the GrantPipe playbooks that map to your weakest areas - Donor Retention Playbook for retention gaps, Major Donor Cultivation Playbook for portfolio work, Donor Stewardship Plan Template for institutionalizing stewardship, Monthly Giving Program Launch Checklist for organizations without a recurring program.

Don't try to skip levels. Each level depends on the work of the level below: stewardship plans without retention measurement don't survive board scrutiny; major donor portfolios without a stewardship plan produce inconsistent moves. The discipline is cumulative.

Retake the assessment at the end of a fiscal year. The score should move; if it didn't, the development plan didn't translate into operational changes.

## What the assessment measures

The assessment scores readiness across the control areas and operating practices that most reliably distinguish well-run mid-sized nonprofits from organizations operating in chronic catch-up mode. Each area is weighted by the operational and audit risk it carries, so the score reflects where remediation effort should go first.

## How to use your score

A score above 80 indicates strong fundamentals across the assessed dimensions. A score between 60 and 80 indicates one or two areas that need attention, usually addressable within a quarter of focused work. A score below 60 indicates structural gaps that benefit from a deliberate remediation calendar with named owners and dates. The questionnaire routes you to the specific remediation worksheet for each gap so you can act on the score immediately rather than scheduling a follow-up project.

## What separates strong programs from weak ones

The pattern across nonprofits that score well: they treat the relevant work as monthly discipline rather than annual scramble. They document as they go, they tie financial and program data together at the moment of entry rather than reconstructing it at report time, and they use systems that surface the relevant constraints and metrics on a regular cadence rather than only at deadline pressure. These are cultural and structural traits more than they are technology choices, but the technology either supports the discipline or fights it.

## After the assessment

Once you have your score, the next step is a remediation calendar with named owners and dates. The remediation worksheets the assessment routes you to are designed to be completed inside one or two work sessions, not as multi-month projects. The principle throughout: shift the work from annual to monthly, document as you go, and the underlying numbers improve on the same multi-year horizon as the underlying balance sheet and operating practices.
