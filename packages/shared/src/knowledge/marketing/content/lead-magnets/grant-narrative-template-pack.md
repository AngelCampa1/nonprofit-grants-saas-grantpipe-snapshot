## Why Templates Help When They Provide Scaffolding, Not Boilerplate

Every grant writer eventually develops their own narrative templates - patterns of phrasing, transitions, and structural choices that work. The templates in this pack are scaffolding versions of those patterns. They give you a starting structure for each section so you spend writing time on substance, not on figuring out how to begin.

Templates fail when they become boilerplate. A reviewer reading 30 proposals can spot reused language by the third paragraph. The fix is to use templates for structure (section flow, transitions, what each paragraph accomplishes) and write your own substance (specifics, data, voice).

This pack covers six narrative sections common to most grant proposals: statement of need, approach, evaluation, organizational capacity, sustainability, and budget narrative. Each has variants for federal, foundation, and state funders.

For complementary guidance, see the [grant proposal writing guide](/resources/guides/grant-proposal-writing-guide) and the [grant lifecycle guide](/resources/guides/grant-lifecycle-guide).

## Section 1: Statement of Need

The needs section establishes that the problem is real, severe, and within your service area. Reviewers look for triangulated data, geographic specificity, and a connection from the broad problem to your specific population.

### Federal template structure

> **Paragraph 1: Frame the problem at scale.**
> Open with national or state-level data establishing the problem's scale. Cite the data source and year. Avoid emotional appeals; reviewers expect rigor in this section.
>
> **Paragraph 2: Localize the problem.**
> Transition from scale to your specific service area. Cite local data: census, county health rankings, school district indicators, or other geographically specific sources.
>
> **Paragraphs 3-5: Specify the affected population.**
> Detail the demographics, geographic concentration, and lived experience of the population you will serve. Use multiple data layers (demographic, behavioral, outcome) to build a coherent picture.
>
> **Paragraph 6: Identify the gap in current services.**
> What is currently being done? Why is it insufficient? Where is the unmet need that your program will address?
>
> **Paragraph 7: Establish your organization's standing to address the need.**
> Why are you the right organization? Brief, transition-paragraph length.

### Foundation template structure

> **Paragraph 1: Open with the human reality.**
> Foundation reviewers respond to specific stories more than federal reviewers. Open with a brief representative situation that illustrates the problem.
>
> **Paragraph 2: Frame the scale.**
> Move from the specific to the general with one or two compelling statistics from authoritative sources.
>
> **Paragraph 3: Connect to the foundation's priorities.**
> Use the foundation's language. If they speak about "thriving children," do not switch to "youth development." Match.
>
> **Paragraph 4: Establish the gap and your standing.**
> Why this work, why now, why your organization?

### State template structure

State narratives typically follow a prescribed RFP structure. The template provides phrasing for common state requirements:

> The [state-defined target population] in [geography] face [specific issue] at rates [X% / Y rate] above the state average. Per [authoritative state source], [supporting statistic]. Current services in [geography] include [list], but [gap analysis]. Our proposed program will serve [number] participants over the project period, focusing on [specific intervention].

### Common needs section weaknesses

- Generic claims without data ("Many families struggle...")
- Data without geographic specificity (national stats applied to a county program)
- Statistics without sources or with stale sources (>5 years old)
- Emotional appeals without rigor (federal); rigor without humanity (foundation)
- No connection from broad need to specific service population

## Section 2: Approach and Methodology

The approach section is the longest in most narratives. Reviewers score whether your activities will plausibly produce your outcomes.

### Federal template structure

> **Goals and objectives.**
> State the program goal in one sentence. Then list 3 to 5 SMART objectives (specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, time-bound).
>
> **Logic model summary.**
> Inputs †’ Activities †’ Outputs †’ Short-term outcomes †’ Long-term outcomes. One sentence per stage.
>
> **Activities detail.**
> For each major activity, describe: what will be done, by whom, how often, with what frequency, for what duration. This is where the proposal earns or loses points on rigor.
>
> **Timeline.**
> Quarterly or monthly milestones across the period of performance. Use a table or timeline graphic if the NOFO allows.
>
> **Staffing.**
> Brief description of who does what, with FTE allocations. Detailed bios go in attachments.
>
> **Theory of change.**
> Why will the activities produce the outcomes? What is the causal logic? This is the most-skipped element and the most-scored.

### Activities detail template

For each activity:

> **[Activity name].** Beginning [date] and continuing throughout the project period, [staff role] will deliver [activity description] to [target population]. The activity will reach [number] participants per [period] for a total of [number] over the project period. Each [unit of activity] will [specific content/intervention]. Quality will be ensured through [training, supervision, fidelity measures].

### Foundation template structure

Shorter, less prescriptive:

> **The work.**
> What you will do, in your voice. Foundations want clarity, not jargon. Avoid acronyms; if you must use them, define them once.
>
> **The plan.**
> A concise timeline, often a few sentences or a brief bulleted list.
>
> **The team.**
> Who is doing the work. Names and roles, brief.

### State template structure

State RFPs often require activities mapped to specific deliverables. The template:

> Activity 1: [Name]. Deliverable: [specific output]. Target: [number, unit]. Timeline: [period]. Responsible: [role]. Performance measure: [metric].

Repeat for each activity.

## Section 3: Evaluation Plan

Evaluation answers: how will you know if it worked?

### Federal template structure

> **Process evaluation.**
> Did we do what we said we would do? Track activity completion, participant counts, fidelity to model.
>
> **Outcome evaluation.**
> Did the activities produce the intended outcomes? Pre/post measures, comparison groups where appropriate, validated instruments.
>
> **Data collection plan.**
> What data, collected by whom, with what instrument, at what cadence, stored where, analyzed by whom.
>
> **Use of findings.**
> How will evaluation inform program adjustments? Quarterly review meetings? External evaluator reports?
>
> **Reporting.**
> How will findings be shared with the funder, participants, and the field?

### Foundation template structure

> **What we will measure.**
> Two or three concrete outcome indicators. Foundations rarely need a full evaluation framework.
>
> **How we will measure.**
> Brief description of data collection.
>
> **What we will report.**
> What the foundation will receive at year-end.

## Section 4: Organizational Capacity

Capacity convinces reviewers that you can actually run the program.

### Template structure (applies across funder types)

> **Organizational history.**
> Founding year, mission, brief history. One paragraph.
>
> **Programs and impact.**
> Current programs, populations served, key outcomes. Cite specific numbers and recent timeframes.
>
> **Leadership.**
> Executive Director and key program staff. Brief bios with relevant experience. Detailed CVs in attachments.
>
> **Board governance.**
> Board size, composition, governance practices. Mention board roster in attachments.
>
> **Financial capacity.**
> Annual budget, revenue mix, audited status. For larger asks, include audit findings (or absence of findings).
>
> **Compliance and prior performance.**
> If you have prior federal awards, summarize performance. Mention single audit history if applicable. See the [Uniform Guidance practical guide](/resources/guides/uniform-guidance-2-cfr-200-practical-guide).
>
> **Infrastructure.**
> Data systems, financial systems, compliance infrastructure. This matters more than most applicants think.

### Capacity weakness signal

If your capacity section is shorter than your needs section, reviewers will worry. Capacity is where mid-sized organizations lose points to larger ones. Compensate with specifics about systems, partnerships, and prior performance.

## Section 5: Sustainability Plan

Sustainability answers: what happens when the grant ends?

### Template structure

> **Revenue diversification.**
> What revenue streams will continue the program? Identify specific sources: earned revenue, recurring donors, government contracts, foundation continuation.
>
> **Partnership formalization.**
> What partnerships will the program build that continue beyond the grant? MOUs, contracts, formal collaborations.
>
> **System integration.**
> How will the program become embedded in existing systems (schools, healthcare, government services) so it does not require continued grant funding?
>
> **Follow-on funding.**
> Identify specific federal, state, or foundation programs that could fund the work after the current grant. Demonstrate that the path forward is real, not aspirational.

### Common sustainability weaknesses

- "We will pursue diversified funding." (Vague.)
- "Foundations will continue to fund us." (Unsupported.)
- "The program will become self-sustaining." (Without showing how.)

Reviewers expect specifics. Name the foundations, the contracts, the partnerships. If you cannot, the sustainability section is weak.

## Section 6: Budget Narrative

The budget narrative justifies every line item from the budget form.

### Template structure

For each line item:

> **[Line item, e.g., Program Manager salary]: $X**
> [Role description in one sentence]. [FTE allocation: e.g., 1.0 FTE for 12 months at $XX,XXX annual salary]. [Calculation: $XX,XXX - 12/12 - 100%]. The Program Manager is responsible for [specific duties tied to project activities].

For benefits:

> **Fringe benefits: $X**
> Calculated at [organization's fringe rate]% of total personnel costs. The fringe rate includes [FICA, health insurance, retirement, workers' comp, unemployment].

For travel:

> **Travel: $X**
> [Specific trips with destinations, purpose, number of travelers, per-trip cost]. Mileage at federal POV rate. Per diem at federal rate for destination.

For supplies:

> **Supplies: $X**
> [Specific categories with quantities and unit costs]. All supplies will be used directly in support of project activities.

For contractual:

> **Contractual: $X**
> [Subcontractor name, scope of work, deliverables, basis for cost]. Procurement followed [organization's policy / federal procurement standards under 2 CFR 200.317-.327].

For indirect costs:

> **Indirect costs: $X**
> Calculated at [negotiated rate / 15% de minimis under 2 CFR 200.414(f)] of [base, typically modified total direct costs]. Documentation of rate is included in attachments.

## Stitching the Sections Together

The narrative reads as one document. Use transitions that connect sections:

- Need †’ Approach: "To address this need, we propose..."
- Approach †’ Evaluation: "We will measure success through..."
- Evaluation †’ Capacity: "Our organization is positioned to deliver this work because..."
- Capacity †’ Sustainability: "Beyond this grant, we will sustain the program through..."
- Sustainability †’ Budget: "The budget below reflects the activities described above..."

## Common Narrative Failures Across Sections

- **Generic boilerplate** that could apply to any organization or any funder.
- **Activities and outcomes disconnected** by missing theory of change.
- **Capacity section thinner than the proposal merits**, signaling implementation risk.
- **Sustainability claims without specificity**, signaling program will collapse post-grant.
- **Budget narrative inconsistent with budget form**, signaling carelessness.
- **Length disproportionate to scoring weight**, leaving high-value sections under-developed.

Use the templates as scaffolding. Write your own substance against the structure. The goal is a narrative that reads as one organization's specific case for this specific grant, not a templated submission. The template helps you start; the substance has to be yours.
