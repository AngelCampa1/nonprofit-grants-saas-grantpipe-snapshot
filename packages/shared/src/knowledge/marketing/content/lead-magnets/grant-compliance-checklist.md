## Why Post-Award Compliance Is Where Nonprofits Fail

Winning a grant feels like the hard part. Writing the proposal, waiting months for a decision, competing against dozens of other organizations. But the grant lifecycle doesn't end at the award letter -- it starts there.

Post-award compliance is the set of obligations you accepted when you signed the grant agreement. These include spending restrictions (the money can only be used for what you said it would be used for), reporting requirements (the funder wants to know what you did with the money), and documentation standards (you need to prove everything).

The stakes are high. Non-compliance can result in having to return grant funds, being barred from future grants from that funder, or being flagged in your single audit. For mid-sized nonprofits managing multiple grants simultaneously, a compliance failure on one grant can cascade: auditors start looking more closely at everything, funders share information, and your organization's reputation takes a hit that takes years to repair.

This checklist covers the post-award compliance process from the moment you receive an award letter through grant closeout. It's organized by the order you'll need to do things, not by topic.

## Step 1: Read the Award Letter and Grant Agreement Completely

This sounds obvious, but most compliance failures trace back to someone not reading the fine print in the grant agreement. Before spending a single dollar, the person responsible for compliance (typically the Development Director, Grants Manager, or Executive Director) should read the entire document and extract:

**Allowable costs.** What specific line items can you charge to this grant? The budget you submitted with the application is usually the baseline, but the award letter may modify it. Some funders restrict indirect cost rates, cap travel expenses, or prohibit certain categories entirely.

**Budget modification rules.** Can you move money between budget line items? Federal grant rules on rebudgeting without prior approval vary by agency and program. Your Notice of Award or agency-specific regulations will specify the threshold (if any) that applies. Private foundations vary widely. Some require approval for any deviation; others give you discretion within broad categories.

**Reporting deadlines.** When are financial reports due? Narrative reports? Progress reports? Federal grants typically require quarterly financial reports (SF-425) and annual or semi-annual narrative reports. Private foundations often want annual reports, but the format and deadline vary by funder.

**Match requirements.** Does the grant require a cash or in-kind match? If so, how do you document it? Match requirements that aren't tracked from day one become a scramble at reporting time.

**Period of performance.** When does the grant start and end? You cannot charge expenses that fall outside this window. If you incur a cost before the start date or after the end date, it's ineligible even if it directly supports the funded program.

**Prior approval requirements.** What changes require funder approval before you act? Common triggers: hiring key personnel not named in the proposal, purchasing equipment above a threshold, subcontracting work, extending the period of performance.

**Single audit threshold.** If your organization spends $1,000,000 or more in federal funds in a fiscal year (for fiscal years beginning on or after October 1, 2024), you're required to undergo a single audit (2 CFR 200 Subpart F). Verify the current threshold at ecfr.gov before applying, as regulatory thresholds can change. This is an annual audit specific to federal funds, separate from your regular financial statement audit.

## Step 2: Set Up Restricted Fund Tracking

The single most important compliance requirement is keeping grant funds separate from unrestricted funds. This doesn't necessarily mean separate bank accounts (though some funders require that). It means your accounting system must track income and expenses by fund or grant at a level of detail that allows you to produce a report showing exactly how much of each grant has been received, spent, and remains.

**What restricted fund tracking looks like:**

Every transaction coded to a grant must include:

- The grant/fund identifier
- The budget category (personnel, supplies, travel, etc.)
- A description that connects the expense to the funded program
- Supporting documentation (receipt, invoice, timesheet, etc.)

**What goes wrong without it:**

- You overspend one grant because you didn't realize expenses were accumulating faster than budgeted
- You charge an expense to the wrong grant (especially common with personnel who work across multiple programs)
- At reporting time, you can't produce a clean financial report because expenses weren't coded correctly throughout the period
- During an audit, you can't demonstrate that restricted funds were used only for their intended purpose

**The spreadsheet breaking point:** Many nonprofits start with a spreadsheet to track grants. This works when you have 1-2 grants with simple budgets. It breaks when you reach 3-5 active grants with different fiscal years, different budget categories, and staff splitting time across programs. The spreadsheet doesn't enforce coding rules, doesn't prevent overspending, and doesn't generate the reports funders expect.

If your organization manages more than $500,000 in grant revenue or more than three active grants simultaneously, dedicated fund accounting software (or a donor management system with fund tracking) will save you more time than it costs. The question isn't whether you can make a spreadsheet work -- it's whether the spreadsheet will survive an audit.

## Step 3: Establish Reporting Cadences

Different funders have different reporting requirements. The first thing to do after receiving an award is calendar every deadline.

**Federal grants (pass-through and direct):**

- SF-425 Federal Financial Report: frequency and due dates vary by agency and award, check your Notice of Award for the exact schedule
- Progress/narrative reports: semi-annual or annual, depending on the agency
- Final financial report: due 120 days after the period of performance ends
- Final narrative report: due 90-120 days after the period of performance ends

**State government grants:**

- Vary widely by state and agency
- Often quarterly financial and narrative reports
- May require expenditure reports in the state's specific format
- Deadlines may not align with your fiscal year

**Private foundations:**

- Most require an annual report (financial + narrative)
- Due dates typically align with the anniversary of the grant award, not your fiscal year
- Some require interim reports for multi-year grants
- Format varies: some provide templates, others accept your format

**Corporate foundations/giving programs:**

- Often have simpler reporting requirements
- May accept a one-page summary with financial highlights
- Deadline enforcement varies (some are strict, others flexible)

**Calendar every deadline immediately.** Use whatever system your organization uses for deadlines -- calendar software, project management tools, or even a wall calendar. Set reminders for 30 days before each deadline. Missed report deadlines are one of the top reasons funders decline future applications.

## Step 4: Implement Time Tracking for Grant-Funded Staff

If any staff members are funded partially or fully by a grant, you need time tracking documentation. This is one of the most scrutinized areas in audits.

**What the auditor wants to see:**

For staff funded 100% by one grant:

- A signed certification (at least semi-annually) confirming the employee worked solely on the funded program

For staff splitting time across multiple grants or between grant-funded and non-grant work:

- Time and effort reports showing actual hours worked on each program/grant
- Reports must be signed by the employee and a supervisor
- Reports must reflect after-the-fact actual activity, not budgeted percentages

**What goes wrong:**

- Staff are charged to grants based on the budgeted percentage without documenting actual time worked
- Time reports are completed at the end of a quarter from memory instead of contemporaneously
- A staff member is charged 100% to a grant but actually spends time on other programs
- Time reports don't exist for the first few months of the grant because "we hadn't set up the system yet"

The 2 CFR 200 (Uniform Guidance) standard for federal grants requires that time and effort documentation be maintained for all employees whose salary is charged to a federal award. Many private foundations adopt similar requirements.

## Step 5: Documentation Standards for Every Expense

Every dollar charged to a grant needs a paper trail. The standard is: could a person who doesn't work at your organization look at this documentation and understand what was purchased, why it was needed for the funded program, and that the cost was reasonable?

**For personnel costs:**

- Signed timesheets or effort certifications
- Payroll records showing salary calculations
- Benefit allocation methodology documentation

**For supplies and materials:**

- Original receipts or invoices
- Documentation of competitive pricing (for purchases above your threshold)
- Connection to the funded program (why was this needed?)

**For travel:**

- Pre-approval documentation (if required by your policy or the funder)
- Receipts for all expenses
- Purpose of travel and connection to funded program
- Per diem calculations (if applicable)
- Mileage logs for driving

**For subcontracts and consultants:**

- Signed contract or agreement
- Deliverables documentation
- Invoices with detail sufficient to verify the work was performed
- If federal funds: verification that the subcontractor is not debarred (check SAM.gov)

**For equipment** (items meeting your organization's capitalization threshold, verify the threshold in your award and organizational policy):

- Purchase documentation
- Competitive bidding documentation for purchases above your procurement threshold (the number of required quotes depends on the purchase amount and your award terms, verify requirements in 2 CFR 200.320 and your award)
- Equipment inventory tags and records
- Disposition documentation when the grant ends (federal grants require you to report equipment status)

## Step 6: Monitor Budget vs. Actual Spending

Run a budget-to-actual comparison at least monthly for every active grant. This report shows, for each budget category, how much you planned to spend, how much you've actually spent, and the variance.

**What you're looking for:**

- **Overspending in any category.** If you've spent more than budgeted in a category, you may need funder approval to reallocate.
- **Underspending overall.** If you're significantly behind on spending midway through the grant period, you'll end up returning funds. More importantly, the funder will wonder whether you actually need the money.
- **Spending rate mismatched with time elapsed.** If you're 6 months into a 12-month grant and you've spent 80% of the budget, you'll run out before the period ends. If you've spent 20%, you're behind.

**How often:** Monthly internal review. The financial report to the funder will happen on whatever schedule they require, but your internal monitoring should be monthly at minimum.

## Step 7: Prepare for Audits Before They Happen

If you meet or exceed the federal single audit expenditure threshold (see Step 1 above for the current threshold), you'll have a single audit. Even below that threshold, funders may conduct their own program audits.

**Audit preparation is a year-round activity, not a scramble:**

- Keep your grant files organized by grant, with all documentation filed as transactions occur
- Complete reconciliations monthly
- Maintain a schedule of federal awards showing each grant, CFDA number, award amount, expenditures, and remaining balance
- Document your internal controls: who can approve expenses, who codes transactions, who reconciles, and who signs checks
- Keep your indirect cost rate documentation current

**Common audit findings for mid-sized nonprofits:**

- Missing or incomplete time and effort documentation
- Expenses charged to the wrong grant
- Insufficient documentation for cost allocation methodology
- Missing subcontractor monitoring documentation
- Late financial report submissions to funders

Each of these is preventable with the systems described in this checklist. The pattern is that organizations without structured compliance processes accumulate small errors throughout the year, and those errors become audit findings.

## Step 8: Grant Closeout

When a grant's period of performance ends, you have a set of final obligations:

**Within 30 days:**

- Stop charging expenses to the grant
- Review all pending transactions to ensure they're coded correctly
- Resolve any outstanding invoices or reimbursements

**Within 90-120 days (varies by funder):**

- Submit the final financial report
- Submit the final narrative/progress report
- Return any unspent funds (if required by the grant agreement)
- Inventory and report on any equipment purchased with grant funds

**After closeout:**

- Retain all grant records for the required period. The minimum under 2 CFR 200.334 is 3 years from the date you submit your final expenditure report, but some programs and agencies require longer; verify the retention period that applies to each award
- Keep records accessible -- don't archive them in a way that prevents retrieval if an audit occurs
- Update your internal records to show the grant is closed

## When Your Current System Is Breaking

Here are the warning signs that your compliance infrastructure needs an upgrade:

- Report preparation takes more than two business days per grant
- You've missed a reporting deadline in the past 12 months
- You're spending time re-coding transactions that were entered incorrectly
- Your budget-to-actual reports are produced by manually extracting data from multiple sources
- You can't answer "how much is left in grant X" without pulling a report
- Staff time tracking is done retroactively (from memory) rather than contemporaneously
- Your auditor has issued management letter findings about grant compliance
- You have more than three active grants and your tracking is in spreadsheets

None of these individually means your organization is failing. But they're friction points that compound. Each one increases the probability of a compliance error, and compliance errors have real consequences: returned funds, damaged funder relationships, and audit findings that follow your organization for years.

The goal of compliance infrastructure -- whether it's software, documented procedures, or both -- is to make the right thing easy and the wrong thing hard. When coding an expense to the correct grant takes the same effort as coding it incorrectly, errors are random and rare. When it takes extra effort to do it right, errors are systematic and frequent.
