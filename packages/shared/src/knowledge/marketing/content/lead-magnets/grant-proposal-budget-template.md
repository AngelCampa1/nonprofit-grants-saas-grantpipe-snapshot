## Why Grant Budgets Get Flagged in Proposal Review

A grant proposal budget is not a wish list. It is a financial argument - a claim that the work you're proposing costs what you say it costs, that the costs are reasonable, and that you have the capacity to manage the funds.

Proposal reviewers - whether foundation program officers or federal grant specialists - see hundreds of budgets. The ones that get flagged are almost always the same: indirect costs with no justification, fringe benefit rates that don't match the narrative, personnel costs that don't add up to 100% across positions, and budget categories that suggest the organization doesn't understand allowable costs under the funding mechanism.

The budget template in this guide is structured to prevent those errors. Work through it in the order presented: personnel first, then direct costs, then indirect costs, then the budget justification. Don't write the budget narrative until you have the numbers right - the narrative has to match the numbers exactly.

## Step-by-Step Budget Construction

### Step 1: Identify All Personnel Charged to the Grant

For each staff member (or position) who will be charged to this grant, you need:

- Name or position title
- Annual salary
- Percentage of time devoted to this grant (as a decimal: 50% = 0.5)
- Duration in months
- Fringe benefit rate

If you're creating a position specifically for this grant, use a realistic market rate, not what you hope to pay. Reviewers flag unrealistically low personnel costs because they suggest the organization hasn't thought through what the work actually requires.

### Step 2: Calculate Fringe Benefits

Fringe benefits (also called employee benefits or indirect personnel costs) are costs paid on top of salary: FICA (Social Security and Medicare), health insurance, retirement contributions, workers' compensation, and unemployment insurance.

Your organization should have an approved fringe benefit rate. If you don't have a formally calculated rate, compute it: divide total annual fringe costs by total annual salaries. This is your rate.

**Typical fringe benefit ranges:**
- Part-time employees (no benefits): 8-12% (FICA only)
- Full-time employees with basic benefits: 20-28%
- Full-time employees with comprehensive benefits (health, dental, vision, retirement): 28-38%

For federal grants, you may be required to document your fringe benefit calculation. Maintain a fringe benefit rate calculation worksheet and update it annually.

### Step 3: Identify Direct Costs

Direct costs are costs that can be specifically attributed to the funded program. Common categories:

- Supplies and materials
- Equipment (typically items over $10,000 per unit are classified as equipment, not supplies)
- Travel (mileage, airfare, lodging, per diem)
- Contractual/consultants
- Participant costs (stipends, transportation for participants, if applicable)
- Other direct costs

For each direct cost, you will need to justify it in the budget narrative. Don't include costs you can't justify - reviewers will ask.

### Step 4: Calculate Indirect Costs

Indirect costs (also called facilities and administrative costs, or F&A costs) are organizational overhead that cannot be attributed directly to a single program: executive leadership, accounting, IT infrastructure, rent, utilities, etc.

**Option 1: Negotiated Indirect Cost Rate (NICRA)**
If your organization has a negotiated rate with a federal agency, use that rate. Apply it to the Modified Total Direct Costs (MTDC) base - which typically excludes equipment, capital expenditures, patient care costs, tuition remission, and subaward costs above $50,000.

**Option 2: De Minimis Rate (15% of MTDC)**
Organizations that have never had a negotiated rate may use the de minimis 15% rate on MTDC, as authorized under 2 CFR 200.414(f). This is available for federal grants and many private foundations accept it. You don't need to apply for it - simply document that you're using the de minimis rate.

**Option 3: No Indirect Costs**
Some private foundations do not allow indirect costs. In that case, you cannot charge them. Do not bury indirect costs in inflated direct cost line items - this is a compliance violation.

## Budget Justification Sample Language

The budget justification (also called the budget narrative) must explain every line item: what it is, why it's needed for the funded program, and why the cost is reasonable. Write one paragraph per major budget category.

### Personnel Justification - Sample Language

**[Position 1 - e.g., Program Coordinator, 0.75 FTE]:**
"The Program Coordinator will devote 75% of their time to the funded project, providing direct program coordination, participant scheduling, data collection, and reporting. The annual salary of $[AMOUNT] reflects [ORGANIZATION NAME]'s current pay scale for this position, which was last reviewed in [DATE]. Fringe benefits are calculated at [__]%, which reflects our organization's actual fringe benefit rate as of [DATE], including FICA, health insurance, dental, and retirement contributions."

### Supplies and Materials Justification - Sample Language

"Supplies costs of $[AMOUNT] include curriculum materials ($[AMOUNT] for [NUMBER] participant workbooks at $[UNIT COST] each), office supplies for program staff ($[AMOUNT] annually), and program supplies including [list items] ($[AMOUNT]). All costs are based on current vendor pricing."

### Travel Justification - Sample Language

"Travel costs of $[AMOUNT] include local mileage for program staff traveling between program sites and participant homes (estimated [NUMBER] trips - [MILES] miles - [CURRENT IRS RATE] per mile = $[AMOUNT]) and travel to the [NAME] annual conference for the Program Director ($[AMOUNT] for registration, airfare, and two nights lodging, per organizational travel policy)."

### Consultant Justification - Sample Language

"Consultant costs of $[AMOUNT] cover the engagement of [CONSULTANT NAME OR TYPE] to provide [SCOPE - e.g., evaluation services]. The consultant will conduct [DELIVERABLES] at a rate of $[HOURLY RATE] per hour for an estimated [HOURS] hours. This rate reflects current market rates for [TYPE OF EXPERTISE] in [REGION/FIELD]."

### Indirect Cost Justification - Sample Language

**Using de minimis rate:**
"Indirect costs are calculated at the de minimis rate of 15% of Modified Total Direct Costs (MTDC) of $[MTDC], totaling $[INDIRECT AMOUNT], as authorized under 2 CFR 200.414(f). [ORGANIZATION NAME] has not established a negotiated indirect cost rate agreement with a federal agency."

**Using negotiated rate:**
"Indirect costs are calculated at [ORGANIZATION NAME]'s negotiated indirect cost rate of [RATE]%, established through a rate agreement with [FEDERAL AGENCY] dated [DATE]. The rate applies to the Modified Total Direct Cost base of $[MTDC], for a total indirect cost of $[AMOUNT]."

## Common Budget Errors That Get Flagged in Proposal Review

**1. No indirect cost justification.** If you're charging indirect costs, the budget narrative must explain how the rate was derived and what it covers. "Indirect costs at 20%" with no explanation is a common flag.

**2. Personnel percentages don't sum to 100% for full-time positions.** If a staff member is charged at 60% to this grant and 40% to another source, both must be documented. Reviewers notice when a full-time position is funded at 60% with no explanation of the other 40%.

**3. Fringe benefit rate not documented.** Your fringe rate should be consistent with your actual costs. If your rate differs significantly from what reviewers expect (e.g., 45% when 25% is typical), it will raise questions.

**4. Equipment misclassified as supplies.** Equipment is generally any item at or above the applicable capitalization threshold, often $10,000 per unit under current federal rules or a lower organizational threshold, with a useful life of more than one year. Federal grants have specific equipment rules (competitive procurement, tagging, disposition). Don't misclassify equipment as supplies to avoid those rules.

**5. Budget and narrative don't match.** The budget table shows $12,000 for travel; the narrative says $10,000. These must match exactly. Work from the numbers, then write the narrative.

**6. Costs not allowable under the program.** Read the solicitation and the applicable cost principles (2 CFR 200 Subpart E for federal grants). Some costs are categorically unallowable: lobbying, entertainment, fines, costs incurred before the grant period. Others require prior approval: foreign travel, equipment purchases, construction.

## Handling Budget Modifications Mid-Grant

Even well-constructed budgets need adjustment during the grant period. The rules for budget modifications vary by funder:

**Federal grants (2 CFR 200):** Generally, you may reallocate up to 10% of the total award between budget categories without prior approval, unless the grant agreement specifies otherwise. Changes that require prior approval include: adding or changing key personnel, transferring funds to or from the indirect cost category, changing the scope of work, and no-cost extensions.

**Private foundations:** Prior approval requirements vary widely. Some foundations allow complete discretion; others require approval for any deviation from the approved budget. Read the grant agreement carefully - and if it's silent on modifications, ask the program officer before you make changes.

**Documentation:** For any budget modification, document it in your grant file. Note what changed, why, what approval (if any) was required and obtained, and when the change was made. This documentation protects you if the modification is questioned later.
