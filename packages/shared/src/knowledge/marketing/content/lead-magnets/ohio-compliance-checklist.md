## Why Ohio Compliance Trips Up Growing Nonprofits

Ohio is one of the more accessible states for charitable registration, but it punishes inattention. The thresholds shift the filing posture every time a campaign succeeds, an inheritance arrives, or a new revenue stream comes online. Organizations that registered once and forgot tend to discover the omission only when an auditor or a funder requests proof of current standing.

The Charitable Law Section inside the Ohio Attorney General's office runs registration and supervision. It enforces Ohio Revised Code Chapter 1716 (the Charitable Organizations Act) and Chapter 109 (the Attorney General's general charitable trust authority). Its enforcement tools are real: cease-and-desist letters, late-fee assessments, and civil penalties for solicitation without registration.

This checklist works through the Ohio compliance lifecycle in the order most organizations encounter it.

## Step 1: Confirm Whether You Need to Register

Most nonprofits soliciting in Ohio must register. The relevant questions are narrower:

- Are you a 501(c)(3) - or a 501(c)(4), (c)(6), or other exempt entity that holds itself out as charitable? Both groups can be subject to the registration requirement when they solicit charitable contributions.
- Do you solicit Ohio residents? Mail, phone, email, social media, an online donate button, a board member's personal ask - all of these count.
- Do you exceed the small-charity threshold? Charities with under $5,000 in gross contributions and fewer than ten contributors in a fiscal year are generally exempt.
- Are you a religious organization within the narrow Section 1716.03 carve-out? Many organizations think they qualify and do not.

Document the determination. If you exempt out, save the analysis. The default assumption inside the Charitable Law Section is that solicitation triggers registration.

## Step 2: Initial Registration

The Charitable Law Section runs registration through its online portal. Plan on collecting these items before you start:

- IRS determination letter granting 501(c)(3) status (or other exemption)
- Articles of incorporation as filed with the Ohio Secretary of State
- Bylaws
- Most recent IRS Form 990, 990-EZ, or 990-N
- List of officers, directors, and key staff
- Description of programs and the geographic scope of solicitation
- Federal Employer Identification Number (EIN)
- Bank and accounting contact information for the registration record

Initial registration must occur before any solicitation. Organizations that solicit first and register later face late fees and may be ordered to suspend fundraising.

## Step 3: Calendar the Annual Financial Report

The annual report is due four months and fifteen days after the fiscal year ends. For calendar-year filers, that is May 15. The report draws financial information from the same Form 990 you file with the IRS, so the practical sequence is:

1. Close the books.
2. File or finalize Form 990 with the IRS.
3. Submit the Ohio annual financial report through the Charitable Law Section portal.
4. Pay the tier-based annual fee.

A six-month extension is available on request, but only before the original deadline passes. After the deadline, the late fee accrues regardless of whether you eventually file.

## Step 4: Monitor the Audit Threshold

Ohio's audit trigger is $500,000 in contributions during the fiscal year. Above that line, the annual report must include an audited financial statement prepared by an independent CPA, in accordance with generally accepted auditing standards.

What this means in practice:

- The first year you cross $500,000 in contributions, you need an audit. CPAs book Q1-Q2 audit work months in advance. Engage early.
- "Contributions" for this purpose tracks the Form 990 Part VIII definition. Government grants with required accounting and program service revenue generally do not count toward the threshold, but read the statute carefully if your revenue mix is unusual.
- An audit is not the same as a financial review or a compilation. The Charitable Law Section requires a full audit at the threshold.

Below $500,000, the report still requires Form 990-series financial data but does not require audited statements.

## Step 5: Maintain Solicitation Records

Ohio expects registered charities to maintain books and records sufficient to verify the contents of their reports for at least three years. This includes:

- Donor records (names, addresses, amounts, dates) at the level of detail used to compile Form 990 Schedule B and the annual report
- Solicitation materials - direct mail pieces, email campaigns, event invitations, web copy
- Contracts with professional fundraisers and fundraising counsel
- Board minutes documenting fundraising approvals and changes in solicitation strategy

Professional fundraisers who solicit on a charity's behalf in Ohio must register separately and file financial reports for each campaign. If a charity hires an outside firm, both the charity and the firm carry compliance obligations.

## Step 6: Update the Registration When Things Change

The Attorney General expects timely updates when material facts change:

- Change in officers, directors, or principal address
- Change in fiscal year
- Change in legal name
- Merger, dissolution, or significant change in scope of activities
- Change in registered agent or service address

These updates can usually be filed through the same portal. Waiting until the next annual report is the wrong approach. Funders, banks, and prospective major donors increasingly check the public registration record, and outdated information is a credibility problem before it becomes a legal one.

## Step 7: Closeout If You Stop Operating in Ohio

Charities that wind down or relocate must file a final report and request termination of registration. Until that filing is accepted, annual report obligations and late fees continue to accrue.

Closeout sequencing:

- File the final IRS Form 990 marking the final return box.
- File a final Ohio annual financial report covering the partial year.
- Submit dissolution documents to the Ohio Secretary of State if the entity is dissolving.
- Request termination of charitable registration in writing through the Charitable Law Section.
- Retain records for at least three years post-closeout, longer if a funder or auditor requires it.

## Common Failure Patterns

The errors that show up most often in Ohio compliance reviews are predictable:

- Registration was filed at incorporation and never renewed.
- Online giving traffic from Ohio residents triggered registration the board never approved.
- Contributions crossed $500,000 without anyone tracking the audit threshold.
- A professional fundraising contract was signed without checking whether the firm was registered with the AG.
- Fiscal-year change at the federal level was never reflected in the Ohio record.
- A merger went through with the Secretary of State but the surviving entity did not update charitable registration.

Each of these is preventable with a quarterly compliance review and a single owner - typically the Development Director or CFO - who carries the calendar.

## Building Compliance Into the Operating Cadence

The pattern that works is straightforward: treat charitable registration like payroll tax. It happens on a fixed cadence regardless of what else is going on. The annual report is on the calendar. The fee is in the budget. The audit, when triggered, is engaged before the books close.

Organizations that approach compliance reactively spend more, miss more deadlines, and accumulate late fees that eventually exceed the cost of doing it right. The Ohio Attorney General's portal is well documented, the statute is readable, and the thresholds rarely change. Once the cadence is in place, the work is small.
