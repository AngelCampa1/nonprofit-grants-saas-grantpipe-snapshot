## Why Cost Allocation Matters

Most nonprofits run multiple programs with shared costs: rent, utilities, finance staff, IT, executive director time. When some of those programs are funded by grants, the organization has to decide how much of each shared cost to charge to each grant. The decision is not arbitrary. Federal grants and most foundations require that allocations be reasonable, consistent, and documented.

The Uniform Guidance at [2 CFR 200](/resources/guides/uniform-guidance-2-cfr-200-practical-guide) sets the framework. Section 200.405 requires costs allocable to a federal award to be incurred in proportion to relative benefit. Section 200.412 requires consistent treatment between direct and indirect costs. Section 200.413 requires direct costs identifiable to a specific cost objective. Together they require a written, applied methodology.

This worksheet walks through building a cost allocation plan: classifying costs, choosing bases, calculating allocations, and documenting the methodology. The result is a plan that survives single audit scrutiny and gives funders confidence in your numbers.

## Section 1: Classify Costs

Before allocating anything, classify every cost into one of three categories.

### Direct costs

Costs that can be identified specifically with a particular cost objective (a program, a grant, an activity). Examples:

- A teacher's salary in an after-school program funded by a specific grant
- Supplies purchased for a specific program
- Travel for a specific program activity
- Subcontractor working on one funded project

Direct costs are charged in full to the cost objective they support.

### Shared (indirect) costs

Costs that benefit multiple cost objectives and cannot be readily identified to a single one. Examples:

- Executive Director salary (overseeing multiple programs)
- Finance staff
- Office rent
- Utilities
- IT and software costs
- General administrative supplies

Shared costs are allocated across cost objectives using a defined methodology.

### Unallowable costs

Costs that cannot be charged to federal awards regardless of methodology, per 2 CFR 200 Subpart E. Examples:

- Alcohol
- Lobbying
- Fundraising
- Bad debts
- Entertainment (unless specific exceptions met)

Unallowable costs must be tracked separately and removed from any base used for allocation.

For a deeper review of allowability, see the [allowable costs guide](/resources/guides/allowable-costs-federal-grants).

## Section 2: Define Cost Objectives

A cost objective is anything to which costs are charged. Common cost objectives:

- Each grant or award
- Each program or service line
- Specific projects within a program
- Functional categories (programs, management and general, fundraising) for Form 990 reporting

Map your organization's cost objectives. The cost allocation plan must allocate shared costs to each cost objective consistently.

## Section 3: Choose Allocation Bases

For each pool of shared costs, choose a base that has a causal or beneficial relationship to those costs.

### Common bases and when to use them

- **Total direct labor cost.** Use for costs that vary with personnel intensity (administrative support, HR services).
- **Total direct labor hours.** Similar to direct labor cost; useful when wage rates vary significantly across programs.
- **Square footage.** Use for facility costs (rent, utilities, janitorial). Allocate based on space occupied.
- **Full-time equivalents.** Use for shared services that scale with headcount (IT, payroll processing).
- **Total direct costs.** Use as a general base when no more specific causal relationship exists.
- **Modified total direct costs.** Total direct costs less equipment, capital expenditures, rental costs, tuition remission, scholarships, and the portion of subawards over $50,000. Used when calculating indirect cost rates.

### Selecting bases

- Each cost pool can use a different base.
- The base must be measurable consistently across cost objectives.
- The base must have a logical connection to the costs in the pool.
- Document why each base was chosen.

### Example: Three cost pools, three bases

- **Facilities pool** (rent, utilities, janitorial): allocated by square footage occupied by each program.
- **Administrative support pool** (HR, finance, IT): allocated by FTE count in each program.
- **General administration** (Executive Director, board governance): allocated by total direct costs.

## Section 4: Calculate Allocations

For each cost pool:

1. Sum the total costs in the pool.
2. Sum the base across all cost objectives.
3. Calculate each cost objective's share of the base (cost objective base / total base).
4. Apply the percentage to the cost pool to determine each cost objective's share.

### Example calculation

Facilities pool: $120,000 annual.
Total square footage: 10,000 sq ft.
Program A: 4,000 sq ft (40%) †’ $48,000.
Program B: 3,000 sq ft (30%) †’ $36,000.
Program C: 2,000 sq ft (20%) †’ $24,000.
Management and General: 1,000 sq ft (10%) †’ $12,000.

Repeat for each cost pool. Sum each cost objective's allocation across pools to get total shared cost charged.

## Section 5: Document the Plan

A cost allocation plan is only useful if it is written. Document:

### Plan components

- **Organization overview.** Brief description of programs, funding sources, and cost structure.
- **Cost classification.** Which categories are direct, which are shared, which are unallowable.
- **Cost pools.** Each shared cost pool with included accounts.
- **Allocation bases.** Base for each pool with rationale.
- **Calculation methodology.** Step-by-step calculation flow.
- **Application.** How allocations are applied in the general ledger and on grant reports.
- **Update schedule.** When and how the plan will be reviewed and updated.
- **Approval.** Who approved the plan (typically CFO or ED) and when.

### Worksheet template

For each cost pool, document:

| Field | Example |
|-------|---------|
| Pool name | Facilities |
| Included accounts | Rent, utilities, janitorial, building maintenance |
| Total annual cost | $120,000 |
| Allocation base | Square footage |
| Base measurement source | Floor plan, updated annually |
| Cost objectives | Program A, B, C, Management and General |
| Allocation percentages | A: 40%, B: 30%, C: 20%, M&G: 10% |
| Allocation amounts | A: $48K, B: $36K, C: $24K, M&G: $12K |
| Frequency of allocation | Monthly journal entry |
| Reviewer | CFO, annually |

## Section 6: Apply Consistently

Section 200.412 requires consistent treatment of direct and indirect costs. A cost cannot be charged as a direct cost on one grant and as an indirect cost on another, in similar circumstances.

### Common consistency failures

- Charging the program manager's salary directly to one grant but including it in the indirect pool for another.
- Charging supplies directly when they are purchased in bulk for general use.
- Allocating rent by square footage on one grant and by direct costs on another in the same period.

### How to maintain consistency

- Document the policy: which categories of cost are always direct, which are always shared.
- Train accounting staff on the policy.
- Review charges quarterly for consistency.
- Update the plan rather than making one-off exceptions.

For cost transfer guidance when corrections are needed, see the [cost transfer policy guide](/resources/guides/cost-transfer-policy-nonprofits).

## Section 7: Indirect Cost Rate Decision

Most organizations using cost allocation also need to decide how to handle indirect costs on federal grants.

### Three options

1. **Negotiated indirect cost rate.** Negotiate a rate with your federal cognizant agency. The rate is then applied to a defined base on each federal award.
2. **de minimis 15% rate.** Under 2 CFR 200.414(f), use 15% of Modified Total Direct Costs as the indirect rate without negotiation.
3. **Cost allocation plan only.** Use direct allocation through the cost allocation plan; do not charge indirect rate on top.

The choice depends on organization size, federal funding volume, and complexity of cost structure. See the [cognizant agency guide](/resources/guides/cognizant-agency-for-indirect-costs) for negotiated rate guidance.

## Section 8: Annual Review

Update the plan annually:

- Re-measure allocation bases (square footage, FTEs, direct costs) using actual data.
- Recalculate allocation percentages.
- Update cost pools if accounts have moved.
- Document any changes in methodology.
- Re-approve the updated plan.

Document the review even when nothing changes. Auditors look for evidence the plan is reviewed, not just that it exists.

## Section 9: Audit Defensibility

A cost allocation plan that survives single audit scrutiny demonstrates:

- Written methodology in place before the period being audited
- Methodology reasonable on its face (bases match cost causality)
- Methodology applied consistently across the period
- Calculations supported by source data
- Annual review documented
- Methodology consistent across funding sources (federal, state, foundation)

Most cost allocation findings come from one of three patterns: no written plan, plan inconsistent with actual practice, or plan changed retroactively to fit grant requirements. Each is preventable.

## Common Cost Allocation Failures

- **No written plan.** Allocations made monthly without documented methodology.
- **Plan does not match practice.** Written plan exists, but actual journal entries follow a different logic.
- **Inconsistent treatment.** Same cost type charged direct on one grant and indirect on another.
- **Bases not measurable.** Plan references "use of services" without a measurable basis.
- **Stale data.** Allocation percentages calculated three years ago; never updated.
- **Unallowable costs in the base.** Lobbying or fundraising included in a base used to allocate to federal grants.

The discipline this worksheet enforces is documentation. Most nonprofits already make reasonable allocations informally. The worksheet captures the reasoning so it survives audit and turnover. Cost allocation is not an exotic compliance burden - it is the explicit version of decisions you are already making.
