## Why Funder Stewardship Is Different

Most nonprofits treat funders like donors. They send thank-you letters, post-award acknowledgments, and an annual report. Then they go silent until the renewal application is due. This pattern produces predictable outcomes: declined renewals, surprised program officers, and long gaps between funding cycles.

Institutional funders need different stewardship. Program officers typically manage large grant portfolios. They cannot remember the substance of your work without consistent, structured updates. They want to share your outcomes with their board, write your story into their annual report, and renew confidently. None of that happens without deliberate stewardship.

This calendar template defines a 12-month stewardship rhythm for institutional funders: foundations, corporate funders, and government agencies. It works for active grant relationships and for cultivation of prospective funders. Adjust the cadence by relationship strength and grant size.

For complementary individual donor guidance, see the [donor stewardship plan 12-month guide](/resources/guides/donor-stewardship-plan-12-month-guide).

## Stewardship Cadence by Relationship Type

Different funder relationships need different cadence. Build your calendar around three tiers.

### Tier 1: Active major grant ($50K+ multi-year)

8 to 12 touchpoints per year. Required reports plus quarterly informal updates, in-person visit annually, board briefing, and renewal cultivation timeline.

### Tier 2: Active grant ($10K-$50K)

5 to 8 touchpoints per year. Required reports, mid-year update, annual visit if geography allows, and renewal cultivation.

### Tier 3: Declined or prospective funder

2 to 4 touchpoints per year. Annual report, occasional outcome update, event invitations, and reactivation outreach.

## The 12-Month Calendar

This calendar assumes a calendar-year grant cycle. Adjust for different fiscal years.

### January

**Active grants:**
- Send year-end gratitude note thanking the funder for the prior year's partnership and previewing the year ahead.
- Confirm reporting deadlines for the calendar year are on the team's calendar.

**Prospective funders:**
- Review prospect pipeline; identify funders to cultivate this quarter.

### February

**Active grants:**
- Submit any required Q4 financial or narrative reports.
- Schedule annual in-person visit or video meeting (target: April-June).

**Prospective funders:**
- Send first cultivation outreach: brief organizational update with one specific outcome story.

### March

**Active grants:**
- Mid-year programmatic update: one-page summary of progress against grant objectives. Send to program officer even if not required.

**All funders:**
- Send annual report or year-end impact piece.

### April

**Active major grants:**
- Conduct in-person site visit or video tour. Brief the program officer on:
  - Year-to-date progress
  - Challenges encountered and adaptations made
  - One representative participant story
  - Connection to the funder's strategic priorities
- Document the visit in the CRM with notes for renewal preparation.

**All grants:**
- Submit any Q1 reports.

### May

**Active grants:**
- Internal review: how is the grant tracking against objectives at the 5-month mark? Surface any underperformance early so it can be addressed before reporting.

**Prospective funders:**
- Second cultivation outreach: invite to upcoming event or share a thought-leadership piece.

### June

**Mid-year stewardship across all active grants:**
- Send a standard mid-year update letter to every active funder. Include:
  - Programs supported
  - Year-to-date numbers
  - One short narrative or photo
  - Acknowledgment of partnership

This is one of the highest-leverage touchpoints. It signals stewardship discipline before any required reporting.

### July

**Active grants:**
- Submit Q2 reports as required.
- For grants ending in calendar Q4, begin renewal preparation: review what worked, what did not, and what the next phase looks like.

### August

**Active grants:**
- Schedule renewal conversations with program officers for grants ending in Q4.
- Send specific outcome update if mid-year data is available.

**Prospective funders:**
- Annual cultivation event or invitation if applicable.

### September

**Active grants:**
- Begin drafting renewal proposals for Q4 deadline grants. See the [grant lifecycle guide](/resources/guides/grant-lifecycle-guide).
- Submit Q3 reports as required.

**All funders:**
- If your fiscal year ends June 30, send a fiscal year impact summary.

### October

**Active grants:**
- Submit renewal proposals as needed.
- Send a fall update letter to all active funders with year-to-date highlights.

**Prospective funders:**
- Year-end giving cycle outreach: brief organizational update.

### November

**Active grants:**
- Schedule end-of-year program officer calls where appropriate.
- Begin preparing Q4 and final reports.

**All funders:**
- Thanksgiving stewardship note (optional but well-received).

### December

**Active grants:**
- Submit any year-end reports due before January 1.
- Document the year's stewardship activity in the CRM for each funder.

**All funders:**
- Year-end stewardship outreach.

## Stewardship Components and Templates

Within the calendar, specific stewardship components recur. Build templates for each.

### Required reports

Federal grants typically require quarterly SF-425 financial reports and annual or semi-annual narrative reports. State grants and foundations vary. See the [federal grant reporting requirements guide](/resources/guides/federal-grant-reporting-requirements) for federal context.

Reporting is the minimum, not the maximum. Build reports that exceed the minimum content requirements when feasible - additional outcome data, participant stories, photos.

### Mid-year update letter

A short, standard format used across all active funders mid-year:

> Dear [Program Officer],
>
> I wanted to share a brief update on the work your investment is supporting. As of [date], we have [specific output number] and [specific outcome metric]. [One representative story, two to three sentences].
>
> Looking ahead, we are focused on [next priority]. We expect to share fuller data in our [next required report].
>
> Thank you for the partnership. Please reach out if you have questions or want to discuss in more depth.
>
> [Signature]

### Site visits and program officer meetings

For Tier 1 funders, an annual in-person visit or substantive video meeting. Prepare:

- Brief agenda sent 1 week in advance
- Year-to-date data summary
- One participant story or program demonstration
- Specific questions for the program officer (their priorities, foundation strategy shifts, peer funders)

Send a follow-up note within 48 hours summarizing key points discussed.

### Board briefings

Some foundations request that grantees brief their board on the work. Approach this as cultivation, not just reporting:

- Concrete outcomes
- One or two stories
- Strategic context
- Questions for the board's perspective

A successful board briefing produces additional advocates within the foundation.

### Event invitations

Invite funders to organization events: program graduations, annual meetings, gala, site openings. Many decline due to schedule, but the invitation itself is stewardship.

### Renewal cultivation

For multi-year grants, renewal cultivation should start 9 to 12 months before the current grant ends. The cultivation arc:

- Month 9-12 before end: Schedule renewal conversation. Surface preliminary outcomes.
- Month 6-9 before end: Submit progress data. Discuss next-phase scope.
- Month 3-6 before end: Submit renewal proposal.
- Month 1-3 before end: Wrap up current grant cleanly. Begin next phase smoothly.

Renewals that arrive cold rarely succeed. Renewals that arrive after 9 months of structured cultivation usually do.

## Recording Stewardship in the CRM

Every stewardship touchpoint should be logged. Minimum data:

- Date
- Type (call, visit, letter, email, report)
- Substance (one-line summary)
- Owner
- Next move and date

The CRM stewardship history is the institutional memory of the relationship. Without it, every staff transition resets the relationship to zero.

## Adjustments by Funder Type

### Private foundations

Most flexible. Personal cultivation works. Program officers value substantive, well-prepared engagement.

### Corporate funders

Often value visibility and employee engagement. Stewardship can include:

- Co-branded outreach opportunities
- Volunteer days for corporate employees
- Recognition in your communications

### Government funders

Federal program officers are constrained by procurement rules. Stewardship is more formal: timely reporting, clean compliance, documented program performance. Personal cultivation is limited and must follow agency communication norms.

### Family foundations

Often less staffed and more relational. The board and family members may be more accessible than at large foundations. Calibrate based on relationship.

## Common Stewardship Failures

- **Stewardship goes silent between reports.** Funder hears from you only when required.
- **Reports treated as compliance only.** Minimum content delivered without programmatic substance.
- **No site visit or substantive meeting in the year.** Relationship stays transactional.
- **Renewal arrives cold.** Funder is asked to renew without the cultivation foundation.
- **Program officer change unaddressed.** New officer inherits a thin file and has to learn the relationship from scratch.
- **No CRM record of touchpoints.** Stewardship lives in inboxes; transitions wipe institutional memory.

The calendar's job is to make stewardship a managed function rather than a reactive scramble. Twelve months of consistent, modest touchpoints build the relationship that makes renewals predictable. The work is small each month and compounding over years.
