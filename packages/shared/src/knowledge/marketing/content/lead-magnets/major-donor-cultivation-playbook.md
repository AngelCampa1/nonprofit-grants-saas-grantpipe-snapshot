## What Makes a Major Donor

"Major donor" is a relative term. The threshold depends on your organization's size, donor base, and fundraising goals. A rough framework:

| Annual Budget | Major Gift Threshold | Typical Major Gift Range |
| ------------- | -------------------- | ------------------------ |
| Under $500K   | $1,000+              | $1,000-$10,000           |
| $500K-$2M     | $2,500+              | $2,500-$25,000           |
| $2M-$5M       | $5,000+              | $5,000-$50,000           |
| $5M-$10M      | $10,000+             | $10,000-$100,000+        |

Whatever your threshold, a major donor is someone whose gift requires a different kind of relationship than your general donor pool - one that involves personal contact, cultivation over time, and a solicitation that is tailored to the individual, not a mass appeal.

The economic argument for major donor development is straightforward: at most mid-sized nonprofits, 80% of contributed revenue comes from fewer than 20% of donors. The major gifts program, even if it consists of only 25-50 relationships, often represents the single highest-ROI fundraising activity in the organization's portfolio. The cost of cultivating a relationship that produces a $15,000 gift is dramatically lower than acquiring 100 new donors at $150 each to generate the same revenue.

## The Cultivation Principle

Relationship before solicitation, every time.

This is not a sentiment. It is a practical constraint on when a solicitation will succeed. A donor who receives an ask before they have a meaningful relationship with your organization - before they understand what you do, trust that you can execute it, and feel some personal connection to the mission - will treat the solicitation like any other cold fundraising appeal. The ask lands in the same mental category as a direct mail piece.

A donor who has been thoughtfully cultivated is not evaluating whether to respond to a solicitation. They are deciding whether the amount you are asking for matches what they were already considering. That is a fundamentally different conversation.

The 90-day timeline in this playbook is a minimum framework, not a maximum. Some major gifts take 18 months of cultivation. Significant first-time gifts from prospective donors who are new to your organization may take longer than 90 days to execute well. The timeline accelerates when you have a strong prior relationship (a board member introduction, a referred prospect, or a lapsed donor you are reactivating). It lengthens when you are starting from a cold introduction.

## 90-Day Cultivation Timeline

### Days 1-14: Discovery

**Goal:** Establish first contact, gather intelligence, and schedule an initial meeting. You should leave this phase knowing what the prospect cares about, what their capacity is (rough estimate), and whether they have a prior relationship with your organization or mission area.

**Touchpoint 1 - Prospect research (internal):**

Before reaching out, spend 2-3 hours on research. What do you know about this person? Check your donor database for any giving history, event attendance, or prior contact notes. Check LinkedIn for professional background, board affiliations, and community involvement. Check local news archives for philanthropic activity. You are looking for: connection to your mission area, evidence of philanthropic capacity, and mutual connections who could make an introduction.

**Touchpoint 2 - Introduction outreach:**

If you have a mutual connection (a board member, a prior donor, a staff member with a relationship), ask them to make a warm introduction before your first contact. A warm introduction is worth 6-8 cold contacts in terms of response rate and relationship quality.

If you are reaching out cold, use a brief, direct note that references something specific about the prospect's background and your shared connection to the mission area. Do not ask for money. Do not use your standard fundraising language. Ask for a brief conversation - 20-30 minutes - to share what your organization is doing and get the prospect's perspective. People give their time more readily than their money, and a conversation is a low-stakes first ask.

**Touchpoint 3 - Discovery meeting:**

The goal of this meeting is to listen. Ask questions: What drew them to this issue area? What organizations are they currently supporting? What does meaningful impact look like to them? What are they hoping to accomplish with their philanthropy over the next few years?

Do not pitch. Share what your organization is doing in a way that responds to what you hear from them - not a canned organizational overview. Take notes. You will use this information to calibrate the rest of your cultivation and the eventual ask.

### Days 15-30: Engagement

**Goal:** Move from introduction to meaningful involvement. The prospect should leave this phase with a direct experience of your organization's work - not just a description of it.

**Touchpoint 4 - Facility tour or program observation:**

Invite the prospect to see your work in action. For most organizations, this means a facility tour, a site visit to a program location, or attendance at a program event. The specific format matters less than the principle: first-hand experience of impact is more persuasive than any written material.

If in-person access is not practical, the next best option is a curated conversation with program staff - not leadership, but the people doing the work. The authenticity of a program staff member talking about what they see every day is different from an Executive Director giving a pitch.

**Touchpoint 5 - Impact story, personalized:**

Following the tour or program observation, send a personal note (handwritten or thoughtfully composed email - not a newsletter) that connects what the prospect saw to a specific impact story. Reference something from your discovery conversation: if they mentioned a particular interest in early childhood outcomes, the story should speak to that, not to a general organizational update.

**Touchpoint 6 - Introduce to program staff or beneficiaries:**

If appropriate and ethically comfortable for your program, facilitate a direct introduction between the prospect and someone whose life has been affected by your work. This is not always possible - it depends on your program area and the wishes of the people you serve. When it is possible, it is among the most powerful cultivation tools available.

### Days 31-60: Deepening

**Goal:** Strengthen the relationship through repeated positive contact. The prospect should be thinking about your organization between touchpoints - not just when you reach out.

**Touchpoint 7 - Board member introduction:**

If you have a board member with a natural connection to the prospect (same professional community, mutual acquaintance, similar background), arrange an introduction. A board member expressing personal enthusiasm for the organization's work adds credibility that staff cannot fully provide. It signals that people the prospect respects are personally invested in this work.

Choose the board member carefully. The goal is a genuine connection, not a transactional introduction. A board member who can speak from personal experience about why they volunteer their time and reputation adds more value than a board member who treats the introduction as a duty.

**Touchpoint 8 - Exclusive or insider experience:**

Consider inviting the prospect to an experience that is not available to general supporters: a planning session, a behind-the-scenes event, a briefing on an emerging initiative. This creates a sense of insider relationship - the prospect is learning about something before it is public - which builds both investment and trust.

Examples: an informal dinner with the Executive Director and program leadership, a preview of a new program before it launches, a conversation about a strategic initiative you are considering.

**Touchpoint 9 - Personal correspondence from leadership:**

A handwritten note or personal email from the Executive Director or Board Chair - not acknowledging a gift, just expressing genuine appreciation for the prospect's interest and time - is a disproportionately powerful touchpoint. Most organizations reserve personal correspondence for gift acknowledgments. Receiving it in the middle of a cultivation sequence signals that the relationship itself is valued, not just the money.

### Days 61-75: Preparation

**Goal:** Align internally on the ask amount, the ask timing, and who will make the ask. This phase is largely internal - you are not making additional prospect contact unless there is a natural reason to do so.

**Internal alignment questions:**

- What is the right ask amount? Base it on prospect capacity (informed by research), what you heard during discovery about their giving philosophy and interests, and what you are trying to fund. The ask amount should be aspirational but not unrealistic - the goal is a serious conversation, not a reflexive decline.
- Who should make the ask? The Development Director, the Executive Director, a board member with a strong relationship, or some combination? The person making the ask should be someone with a genuine relationship to the prospect - not the most senior person available.
- What is the ask for? The gift should be connected to something specific - a program, an initiative, an endowment fund - that aligns with what you learned during discovery. Unrestricted gifts are valuable, but most major donors want to know their gift is going toward something meaningful. Come prepared with 1-2 options, not a menu of 10.
- What is the follow-up plan if the answer is "I need to think about it"? Have a clear next step ready: "I'll call you on the 15th to see if you have any questions" is specific. "I'll follow up" is not.

**Prepare a leave-behind:**

A brief written summary of what you are asking for - the program, the impact, the specific funding opportunity - that the prospect can review after the meeting. It should be one page. It should include one compelling story and a clear description of what a gift at the ask level will accomplish. It should not be a brochure.

### Days 76-90: Solicitation

**Goal:** Make the ask in person, manage the response, and establish the follow-up plan.

**The ask meeting:**

Request an in-person meeting. Phone is a last resort; video is acceptable if in-person is truly not possible. Frame the meeting as a conversation about a specific opportunity, not a follow-up conversation.

Open by referencing something from your prior conversations - a program element they reacted to, a question they raised, something you learned about their interests. This signals that you have been listening and that this is a tailored conversation, not a scripted pitch.

Make the ask clearly and specifically: "We are hoping you will consider a gift of $\_\_\_\_\_ to support [specific program/initiative]. Your gift would allow us to [specific, concrete impact]."

Then stop talking.

The silence after an ask is uncomfortable. Resist the urge to fill it. The prospect is processing. Let them. A rushed clarification or backpedaling immediately after the ask dilutes the seriousness of the request.

**Handling "I need to think about it":**

This is the most common response and is not a no. Respond with: "Of course - I'd love to follow up. Would it work if I called you on [specific date]?" Confirm the date and commit to it. Do not leave the meeting without a specific next step.

Some prospects use "I need to think about it" as a gentle decline. You will learn to distinguish these over time. If you suspect it may be a soft no, add: "Is there anything about the program or the gift structure that would be helpful to discuss further?" This gives the prospect an opening to raise concerns without forcing them to say no outright.

**If the answer is yes:**

Thank them sincerely - not effusively. Confirm the details: the amount, the timing, the designation, and how the gift will be made. Provide the leave-behind. Follow up within 48 hours with a written confirmation of what was discussed.

**If the answer is no:**

Thank them for their time and their candor. Ask - only if it feels appropriate - whether there is anything about the timing or the focus area that made this not the right fit. Listen to the answer without arguing. A no today is sometimes a yes in two years if the relationship is maintained respectfully.

## Touchpoint Quality vs. Quantity

Three quality touchpoints beat ten check-ins.

A quality touchpoint is one that advances the relationship - it gives the prospect new information, a new experience, or a reason to think about your organization in a more personal way. A check-in email that says "just following up to see if you have any questions" is not a quality touchpoint. It is noise.

For each touchpoint you plan, ask: what does this touchpoint do for the prospect, not just for your cultivation timeline? If the answer is "it keeps us top of mind," find a better touchpoint. If the answer is "it gives them a direct experience of our impact" or "it introduces them to someone whose work they will find meaningful," it is worth doing.

Quality touchpoints require preparation. A personal note takes 15 minutes to write well. A curated board introduction requires advance conversation with the board member. A program observation visit requires coordinating with program staff. The effort is visible to the prospect in a way that mass communications never are.

## Post-Gift Stewardship: The Ask Is the Beginning

A major gift is not the conclusion of a relationship - it is the beginning of a new phase. Donors who feel well-stewarded after their first major gift give again, give more, and refer other donors. Donors who feel forgotten, or who receive generic annual fund appeals immediately after a major gift, do not.

**First 30 days after a major gift:**

- Written acknowledgment from the Executive Director within 48 hours of receiving the gift (not a form letter - a personal note referencing the specific gift and the relationship)
- Phone call from the person who made the ask, expressing genuine gratitude
- Confirmation of how the gift is designated and what reporting the donor will receive

**First year of stewardship:**

- At least one substantive update on the specific program or initiative funded by the gift - not a newsletter, but a direct communication about what their gift made possible
- At least one in-person interaction, unrelated to another ask
- Inclusion in any insider experiences (briefings, events) that are available to your closest supporters
- Annual report delivered with a personal note, not just mailed generically

**Before the next ask:**

The minimum cultivation period before approaching a major donor for a renewal or increased gift is 12 months from the prior gift. The exception is a specific, time-limited opportunity that aligns clearly with the donor's stated interests and that you are bringing to them as an insider before it is generally available. Even then, the ask should feel like an opportunity, not an obligation.

## Tracking Cultivation in Your CRM vs. Spreadsheet

Major donor cultivation requires longitudinal tracking. The touchpoints in a 90-day cycle, the notes from each conversation, the giving history, the prospect's interests and stated priorities - these need to be accessible to every person who might interact with the donor, including board members who may encounter the prospect at an event.

A spreadsheet can technically hold all of this information. The problems: it is accessible only to whoever has the file, it does not surface reminders for follow-up actions, it is not connected to giving history, and it is difficult to use when managing more than 20-30 active cultivation relationships simultaneously.

A CRM with contact management and activity tracking allows you to log every touchpoint, set follow-up reminders, attach notes from conversations, and see the complete relationship history in one view. The goal is not to replace human judgment with software - it is to make the relationship history visible enough that it can survive staff turnover, inform the next touchpoint, and surface when a relationship is going stale.

If you are tracking cultivation in a spreadsheet, the signal to upgrade is when you have more than 15-20 active major gift prospects and you are losing track of where conversations left off or when follow-up is due. That is the spreadsheet breaking point for cultivation management, just as it is for grant tracking.

## Common Mistakes That Lose the Gift

These are the errors that end cultivation sequences before the ask, or that produce a no when a yes was available.

**1. Asking too soon.**

The most common failure in major donor cultivation. An organization meets a prospect at a gala, has one follow-up conversation, and sends a $10,000 ask six weeks later. The prospect declines - not because they were unwilling to give, but because they had not yet formed a relationship with the organization that made a $10,000 commitment feel reasonable. Cultivation earns the right to ask. Skipping it produces rejection rates that experienced fundraisers recognize as entirely avoidable.

**2. Asking by email instead of in person.**

A major gift ask made by email signals that the organization does not believe the gift is significant enough to warrant a real conversation. Most donors read this correctly. In-person is the standard for asks above your major gift threshold. Phone or video is acceptable when in-person is genuinely impractical. Email is for confirming logistics - not for making a significant financial request of someone you are asking to trust your organization with their philanthropy.

**3. Not including a board member when the prospect has a natural connection.**

Board member involvement in major gift cultivation is a credibility multiplier. A board member who can say "I volunteer my time and my own giving to this organization because I believe in what they do" communicates something that staff cannot. If a board member has a genuine relationship with the prospect - not a transactional introduction, but a real connection - failing to involve them is a missed opportunity that costs real gifts.

**4. Failing to steward the first gift.**

First major gifts test whether the organization knows how to hold a relationship. Donors who receive a generic acknowledgment letter, then hear nothing for six months, then receive an annual fund appeal, draw an accurate conclusion: the organization wanted their money, not their relationship. A first major gift should trigger the full stewardship sequence - personal acknowledgment, impact report, in-person touchpoint, and a next contact that is not an ask. The renewal, if done well, is almost unnecessary to solicit.

**5. Letting the cultivation go quiet after a soft no.**

"I need to think about it" is not a final answer, but it expires. A prospect who said "let me think about it" in March and has not heard from the organization in September has correctly concluded that the organization is not serious about the relationship. If a cultivation goes quiet after an inconclusive ask, reactivate it with a quality touchpoint - a program update, a relevant news item, a personal note - before attempting another ask. Going quiet and then re-emerging only when you need something is the pattern that converts a soft no into a permanent one.
