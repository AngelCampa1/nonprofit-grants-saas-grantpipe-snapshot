## Why Prospect Research Saves More Time Than Writing Faster

The most expensive grant writing is the proposal that never had a chance. Every grant writer can name a 40-hour proposal that was rejected because the funder does not actually support that type of program, that geography, or organizations of that size. The information was findable in 30 minutes before the writing started.

The skill that distinguishes mature grants programs is not faster writing. It is faster filtering. They run prospects through a structured screen before drafting. Prospects that fail the screen do not get a proposal, even if a board member is excited or the program officer was friendly at a conference. The discipline is hard, but it concentrates writing time on opportunities where the probability of funding is real.

This template provides the screening structure. It works for private foundations, federal programs, state programs, and corporate funders. The fields are slightly different for each funder type, but the logic is the same: fit, capacity, requirements, competition, and pipeline scoring.

For broader context on the grant lifecycle, see the [grant lifecycle guide](/resources/guides/grant-lifecycle-guide).

## Stage 1: Initial Screen (15-20 minutes)

The initial screen kills most prospects fast. Five questions:

1. **Mission alignment.** Is your program in the funder's stated priority areas?
2. **Geographic fit.** Does the funder give in your geography?
3. **Organization type.** Does the funder fund organizations of your type and size?
4. **Giving size.** Are typical grant sizes meaningful for your needs?
5. **Application status.** Are they currently accepting applications?

If any answer is "no" or "unclear after 10 minutes of looking," the prospect fails the screen. Move on.

### Where to look in 15 minutes

- Funder website: priorities, geography, grant types, grant ranges, how to apply
- Most recent annual report: tone, recent grantees
- Most recent 990 (Schedule I): list of grants made

Foundation 990s are public. Pull them from Candid or ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer. Schedule I shows every grant of $5,000 or more with recipient name, amount, and purpose. This is the most reliable source of funder behavior data - far more accurate than the website.

## Stage 2: Deep Fit Research (1-2 hours)

If the prospect passes the initial screen, move to deep research.

### Funder priorities

Read the funder's stated priorities carefully. Note specific language they use. A funder that says they support "youth development" may mean after-school programs, mentorship, or college readiness. Match your language to theirs in any future proposal.

### Recent giving pattern

From the last two 990s, capture:

- Total grants paid (annual)
- Number of grants
- Median and average grant size
- Geographic distribution
- Subject area distribution

Build a profile: "This funder makes 45 grants per year, median $35,000, primarily in [region], 70 percent for [topic]."

### Funded peer organizations

Identify 3 to 5 organizations similar to yours that the funder has supported. Look for:

- Mission similarity
- Budget size similarity
- Geographic overlap or analogue

If you cannot find peers, the funder may not fund organizations like yours regardless of stated priorities.

### Renewal and continuation pattern

Some funders fund the same organizations year after year (low new-grantee turnover). Others rotate (high new-grantee turnover). Compare grantee lists across two 990s. If 80 percent are the same organizations both years, breaking in is hard. If 30 percent are new, the funder is more accessible.

### Program officer or staff structure

Identify who handles your subject area or geography. Note their professional background. Check whether they have public talks, articles, or social media that reveal their thinking.

## Stage 3: Application Requirements

Before committing to apply, document exactly what the application requires.

### Required documents

- Letter of inquiry or full proposal?
- Page or word limits
- Budget format
- Audited financials (most recent fiscal year)
- IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter
- Board roster
- Logic model or theory of change
- Letters of support
- Evaluation plan
- Sustainability plan

### Format requirements

- Online portal vs PDF upload vs email
- Specific font, margin, or page size requirements
- Cover page format
- Specific section headings required

### Timeline

- Letter of inquiry deadline (if applicable)
- Full proposal deadline
- Board review schedule
- Notification timing
- Grant period start
- Reporting requirements

If the timeline does not match your need, pause. A grant that arrives 9 months after you needed it may not solve the problem.

## Stage 4: Federal Program Research

Federal opportunities require a different research approach.

### Where to look

- Grants.gov for all current funding opportunities
- SAM.gov for entity registration
- Agency websites for program-specific guidance
- USAspending.gov for historical award data
- The Federal Audit Clearinghouse for single audit data on prior recipients

### Key data points

- CFDA number (Catalog of Federal Domestic Assistance)
- Authorizing legislation
- Funding agency and program office
- Total funding available and number of expected awards
- Award size range
- Match requirements (cash or in-kind)
- Period of performance
- Allowable cost rules under [2 CFR 200](/resources/guides/uniform-guidance-2-cfr-200-practical-guide)
- Single audit threshold ($1,000,000 in federal expenditures triggers single audit)

### Recent awardees

USAspending.gov shows every prior award under a CFDA number. Look at:

- Geographic distribution of awards
- Organization type (nonprofit, government, university)
- Award amounts
- Repeat awardees

If your organization type or geography is underrepresented, the program may have implicit preferences.

### Capacity assessment

Federal grants have substantial compliance burden. Before applying, confirm you can meet:

- Single audit if threshold triggered
- Time and effort reporting
- Subrecipient monitoring (if you pass funds through)
- Procurement standards under 2 CFR 200.317-.327
- Indirect cost rate documentation

For organizations new to federal grants, see the [grant management best practices guide](/resources/guides/grant-management-best-practices).

## Stage 5: Corporate Funder Research

Corporate funders behave differently from foundations.

### Two types

1. **Corporate foundation:** Organized as a separate 501(c)(3). Behaves more like a private foundation, with stated priorities and an application process. 990 data available.
2. **Corporate giving program:** Direct corporate giving without a separate foundation. Often opaque, relationship-driven, and tied to marketing or HR objectives.

### What to research

- Stated CSR (corporate social responsibility) priorities
- Geographic footprint (offices, employees)
- Employee giving and matching gift programs
- Volunteer engagement programs
- Sponsorship history
- Recent press releases about giving

Corporate funders often value visibility and employee engagement opportunities more than program impact alone. Tailor proposals accordingly.

## Stage 6: Pipeline Scoring

After research, score the prospect on a 1-5 scale across five dimensions:

| Dimension | 1 | 3 | 5 |
|-----------|---|---|---|
| Mission alignment | Vague or partial | Clear topic match | Direct stated priority |
| Geographic fit | Outside footprint | National funder, your area eligible | Stated focus on your area |
| Size match | Grants smaller or larger than need | Range overlaps your need | Median grant matches need |
| Application accessibility | Invitation only or closed | Open, complex application | Open, simple LOI process |
| Competition | High demand, low new grantee rate | Moderate competition | Low competition, growing program |

Sum the scores. Use the total to prioritize:

- **20-25:** High-priority pursuit. Move to LOI or proposal.
- **15-19:** Medium-priority. Add to pipeline; pursue when capacity allows.
- **Below 15:** Low-priority. Park unless something changes.

## Stage 7: Recording and Follow-Up

Every researched prospect goes into your grants pipeline with the following data:

- Funder name and tax ID
- Researcher and research date
- Pipeline score and reasoning
- Stated priorities
- Recent grant size range
- Application requirements summary
- Next action and date
- Owner

Re-research priority funders every 12 to 18 months. Funder priorities shift, program officers change, and grant ranges drift.

## Common Prospect Research Failures

- **Skipping the 990.** Relying only on the website and missing the actual giving pattern.
- **Researching without scoring.** Producing pages of notes that do not lead to a yes/no decision.
- **Pursuing every prospect that says yes.** Mission alignment without fit on size, geography, or accessibility wastes time.
- **No record of research.** Repeating research because the previous research is unfindable.
- **Confusing relationship with fit.** A board member's friend at a foundation does not change the foundation's priorities.

The discipline this template enforces is simple: decide before you write. Most rejected proposals were predictable rejections. The hour you spend on the screen saves the 40 hours you would spend on a proposal that never had a chance.
