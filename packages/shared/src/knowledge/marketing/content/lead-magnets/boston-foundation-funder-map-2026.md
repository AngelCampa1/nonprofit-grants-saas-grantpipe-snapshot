## Why This Map Exists

Greater Boston foundation prospecting works best when the team verifies current 990-PF giving, intake mode, and program-fit evidence before drafting. A few major funders have recognizable patterns, but the useful work is documenting which pattern applies to each prospect this year.

The most expensive mistake mid-sized Boston nonprofits make is treating these funders as if they were interchangeable. They are not. Cummings will fund a $30K general operating grant for a Lawrence-based food pantry that Barr would never consider, and Barr will fund a $250K multi-year climate initiative that Cummings is structurally not built to support. Confusing the two costs you cycles you cannot get back.

This map fixes the confusion at the source. Every entry pairs the funder's stated priorities with what the 990-PF actually shows the funder paid out, surfaces the grant size and geography mismatches that kill LOIs before they reach a program officer, and notes the intake mode (open application, rolling LOI, invitation-only) so your prospect list reflects who can actually be approached.

The map includes 25 Greater Boston-area funders ranked by 2024 distributions: Barr Foundation, Klarman Family Foundation, The Boston Foundation, Eastern Bank Charitable Foundation, Cummings Foundation, Yawkey Foundations, Bennett Family Foundation, Liberty Mutual Foundation, State Street Foundation, Highland Street Foundation, Linde Family Foundation, Amelia Peabody Foundation, Paul and Edith Babson Foundation, the Boston Bruins Foundation, Frances R. Dewing Foundation, Riley Foundation, Hyams Foundation, Cabot Family Charitable Trust, Beveridge Family Foundation, Boston Children's Hospital Trust, Schrafft Charitable Trust, Foundation for MetroWest, Cambridge Community Foundation, the Devonshire Foundation, and the Charlestown Foundation.

For each funder the map shows median grant size and range, typical grant count per cycle, the program areas funded over the last three fiscal years (not the priorities page), the application intake mode, the realistic time-to-decision, and any flags around program-officer turnover or strategic shifts.

## Who This Is For

Development directors and executive directors at Greater Boston nonprofits with operating budgets between $500K and $10 million, particularly those in human services, education, arts and culture, health, and environment. Especially useful for organizations whose foundation revenue mix is overweighted toward one or two anchor funders and that need to diversify against concentration risk, and for organizations that have been declined by Barr or the Boston Foundation and need to understand why before re-applying.

The map is less useful for very small organizations (under $250K budget) whose prospect universe is local family foundations not covered here, and for very large institutions ($25M+) whose major-gift relationships are managed through development officers rather than open application cycles.

## How to Use This Map

Open it next to your current Boston-area prospect list. Cross out the funders where the median grant size, geographic focus, or program area no longer matches your work. The crossed-out names get removed from your tracking. The remaining names get a 24-month giving pull from ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer or Candid before any LOI is drafted. Either the pull confirms the match or it surfaces a gap that would have killed the LOI anyway - and either answer saves time.

Most organizations cut their tracked Boston prospect list by 30-50% after one pass with this map, and reallocate that capacity to deeper relationship work with the funders that remain.

## Common Failure Modes This Map Surfaces

Greater Boston foundations have several patterns that catch newer development staff:

- Barr's program areas have shifted twice in the last six years; the website is current but historical decline patterns reflect older priorities. The map flags this so you do not over-weight Barr's older-cycle behavior.
- Cummings' open application is geographically restricted to specific Massachusetts counties; organizations outside that footprint waste capacity preparing applications they cannot submit.
- The Boston Foundation manages roughly 1,000 donor-advised funds and the public grants list mixes discretionary and DAF-directed grants. The map separates the two so your read of the foundation's actual priorities is not contaminated by donor-driven distributions.
- Klarman's invitation-only intake means LOIs sent through generic contact channels almost always go unread. The map suggests how to route relationship requests through existing grantees instead.
- Several family foundations on the list have program-officer turnover patterns that reset relationships every 18-24 months; the map flags those so you do not invest in a relationship that will be handed off before your proposal is reviewed.
