## What Separates a Grants Module from a Compliance System

Every major nonprofit CRM has added grant tracking in the last five years. Most of them track the same basic fields: funder name, award amount, application deadline, award status, grant contact.

None of those fields constitute a compliance system.

Grant compliance begins after the award. It requires tracking restricted fund balances as expenditures accumulate - not just recording an award amount. It requires a federal financial reporting workflow, not just a deadline reminder. It requires documentation of how personnel costs were allocated to the award, not just a note that staff are funded by the grant. It requires subrecipient monitoring records, not just a list of partner organizations.

The platforms on this list were evaluated on that distinction. How deep does the compliance infrastructure actually go?

## The Evaluation Framework

Five questions separate grant compliance tools from grant tracking tools:

**Where are restricted balances visible?** Can the development director see the available balance remaining in each budget category for each active grant without opening a spreadsheet?

**How is documentation attached to spending?** Does every expenditure charged to the grant have a documentation path that connects it to a source document - invoice, receipt, payroll record?

**Who sees reporting deadlines before they become urgent?** Is the SF-425 due date visible to finance before the week it is due, with the data required to prepare it already organized?

**Can finance and development explain the same grant status without translation?** Or does finance see one version in the accounting system and development see another in the CRM?

**What still depends on a spreadsheet owner after implementation?** This is the most revealing question. In most systems, the answer is still "a lot."

## Why Configurable Platforms Are High-Risk for Compliance

Salesforce Nonprofit is genuinely capable of supporting federal grant compliance - if it is configured correctly, maintained consistently, and supported by staff with Salesforce expertise. That is a conditional statement with three failure points.

Configuration correctness: compliance workflows that are built incorrectly may seem to work until an auditor finds the gap. Unlike an accounting error that surfaces in a reconciliation, a compliance documentation failure may not surface until fieldwork begins.

Maintenance consistency: every time federal compliance requirements change - new OMB guidance, agency-specific requirements, updated Compliance Supplement guidance - the configuration needs to be updated. In enterprise institutions, this is a platform administrator's job. In a mid-sized nonprofit, it often does not happen.

Salesforce expertise: small and mid-market nonprofits that have Salesforce licenses often have them because a board member donated them or they followed a consultant's recommendation. Sustained platform expertise is a different matter.

The organizations that succeed with Salesforce for compliance are those that deliberately built that expertise. It is a meaningful investment that the right organizations should make. For the majority of mid-sized nonprofits, the investment required exceeds what the problem warrants.

## The Purpose-Built Case

GrantPipe exists at the intersection of two problems that mid-sized nonprofits face simultaneously: managing donor relationships and managing the compliance obligations on the grants they receive.

Most platforms solve one of those problems. Donor CRMs like Neon, Bloomerang, and Little Green Light solve the donor relationship problem. Grant compliance tools that exist tend to be either enterprise platforms requiring implementation (SmartSimple, Fluxx) or accounting-adjacent systems that handle funds at the ledger level without the development workflow layer.
