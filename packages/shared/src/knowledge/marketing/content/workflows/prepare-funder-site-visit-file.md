A funder site visit goes better when staff can answer from one file. The file should show what the grant promised, what the team has done, where the budget stands, which records support the work, and what issues need an honest update.

This workflow is for a planned funder visit, program officer check-in, monitoring visit, or renewal conversation. It is not about staging a performance. It is about helping staff give clear, accurate answers.

## Step 1: confirm the visit purpose

Ask the funder what they want to cover. The visit may focus on program progress, spending, participants, outcomes, compliance, relationship building, renewal, or a specific concern.

Confirm the date, format, attendees, agenda, documents requested, and whether the visit includes a facility walk-through or participant records review. If the funder sends an agenda, save it in the file.

Do not guess the purpose. A renewal visit needs a different packet than a monitoring visit.

## Step 2: assign an owner

One person should own the site visit file. That person does not have to answer every question. They do need to make sure the file is complete and staff know where to find it.

The grants manager often owns the file. Program staff own service evidence. Finance owns budget status. Leadership owns relationship context and strategic questions.

GrantPipe can help keep the grant record, documents, budget, tasks, and notes together, which makes the site visit file easier to assemble.

## Step 3: collect award terms

Start with the signed award, amendments, approved budget, reporting requirements, performance measures, special conditions, and funder contacts.

Pull out the terms staff are likely to discuss. These may include target population, service area, deliverables, match, restricted uses, reporting dates, spending limits, and closeout duties.

Do not bring a giant contract and expect staff to find answers during the visit. Prepare a short reference sheet.

## Step 4: review recent reports

Add the most recent narrative report, financial report, outcome report, and submission receipt. If the funder asked follow-up questions, include the answers.

Compare what the team reported to what staff plan to say during the visit. Inconsistent language creates confusion. If a number changed after the report, be ready to explain why.

For federal or government grants, keep performance and financial monitoring records in mind. Reports are often part of the evidence trail.

## Step 5: prepare budget status

Finance should prepare a current budget to actual summary. It should show approved budget, actual spending, encumbrances if used, remaining balance, reimbursement status, and any expected budget changes.

Also note cash timing. If reimbursement lag is affecting work, leadership may need to discuss it carefully with the funder.

Do not hide budget problems. Prepare a clear explanation and a proposed next step. Funders may be more open to a fix when the team names the issue early.

## Step 6: gather program evidence

Program evidence should match the grant's promises. Examples include attendance records, case notes, service logs, training rosters, outcome summaries, partner records, photos when appropriate, and client feedback if allowed.

Use privacy rules. Do not put sensitive participant records in a general visit packet. If the funder is allowed to review private records, prepare a controlled review process.

Evidence should be specific enough to support progress without exposing information the funder does not need.

## Step 7: prepare staff roles

Create a simple staff agenda. Name who will welcome the funder, who will describe the program, who will answer budget questions, who will handle data questions, and who will take notes.

Do a short prep meeting. Review likely questions, current risks, recent wins, budget status, and any terms staff should avoid overstating.

Plain, accurate answers are better than polished answers that stretch the facts.

## Step 8: list open issues

Every active grant has some friction. List open issues before the visit. Examples include hiring delays, lower enrollment, spending delays, changed vendor timing, pending reports, or data cleanup.

For each issue, prepare the fact, cause, effect, and next step. That structure keeps the conversation useful.

If the issue may require funder approval, bring the approval path and draft request.

## Step 9: run the visit from the file

During the visit, use the file as a reference. Take notes on questions, funder feedback, promised follow-up, concerns, and renewal signals.

Do not promise a document on the spot unless someone owns it. Write the owner and due date before the meeting ends.

If the funder asks for something sensitive, pause and confirm privacy, grant, and legal rules before sending it.

## Step 10: close the loop

After the visit, send thanks, confirm follow-up items, assign owners, and save the visit notes. Update the grant record with funder feedback and any new risk or renewal guidance.

The finished workflow should produce a site visit file, staff agenda, budget packet, program evidence packet, and follow-up tracker.

Common failures include preparing only program stories, leaving finance out, using stale reports, and failing to track promised follow-up. A good file prevents those problems by putting facts, evidence, and owners in one place.
