steps:
  - title: "Audit current fund mix and restricted vs. unrestricted revenue"
    content: "Pull the last three years of financial statements and build a table showing each revenue source, its amount, its restriction status (unrestricted, temporarily restricted, permanently restricted), and its renewal probability (confirmed, likely, uncertain, one-time). The finance director or ED produces this table before any planning conversations begin. The output is a funding map that shows what revenue the organization can actually count on - the foundation every subsequent planning decision is built on."
  - title: "Conduct board and leadership environmental scan (one-day session)"
    content: "Convene the board and senior leadership for a structured one-day session. The agenda has four parts: external trends affecting the mission field, peer organization moves, funder priority shifts, and organizational strengths and gaps. The ED facilitates or brings an external facilitator; the output is a documented set of observations, not a list of aspirational priorities. Everything in this session should be grounded in evidence - news, funder RFPs, field reports - not opinion."
  - title: "Identify 3-5 strategic priorities with board vote"
    content: "Following the environmental scan, the board works from the documented observations to a short list of strategic priorities - the 3-5 areas where the organization will direct focused attention and resources over the planning period. Priorities are framed as outcomes, not activities: 'Expand direct service reach to 2,000 annual clients' rather than 'grow programs.' The board votes to confirm the short list before any implementation planning begins. More than five priorities means no real prioritization has occurred."
  - title: "Stress-test each priority against projected funding mix"
    content: "For each confirmed priority, the finance director builds a cost estimate covering staff time, program costs, and indirect. That estimate is laid against the funding map from Step 1 and a three-year revenue projection. Priorities that require funding with no identified source are flagged as 'aspirational pending funding' rather than committed priorities. This step is where most strategic plans fail - organizations skip it and commit to priorities they cannot fund, then abandon them quietly mid-plan."
  - title: "Write goal statements at three levels: strategic priority, annual objective, quarterly activity"
    content: "Each confirmed priority is decomposed into three levels. The strategic priority states the three-year outcome and the measure that defines success. The annual objective states what must be accomplished in the next 12 months to stay on track. The quarterly activity lists the specific actions with owners and completion dates for the next 90 days. The ED and program leads draft these; the board reviews at the priority level only. The three-level structure is what separates a plan from a wish list."
  - title: "Assign accountability (person and board committee) for each priority"
    content: "Each strategic priority gets a named staff lead and a named board committee responsible for oversight. The staff lead is accountable for execution; the board committee receives quarterly progress reports. Unassigned priorities do not move forward. If a priority cannot be assigned to an existing staff person without removing something from their current responsibilities, that capacity constraint must be resolved before the plan is finalized - either by hiring, redistributing work, or narrowing the priority."
  - title: "Build the financial model connecting strategic goals to the budget"
    content: "The finance director builds a three-year financial model with three scenarios: base (confirmed funding), conservative (confirmed funding minus the two most uncertain sources), and optimistic (confirmed plus one high-probability new source). Each scenario shows whether the funded priorities can be sustained, what deficit or surplus looks like in year two and three, and what the operating reserve trajectory is. The model is attached to the written plan as an appendix and reviewed annually."
  - title: "Draft the written plan (8-15 pages)"
    content: "The ED drafts the written plan. Sections include: mission and context (one page), summary of the environmental scan findings (one page), strategic priorities with three-level goal statements (three to five pages), accountability matrix (one page), financial summary (one page), and quarterly review schedule (one page). The plan is written for a board member who was in the room and a new board member who was not - clear enough for the second reader, specific enough for the first. Appendices hold the full financial model and the detailed quarterly activity plans."
  - title: "Present to board for approval and schedule quarterly review dates"
    content: "The ED presents the draft plan at a board meeting with dedicated agenda time - not as a consent item. The board votes to approve the plan and formally adopts the accountability matrix. Before adjourning, the board sets four quarterly review dates for the planning period and assigns the board committee responsible for each review. The first quarterly review should fall 90 days from plan approval. A plan without scheduled reviews is aspirational; the review dates make it operational."
faqs:
  - q: "How long should a nonprofit strategic plan cover?"
    a: "Three years is the standard planning horizon for most nonprofits in the $500K-$10M budget range. Five-year plans produce outdated priorities in the outer years; one-year plans don't give the organization time to move meaningfully. Three years is long enough to see real change and short enough that the funding environment remains somewhat predictable."
  - q: "Should we hire a consultant to run the strategic planning process?"
    a: "A facilitator for the one-day environmental scan session is worthwhile if the board has strong personalities or a history of unproductive meetings. The rest of the process - fund mix audit, financial modeling, goal writing, accountability assignment - should be done by staff. A consultant who writes the plan for you produces a document the staff doesn't own and won't execute."
  - q: "What if the board can't agree on priorities?"
    a: "Bring the funding map into the room. When priorities are debated in the abstract, everyone has opinions. When each priority is attached to a cost estimate and a funding gap, the conversation becomes concrete. Priorities that lack a funding pathway either get paired with a specific fundraising plan or they move to the aspirational list. The funding reality resolves most priority debates."
  - q: "How do we handle a priority that requires a major grant we haven't won yet?"
    a: "Mark it as 'aspirational pending funding' in the plan and build the grant pursuit as a separate work item with an owner, a timeline, and a go/no-go date. If the grant is not secured by the go/no-go date, the priority either gets scaled back or removed from the plan. Organizations that treat pending grants as confirmed revenue build plans that collapse the moment the award notification comes back as a decline."
  - q: "How often should the strategic plan be updated?"
    a: "Review progress quarterly; revise the plan annually at the board's annual retreat or planning session. Major external events - a significant funder exit, a leadership transition, a program failure - warrant an unscheduled plan review. A plan that never gets revised is a plan that has stopped being used."

Strategic plans fail for two reasons: they are written for the board meeting and forgotten the day after approval, or they are written with priorities that were never fundable. The planning process described here is designed to prevent both failure modes. Every priority is stress-tested against real funding before it enters the plan, and every priority has a named person, a named board committee, and a quarterly review date before the plan is approved.

## When to run this workflow

Run this workflow when the organization's current strategic plan is expiring, when a new Executive Director is taking over and needs to establish direction, or when the external environment has shifted significantly enough that the current plan no longer reflects reality. Strategic planning is not a response to a crisis - a crisis calls for a different process. This workflow is for stable organizations building a clear path forward for the next three years.

## Common pitfalls

**Starting with programs, not funding.** Most strategic planning conversations start with "what do we want to accomplish?" and never get to "what can we afford?" The fund mix audit must precede any prioritization conversation. An organization that agrees on priorities before building the financial model will resist cutting priorities later, even when the funding math demands it.

**Treating the written plan as the deliverable.** The plan document is not the deliverable - the accountability matrix and the quarterly review calendar are the deliverables. Organizations that invest heavily in formatting the written plan and lightly in scheduling the reviews have confused the artifact with the outcome.

**Including too many priorities.** A strategic plan with eight priorities is a statement of everything the organization currently does, not a statement of direction. The board's job in Step 3 is to choose, not to list. Choosing means some things do not make the plan.

**Skipping the three-level goal structure.** Strategic priorities without annual objectives and quarterly activities cannot be managed. The three-level structure is what allows a quarterly check-in to produce an answer to the question "are we on track?" rather than an extended discussion of what on-track even means.
