A grant amendment request is a control point. It keeps the team from quietly changing the work, budget, or timeline without funder approval.

Amendments are common. Programs change. Costs move. Hiring is delayed. A partner leaves. A deadline needs more time. The risk is not the change itself. The risk is making the change without a clear request and approval.

Use this workflow when the team may need to change scope, budget, dates, match, deliverables, or another award term. Follow the funder rules and award agreement. Ask for professional advice when the change has legal, contract, or accounting impact.

## Step 1: name the change

Write the change in one clear sentence. Avoid vague language.

Good examples:

- Move $8,000 from travel to participant supplies.
- Extend the grant end date from June 30 to September 30.
- Reduce the number of workshops from 12 to 10 and add two coaching sessions.

The first sentence should tell the reviewer what is changing. Details can come later.

## Step 2: check the award rules

Read the award agreement, notice, grant manual, and funder email instructions. Some changes need prior approval. Some can be made within a small limit. Some are not allowed.

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.308 covers revision of budget and program plans. The award terms and agency rules may add more detail.

Do not act on memory. Save the rule or cite the section in the amendment file.

## Step 3: explain why the change is needed

The reason should be factual. Explain what happened, when it happened, and why the current terms no longer fit.

Keep the tone plain. Do not blame staff or overstate the problem. A funder needs enough information to decide whether the change still supports the grant purpose.

If the change is due to delayed hiring, vendor cost changes, lower turnout, weather, partner issues, or data limits, say so.

## Step 4: show program impact

Explain whether the change affects service levels, people served, activities, outcomes, or report measures.

If the impact is small, say why. If the impact is real, name it. Do not promise that nothing will change if the records show otherwise.

Also explain how the team will keep the work on track. The funder should see that the change has an owner and a plan.

## Step 5: show budget impact

Finance should prepare the revised budget or budget line detail. Show the current approved budget, proposed change, and new total.

If money moves between lines, show both the decrease and increase. If the total award does not change, say that. If match changes, explain the source and support.

Check whether the change affects indirect costs, reimbursement timing, restricted fund releases, or cash flow.

## Step 6: get internal approval

Before submission, route the request to finance, program, grants, and leadership as needed.

The internal approval should say who reviewed the change, what they approved, and when. If leadership accepts a risk, record it.

This matters later. If an auditor, board member, or funder asks why the grant changed, the file should show the decision path.

## Step 7: submit through the right channel

Use the channel the funder requires. This may be a portal, email, signed form, or contract system.

Include the request, reason, revised budget or timeline, and support files. Keep the message short and complete.

Do not send private client details unless the funder requires them and the data handling process allows it.

## Step 8: save the response

Save the funder response. If approved, save the amended terms with the award file. If denied, save the denial and the internal plan.

Do not overwrite the original award. Keep the original terms and amended terms together so the history is clear.

GrantPipe can help connect amendment requests, report dates, revised budgets, and approval records. That helps staff see which terms are current before they submit a report or reimbursement request.

## Step 9: update the grant record

After approval, update the budget, work plan, report calendar, reimbursement schedule, and evidence checklist.

Tell the people affected by the change. This may include finance, program staff, payroll, partners, leadership, and the report writer.

If the change affects a report already in progress, add a note to the report file. The narrative may need to explain the change.

## Step 10: monitor after approval

An amendment is not done when the approval arrives. The team still needs to follow the new terms.

Review the grant after the next close or report cycle. Check whether costs are coded to the revised budget and whether program records match the new plan.

A good amendment file should answer what changed, why it changed, who approved it, and how the records were updated.
