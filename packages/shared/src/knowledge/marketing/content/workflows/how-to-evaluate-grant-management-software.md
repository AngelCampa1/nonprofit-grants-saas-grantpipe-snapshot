steps:
  - title: "Document your current pain points in writing before looking at any vendor"
    content: "Before opening a browser tab with any vendor's website, spend two hours documenting every problem the current system or process causes. Interview everyone who touches grant tracking: the grants manager, the finance director, the ED, any program staff who submit time records or reports. Write down specific incidents - a missed deadline, a version conflict, a reporting error, an audit finding. These specific failures become the evaluation scenarios in Step 5. Organizations that skip this step end up buying software that solves problems they do not have."
  - title: "Identify your non-negotiables: the 3-5 features you will not compromise on"
    content: "From the documented pain points, identify the three to five features that your current system cannot provide and that the new system must provide. Write each non-negotiable as a specific functional requirement, not a category name: 'automated email reminders 30 and 14 days before each reporting deadline' is a non-negotiable; 'better deadline tracking' is not. Non-negotiables are pass/fail - a vendor that cannot meet them is not evaluated further, regardless of other features. A list of more than five non-negotiables usually means the list needs to be prioritized."
  - title: "Build a shortlist of 4-6 vendors from peer recommendations, G2/Capterra, and category research"
    content: "Collect vendor names from three sources: organizations similar to yours in budget size and fund mix (ask your auditor, fiscal sponsors, and peer networks for what they use), review sites (G2 and Capterra for the grant management and nonprofit CRM categories), and direct searches. Aim for four to six vendors on the shortlist - fewer than four limits comparison; more than six creates evaluation fatigue that degrades decision quality. Do not include vendors that are clearly sized for enterprise nonprofits ($50M+ budgets) if you are evaluating for a $1M-$5M organization."
  - title: "Send the same 10 questions to each vendor before scheduling a demo"
    content: "Email the same standardized question list to each vendor before scheduling any demo calls. The 10 questions should cover: pricing model and what is included, implementation timeline and support included in the contract, data portability (how do you export all your data if you cancel), price escalation terms over three years, which of your non-negotiables are included in the base plan, reference customers at similar organizations, minimum contract term, and cancellation terms. Vendors who answer the questions fully are more transparent than vendors who redirect to a demo call. The answers also reveal whether the salesperson understands the product."
  - title: "Run structured demos using your own data scenarios, not vendor-prepared examples"
    content: "Schedule 60-minute demos with each vendor on your shortlist. Send the vendor your scenarios in advance: a specific grant award with real-ish numbers, a reporting deadline, a budget modification scenario, and a staff transition scenario (new user onboarding). Ask the vendor to demonstrate how the software handles each scenario using your data, not their standard demo data. Take notes on where the workflow requires workarounds, where the vendor says 'that's on our roadmap,' and where the software works exactly as described. 'On the roadmap' means it does not exist today."
  - title: "Check references with three organizations of similar size and fund mix"
    content: "For each finalist vendor, ask for three reference contacts at organizations similar to yours. Request organizations with 3-10 active grants and a $1M-$10M budget, not the vendor's marquee enterprise customers. Call or email each reference with the same five questions: how long have you used the product, what works well, what does not work as advertised, how is customer support, and would you choose the same vendor again if you were starting over. References who answer question four and five candidly are the most useful. A reference who only gives positive answers is a vetted marketing contact, not a candid peer."
  - title: "Evaluate contract terms: data portability, price escalation, implementation support, cancellation"
    content: "Before moving to a pilot or final decision, have a staff member with contract review experience (or outside counsel if the annual contract value exceeds $15,000) review the contract terms. Look specifically for: data export rights (you must be able to export all your data in a standard format upon cancellation), price escalation caps (what is the maximum annual price increase), what implementation support is included versus billed separately, and the cancellation notice period. Contracts that require 90-day notice for annual renewals can trap you in a renewal if you miss the window."
  - title: "Run a parallel pilot with your top two choices if budget allows (90-day trial)"
    content: "If both finalist vendors offer a trial period, run them simultaneously using one real grant award as the test case. Enter the grant, set up the reporting deadlines, run the reporting calendar, and attempt the budget-vs-actual view. The parallel pilot reveals usability problems that do not appear in demos: data entry friction, counterintuitive navigation, unclear terminology. Assign one staff member to each pilot system so you have direct comparison feedback from the same type of user. If a free trial is not available, negotiate a 30-day paid pilot clause into the contract before signing."
  - title: "Build a total cost of ownership model: license + implementation + training + internal time"
    content: "The license fee is not the total cost. Build a three-year total cost of ownership model for each finalist that includes: annual license cost (with escalation applied per contract terms), one-time implementation cost, first-year training cost, internal staff time for migration and setup (estimate in hours and convert to salary cost), and annual support cost. The TCO model often changes the rank order of finalists - a lower-priced software with high implementation costs and significant internal time requirements can be more expensive than a higher-priced software with a supported onboarding process."
  - title: "Present final recommendation to board with written rationale"
    content: "Present the selection recommendation to the board (or the designated board committee) as a written document before the meeting, not as a verbal presentation only. The recommendation document includes: the problem being solved, the evaluation process summary, the finalist options with TCO comparison, the non-negotiable assessment, and the selected vendor with rationale. The board approves the purchase authority; it does not select the vendor. If the software cost exceeds the ED's spending authority under the board-approved policy, board approval is required. Submit the final contract to the board treasurer or finance committee for signature review before execution."
faqs:
  - q: "How much should we budget for grant management software?"
    a: "For a nonprofit managing 3-10 active grants with a $1M-$5M budget, expect to spend $3,000-$15,000 per year for a purpose-built grant management system. All-in-one platforms that combine grant management with donor management and financial tracking often start higher. The more important number is total cost of ownership over three years - ask vendors for a three-year price lock or escalation cap before comparing license fees."
  - q: "Should we evaluate all-in-one platforms or point solutions?"
    a: "For organizations where grants and donor management are separate functions with separate staff, point solutions can be appropriate. For organizations where the same staff manages both grants and donors, or where the ED needs a unified view of restricted and unrestricted revenue, an integrated platform reduces the risk of data living in two disconnected systems. The real cost of disconnected systems is not the license fee - it is the staff time spent reconciling data between systems."
  - q: "How long does implementation typically take?"
    a: "For a mid-sized nonprofit with 5-10 active grants and a clean existing dataset, a purpose-built grant management system should be operational within 30-45 days of contract execution. Implementation that takes longer usually indicates a data quality problem in the source data, a vendor with insufficient onboarding support, or an internal project management failure. Ask vendors for their median time-to-first-report for organizations of your size."
  - q: "What happens to our data if we cancel?"
    a: "This question must be answered before you sign the contract, not after you decide to leave. Require a data export provision in the contract that gives you the right to export all your data in CSV or equivalent format upon request and upon cancellation. Verify that the export includes not just grant records but all attachments, notes, and historical data. A vendor that will not agree to a data portability clause in the contract is a vendor you should not sign with."
  - q: "Do we need to involve the board in the selection decision?"
    a: "If the contract value exceeds the ED's unilateral spending authority per the organization's financial policies, yes. Most nonprofit financial policies set ED spending authority between $10,000 and $25,000 for multi-year commitments. The board does not select the vendor - the staff evaluates and recommends - but the board approves the expenditure. Present the recommendation with the written rationale described in Step 10."

Software evaluations that go wrong tend to go wrong in the same way: the organization schedules vendor demos before defining what problem they need to solve, watches impressive demos of features they will never use, and selects based on the quality of the sales experience rather than the fit to their actual workflow. The evaluation process described here is designed to prevent that outcome by putting problem definition and scenario testing ahead of vendor contact.

## When to run this workflow

Run this workflow when the current grant tracking system (spreadsheet or existing software) has produced a documented compliance failure, when a staff transition has revealed that the current system is person-dependent rather than process-dependent, or when grant portfolio growth has outpaced the current system's capacity. Do not run a software evaluation during the peak of grant reporting season - the evaluation deserves focused attention.

## Common pitfalls

**Letting the vendor build the demo agenda.** A vendor-built demo is a highlight reel. The vendor will show the features that work smoothly and gloss over the ones that require workarounds. The structured scenario approach in Step 5 forces the vendor to demonstrate their system against your actual use cases, not their showcase features.

**Evaluating by feature list, not by workflow.** A feature checklist comparison can make two products with identical feature lists look equivalent when their actual usability is dramatically different. The parallel pilot in Step 8 reveals workflow friction that no feature list can show.

**Ignoring contract terms until it is too late.** Data portability and cancellation terms are negotiable before you sign and effectively non-negotiable after. The time to negotiate is before contract execution, not when you want to leave.

**Underestimating internal time costs.** Implementation always requires more internal staff time than the vendor estimates. The data migration, the workflow configuration, the training, and the parallel-running period all consume staff hours that come from somewhere. Budget the internal time explicitly or the implementation will be incomplete.
