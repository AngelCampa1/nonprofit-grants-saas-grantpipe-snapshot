A renewal request should not start with a blank proposal. It should start with a clear look at the current grant. Did the team do the work? Did spending match the budget? Were reports on time? Is the funder relationship healthy? Are any restrictions still open?

A renewal risk brief turns those questions into one short record. It helps the executive director, grants manager, program owner, and finance lead decide whether to renew, wait, change the request, or step away.

This workflow fits foundation grants and many public grants. For federal awards, also check the award terms and reporting rules. The Uniform Guidance performance reporting section at 2 CFR 200.329 is a useful reference for federal reporting expectations, but each award can add more rules.

## Step 1: open the current grant file

Start with the current grant agreement. Do not rely on the last proposal alone. The agreement is the active record. It may include reporting dates, restricted use, payment terms, budget limits, and notice rules.

Pull these items into the review:

- Award letter or contract
- Approved budget
- Proposal and work plan
- Report schedule
- Last submitted report
- Funder emails or call notes
- Budget to actual report
- Outcome data
- Open compliance notes

If any of these are missing, mark that in the brief. Missing evidence is itself a renewal risk.

## Step 2: check deadlines and report status

List each report due under the current grant. Mark it as submitted, upcoming, late, or not found. Add the date, owner, and link to the submitted file.

Late reporting does not always end a renewal path, but leaders should know before they contact the funder. If a report is late, write down why, who owns the fix, and when it will be sent.

GrantPipe can help keep grant compliance deadlines, reporting tasks, and audit evidence tied to the same grant record. That matters when the team needs a quick answer before a renewal call.

## Step 3: review spending

Compare the approved budget to actual spending. Focus on the story the numbers tell.

Look for underspending, overspending, large budget shifts, unspent restricted funds, costs outside the grant period, and costs that do not match the funded program. If the funder requires approval for budget changes, check whether the approval is in the file.

A renewal ask should not ignore the current balance. If the program is behind, the team may need a smaller renewal, a no-cost extension, a budget change, or a better explanation of timing.

## Step 4: test outcome evidence

Outcome evidence should match the promise made in the proposal. Pull the measures, counts, stories, surveys, attendance logs, case notes, or partner reports that support the last report and the renewal story.

Separate strong evidence from weak evidence. Strong evidence has a date, source, owner, and clear link to the funded work. Weak evidence is vague, late, or hard to trace.

Do not invent results to fill a gap. If data is missing, say so. The brief can recommend a fix before the renewal request goes out.

## Step 5: review restrictions

Check whether the current grant has purpose, time, budget, or location limits. Finance should say whether restricted funds were tracked and whether any amount remains restricted.

This is a practical review, not accounting advice. For unusual restrictions or unclear grant terms, ask a CPA or legal advisor before changing the accounting or accepting a new award.

The point of the brief is to show risk early. A renewal that adds money to a messy restricted fund can make reporting harder.

## Step 6: summarize the funder relationship

Add plain notes from the last year. Did the funder ask questions? Did staff meet with them? Did the funder praise, warn, or redirect the work? Were there staff changes on either side?

Use facts, not vibes. "Program officer asked for more expense detail on March 12" is useful. "Relationship seems good" is not enough.

## Step 7: make a recommendation

End the brief with one recommendation:

- Renew now
- Renew after fixes
- Renew with a changed request
- Ask for an extension first
- Do not renew

Name the reason. If the answer is renew after fixes, list the fixes, owner, and due date. Keep the brief short enough for leadership to use before a call.

## Step 8: save the brief

Save the brief in the grant file with the evidence used to write it. If the team moves forward, link the brief to the proposal plan. If the team does not renew, keep the reason. It will help next year when the same funder comes up again.

A good renewal brief does not try to sell the grant. It helps leaders see the current record clearly before they ask for more support.
