Grant budgets change because real work changes. Staff leave. Vendor costs rise. A program serves more people than expected. A line item that looked right in the proposal may not fit the field work six months later.

The control point is not whether change is allowed. The control point is whether the team approves the change before spending follows it.

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.308 and 2 CFR 200.407 are the main starting points. Award terms may add tighter rules. Foundation and state grants have their own terms, but the workflow is still useful.

## Step 1: write the request plainly

Start with a short request. Name the award, budget period, amount to move, category losing funds, category gaining funds, and reason.

Attach a current budget-to-actual report. Show the approved budget, actual spending, remaining balance, proposed change, and revised budget.

The reason should be specific. "Program needs changed" is weak. "The approved training vendor raised rates, and the program needs to move $4,200 from supplies to contracted training to complete the approved sessions" is better.

## Step 2: check whether scope changes

A budget change can hide a scope change. If the work, service level, target group, deliverable, location, or key staff changes, pause.

A scope change is more likely to need funder approval. It may also affect reports, outcomes, match, or indirect costs.

Ask program staff to confirm whether the revised budget still supports the approved work. Save that answer with the request.

## Step 3: review prior approval rules

For federal awards, check 2 CFR 200.308 and any agency terms. Some changes need prior approval. Examples may include a change in scope, key person changes, certain budget transfers, participant support cost changes, or other items named in the award.

Also check whether the award has a rebudgeting threshold. Some awards allow movement between categories up to a limit. Others require approval for any movement.

If prior approval is needed, do not spend under the revised budget until approval is received.

## Step 4: test allowability

The new spending must still be allowable. Use the basic allowability test in 2 CFR 200.403: the cost should be necessary, reasonable, allocable, consistent, and supported.

Budget approval does not make a bad cost allowable. If the new category includes food, equipment, travel, incentives, or consultant costs, check extra rules and award terms.

If the cost needs procurement support, link the budget revision to the procurement file. The [procurement file test](/workflows/test-procurement-file-before-audit) can help.

## Step 5: check match and reporting effects

A revision can affect match, cost share, cash flow, and reports. It can also affect an [SF-425 report](/workflows/prepare-sf-425-quarterly-report) if the report compares spending by category.

Ask finance to confirm whether the revision changes:

- match or cost share
- indirect cost recovery
- restricted cash needs
- report lines
- subrecipient budgets
- closeout timing

Document the answer before approval.

## Step 6: get the right approval

Internal approval should include program and finance. Grants staff should confirm funder terms. Executive approval may be needed for larger changes or changes that affect commitments.

If funder approval is needed, submit the request through the required portal or email. Save the submission and response.

Use the approved date as the start date for the revised budget unless the funder says otherwise.

## Step 7: update the grant file

Save the request, old budget, new budget, approval trail, funder response, and report notes. Update the working budget so staff stop using the old version.

Do not leave two active budgets in circulation. That is how spending errors happen.

## Communicate the change

After approval, tell the people who will spend, code, review, and report on the grant. Program staff need to know what changed. Finance needs the revised categories. Grants staff need the reporting effect.

Use one short notice. Include the approval date, effective date, revised categories, and any limits. Attach the approved budget.

If the revision is denied, communicate that too. A denied request can still prevent a future mistake if staff know the old budget remains in force.

## Test the next report

After the revision is approved, look ahead to the next report. Confirm the revised budget will appear correctly in the report format. Some funder portals require the budget revision to be entered before expenses can be reported against the new category.

Also check whether prior spending needs to be recoded. Do not recode old costs unless the approval allows it and the costs fit the revised budget period.

The final test is simple: a reviewer should be able to open the grant file and see why the budget changed, who approved it, and which budget staff now use.
