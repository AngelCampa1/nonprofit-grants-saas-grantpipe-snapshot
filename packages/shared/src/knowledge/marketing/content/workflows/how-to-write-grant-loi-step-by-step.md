steps:
  - title: "Research the funder's stated priorities and recent grants (990-PF search, foundation website, GuideStar)"
    content: "Before opening a document, spend 90 minutes on funder research. On the foundation website, read the current grantmaking priorities, eligibility criteria, and any recent grantee stories. On ProPublica Nonprofit Explorer or the IRS website, pull the foundation's most recent Form 990-PF and review Part XV (grants paid) to see which organizations received awards, in what amounts, and for what described purposes. Look for pattern matches: organizations similar to yours in size, geography, and mission area. If the 990-PF shows no grants to organizations like yours in the last three years, stop - this funder is not a match regardless of what the website says."
  - title: "Confirm LOI format requirements (length, format, submission method)"
    content: "Most foundations publish explicit LOI guidelines on their websites. Find and record: the maximum page length (most LOIs are one to three pages), the required format (letter, online form, email), the submission method (portal, email, postal mail), the submission deadline or open cycle, and any required attachments. If the funder uses an online portal, create an account before writing a word - portals sometimes have character limits that differ from the stated page limit, and discovering this after the draft is complete costs time. Print or save the guidelines as a PDF and keep them open while writing."
  - title: "Draft the opening paragraph: funder alignment and organization credibility in three sentences"
    content: "The opening paragraph does two things in three sentences: it demonstrates that you read the funder's current priorities (not their priorities from two years ago), and it establishes your organization's standing to address those priorities. Sentence one names the specific priority or program area you are responding to, using the funder's own language. Sentence two states your organization's mission and relevant track record in one sentence. Sentence three states the program you are proposing and its connection to the priority. Do not open with organizational history, founding story, or a statistic about the problem - those belong in the body."
  - title: "Write the problem statement using local data (census, state data, partner research)"
    content: "The problem statement establishes why the proposed program is necessary in the specific geography you serve. Use data that is current (within three years), specific to your service area (county or city level, not national), and sourced (cite the source inline). Acceptable sources include U.S. Census Bureau data, state agency reports, local United Way community assessments, and peer-reviewed research. Do not use statistics you cannot cite, and do not use statistics that do not apply to your geography. A national poverty rate is not a substitute for a county-level poverty rate when you serve a specific county."
  - title: "Describe the proposed program in two to three paragraphs: activities, timeline, population"
    content: "The program description covers three things: who will be served (the specific population, with demographic detail if relevant), what activities will occur (in chronological order, with key milestones), and when the program will run (start date, end date, or ongoing). Write this section in plain language - no jargon specific to your program model that a program officer unfamiliar with your field would not understand. If the program uses an evidence-based model, name it and cite the evidence. If the program is new, say so and describe the pilot or planning process that preceded it."
  - title: "State the dollar ask with brief budget context"
    content: "State the specific dollar amount requested from this funder. Then provide two to three sentences of budget context: the total project budget, the percentage of the total budget the grant represents, and the other confirmed or anticipated sources that complete the funding. Avoid round numbers that signal a lack of specificity ($50,000 is less credible than $48,400). If the funder has a typical grant range (visible in the 990-PF), stay within that range unless you have a specific reason to exceed it and can explain it."
  - title: "Write the sustainability paragraph"
    content: "The sustainability paragraph answers the implicit question every funder asks: what happens to this program when our grant ends? The answer must be specific - not 'we will seek additional funding' but 'the program will transition to a fee-for-service model serving 60% of clients at cost, with the remaining 40% subsidized by an endowment fund currently in planning.' If the program is genuinely grant-dependent with no sustainability plan, say so and explain why continued grant funding is the appropriate model for this type of work. Vague sustainability language is worse than an honest explanation of grant dependency."
  - title: "Include required attachments (most LOIs require IRS determination letter and financials)"
    content: "Review the funder's guidelines for required attachments. Common requirements include the IRS 501(c)(3) determination letter (the original letter from the IRS, not a copy of your tax-exempt application), the most recent audited financial statements or Form 990, the current year operating budget, and a list of the board of directors. Prepare all attachments before finalizing the LOI - last-minute attachment gathering is how submissions miss deadlines. Name each file clearly using the grant ID format from your tracking system: FunderName_IRS990_2025.pdf."
  - title: "Internal review: does every paragraph connect to something the funder said they care about?"
    content: "Before submitting, do a single-pass review against the funder's published priorities. Print or display the priorities document alongside the draft. For each paragraph, identify the specific funder priority it addresses. If a paragraph cannot be connected to a stated priority, either cut it or rewrite it to make the connection explicit. This review takes 20 minutes and prevents the most common rejection reason: a well-written LOI that does not demonstrate funder alignment because the writer assumed alignment was obvious."
  - title: "Submit and calendar the follow-up date (30 days after submission if no response protocol)"
    content: "Submit through the specified method and save a confirmation (screenshot, email receipt, portal confirmation number) in the grant's document folder. Update your grants register with the submission date and the status 'LOI submitted.' Set a calendar reminder for 30 days post-submission if the funder's guidelines do not specify a response timeline. The follow-up at 30 days is a brief email confirming receipt and offering to provide additional information - not a status request. If the funder acknowledges receipt in the submission confirmation, move the follow-up to 60 days."
faqs:
  - q: "How long should an LOI be?"
    a: "Follow the funder's guidelines exactly. If they specify two pages, write two pages - not 1.8 and not 2.1. If guidelines are silent, one to two single-spaced pages in 11- or 12-point type is the default. Shorter is usually better: a tightly written one-page LOI that answers the funder's questions is more likely to advance than a sprawling two-page LOI that answers questions the funder did not ask."
  - q: "Should we call the funder before submitting an LOI?"
    a: "If the foundation has a program officer listed on its website and the guidelines do not explicitly discourage contact, a brief pre-submission call or email is appropriate. The purpose is to confirm that the proposed program fits the current funding cycle and that the ask amount is in range - not to pitch the program. Keep pre-submission contact to one touchpoint. Foundations that receive multiple calls about a single prospective LOI remember it negatively."
  - q: "What if the funder has no LOI requirement and goes straight to full proposals?"
    a: "Use the same research and alignment check from Steps 1 and 9, but skip the LOI format entirely. The opening paragraph, problem statement, program description, ask, and sustainability sections map directly to the standard full proposal structure. The difference is length and detail, not structure."
  - q: "How do we track which LOIs advance to full proposals?"
    a: "Add two columns to your grants register: LOI submitted date and LOI outcome (invited to full proposal, declined, pending, no response). Track conversion rate annually - the percentage of LOIs that advance to full proposals. A conversion rate below 30% usually signals a funder research problem, not a writing problem: you are submitting to mismatched funders."
  - q: "Can we reuse LOI text across multiple funders?"
    a: "The problem statement, program description, and budget context can be adapted for multiple LOIs. The opening paragraph (funder alignment) and the sustainability paragraph (context-specific) must be written fresh for each funder. An LOI that opens with the wrong funder's priority language because someone forgot to update it is an immediate decline. Build a master program description document and adapt from it, rather than copying from a previous LOI."

Foundation program officers read dozens of LOIs each week. The ones that advance share two characteristics: they demonstrate genuine familiarity with the funder's current priorities, not the priorities listed on a website that hasn't been updated since 2023, and they describe a program clearly enough that the program officer can summarize it in two sentences to a colleague. Neither characteristic requires writing talent; both require preparation.

## When to run this workflow

Run this workflow each time you prepare a new letter of inquiry. Do not adapt an LOI written for a previous funder without completing Step 1 from scratch - funder priorities change, and an LOI written for last year's priority language is often declined before the program description is read.

## Common pitfalls

**Starting with the draft instead of the research.** Writers who open a document before completing funder research spend the most time on the LOI and produce the least aligned result. The 90-minute research phase in Step 1 makes every subsequent step faster and more specific.

**Using the same opening paragraph for all funders.** The opening paragraph is the most important and the most often recycled. A program officer who sees an opening paragraph that could have been written for any funder in the field knows immediately that the organization did not do its homework. The opening must contain language that only makes sense for this specific funder.

**Inflating the problem with national statistics.** A program serving clients in Tarrant County, Texas should use Tarrant County data, not national statistics. National data is available and easy to cite; local data requires more effort to find and demonstrates more credibility.

**Submitting without internal review.** The alignment check in Step 9 catches the most common rejection reason before submission. It takes 20 minutes. Organizations that skip it submit LOIs that read as technically competent but strategically misaligned.
