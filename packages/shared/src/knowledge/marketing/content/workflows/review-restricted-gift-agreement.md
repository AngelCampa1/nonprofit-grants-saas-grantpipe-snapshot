A restricted gift agreement can be generous and hard at the same time. The donor may want to support a program, building, scholarship, or time period. The nonprofit may want to say yes. Finance still needs to know whether the terms can be met, coded, reported, and released.

This workflow helps teams review the agreement before acceptance. It does not replace legal or accounting advice. Use advisers when the terms are large, unusual, or unclear.

## Step 1: read the whole agreement

Start with the full text, not the summary email. Look for the donor name, amount, payment timing, purpose, time limit, conditions, reporting duties, naming terms, investment terms, and any right of return.

Mark each term in plain words. If a sentence is unclear, do not assume the friendliest meaning. Ask for a revision or written clarification.

GrantPipe can keep donor CRM notes, restricted funds, deadlines, reporting, and audit evidence connected. That helps after acceptance, but the first control is still careful reading.

## Step 2: tell restriction from condition

A restriction limits how or when funds can be used. A condition may create a barrier that must be overcome before the gift is recognized. FASB ASU 2018-08 is useful for this review.

Do not make this call from memory on a material gift. If the agreement says funds must be returned if a barrier is not met, pause for accounting review.

The agreement should leave finance with a clear rule. What must happen before revenue, release, or reporting?

## Step 3: test mission and capacity

The gift should fit the nonprofit's mission and real capacity. A restricted gift can create work, deadlines, reporting duties, staffing needs, and cash pressure.

Ask:

- Can we use the gift for the stated purpose?
- Do we already run this program?
- Can we track spending for this restriction?
- Can we meet reporting deadlines?
- Does the gift require matching funds?
- Does it create naming or public recognition duties?
- Does it conflict with policy?

If the answer is no, revise or decline the terms before acceptance.

## Step 4: assign fund coding and owner

Finance should assign the fund code, revenue account, campaign code, and release trigger before the gift is posted. Development should assign the relationship owner. Leadership should own unusual risk.

The agreement review should not end with a signed PDF. It should create working records: fund, deadline, report, release rule, and evidence folder.

## Step 5: review receipt and acknowledgment language

The thank-you language should fit the agreement. It should not promise outcomes the nonprofit cannot prove. It should not add tax wording that is not supported.

If the gift is made through a donor advised fund sponsor, review the sponsor instructions. The person who advised the grant may receive a thank-you note when allowed, but the sponsor may be the donor of record.

## Step 6: approve or revise

Use a simple decision:

- Accept as written
- Accept after donor revision
- Hold for legal or accounting review
- Decline

Save the decision with the agreement. If terms changed, save the final version and mark the old one as draft.

## Step 7: create release and reporting tasks

After approval, create tasks for donor reports, grant compliance deadlines, spending reviews, and release from restriction checks.

If the gift is for a program, link the release to qualifying costs or program evidence. If it is time restricted, link it to the date. If it includes both, track both.

## Outputs

The workflow should produce a reviewed agreement, restriction notes, condition review notes, fund coding, receipt rule, reporting owner, and release evidence plan.

## Common failures

Common failures include accepting unclear language, treating a condition as a restriction, missing a donor report, sending a receipt that does not match the gift, and failing to tell finance about a side letter.

Another failure is keeping the agreement in development files only. Finance needs the agreement to code the gift and release the restriction. Leadership needs it to understand risk.

## Automation ideas

Automation can route agreements with words like return, condition, scholarship, endowment, capital, naming, report, or match. It can create deadlines and release review tasks. It can also remind staff when an accepted agreement has no fund code.

Automation should not approve terms. It should make sure the right people review the agreement before the nonprofit says yes.
