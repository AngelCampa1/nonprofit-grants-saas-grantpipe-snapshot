A grant readiness self assessment is a calm way to ask one question: can we manage the grant after we win it?

Many teams only ask whether they can write the proposal. That is too narrow. A strong proposal can still create trouble if finance cannot track the costs, if program staff cannot collect report data, or if leadership has not approved the match plan.

Use this workflow before a grant push, before a large application, or when a new program starts chasing restricted money. It is not legal or accounting advice. It is a plain operating check. Use the funder rules, your finance policy, and professional advice when judgment is needed.

## Step 1: name the grant lane

Start by naming the type of grants the team wants. Do not assess readiness in the abstract.

Write down the likely funder type, program area, grant size, period, and report burden. A small foundation grant may need a short report and a budget note. A federal award may need tighter cost rules, records, and performance reporting.

This first step keeps the review practical. The answer may be yes for a $25,000 program grant and no for a complex reimbursement award.

## Step 2: check basic eligibility

Confirm the simple gates first. Check tax status, geography, population served, program type, registration needs, matching rules, and any past performance limits.

If the funder requires a unique entity ID, active registration, audit, insurance, board approval, or partner letter, note the status. Do not assume it can be handled later. Late setup work can turn into a missed deadline.

Save links or screenshots for the rules you checked. The file should show why the team thought it was eligible.

## Step 3: test program fit

Ask the program lead to explain the funded work in plain words. What will staff do? Who will be served? What records prove the work happened? What result will be reported?

Then compare that answer to the likely funder goal. If the fit is forced, stop and discuss it. A weak fit often leads to vague promises and hard reports.

Do not stretch the program just to match a funder. If the work would change only to chase the grant, leadership should approve that choice before staff spend time writing.

## Step 4: review budget readiness

Finance should check whether the organization can build and track a grant budget. Review salary allocations, fringe, supplies, travel, partner costs, indirect costs, match, and shared costs.

Ask whether each cost can be tied to a source record. Payroll needs time or allocation support. Shared costs need a method. Match needs proof. Reimbursements need invoices and payment records.

If the organization cannot track the money by grant, fund, class, project, or another field, mark that as a gap. GrantPipe can help connect grants, restricted funds, deadlines, and evidence in one place, but the team still needs clear rules for how costs are coded.

## Step 5: check reporting capacity

Look at reporting before the award. Ask what data the funder may request. This may include people served, sessions held, outcomes, demographics, budget to actual detail, and narrative notes.

For each measure, name the source system and owner. If the answer is a spreadsheet owned by one person, note the risk. If the answer is "we can estimate it," the metric is not ready.

Use the same care for finance reports. The team should know who can produce a budget report, who reviews it, and where the support file lives.

## Step 6: review evidence habits

A ready team saves proof as work happens. It does not rebuild the file at closeout.

Check whether staff save award letters, budgets, amendments, invoices, receipts, time records, attendance logs, report drafts, approvals, and funder emails. Check whether files have clear names. Check whether private client data is handled with care.

If the organization has no file rule, write a simple one. It can start with a folder by grant, then subfolders for award, budget, finance, program data, reports, and closeout.

## Step 7: score the gaps

Use three scores.

Go means the team can apply now.

Wait means the grant looks useful, but the team needs to fix gaps first.

No means the funder fit, rules, or capacity risk is too high.

Do not hide weak areas. The goal is not to pass the test. The goal is to avoid winning work the team cannot support.

## Step 8: assign owners

Every gap needs an owner and date. Common owners include finance, grants, program, operations, and leadership.

Some gaps must be fixed before application. Examples include eligibility, required registration, and budget math. Other gaps can be fixed before award setup, such as folder structure or report calendar setup.

Write the decision at the end. Include the grant lane, score, open risks, owners, and next review date. That record helps the team avoid repeating the same debate for each opportunity.

A good self assessment should make the next grant meeting shorter. The team can spend less time guessing and more time choosing the right grants to pursue.
