steps:
  - title: "Pull the Statement of Activities and Statement of Financial Position from your accounting system for the period"
    content: "After month-end close, generate two core statements: the Statement of Activities (income statement equivalent) showing year-to-date revenue and expenses against budget, and the Statement of Financial Position (balance sheet equivalent) showing assets, liabilities, and net assets as of month-end. Both statements should be available from your accounting system as standard reports - QuickBooks, Sage Intacct, and similar systems produce these in their standard reporting menus. Run both reports the morning after month-end close while the period data is fresh. If your accounting system cannot produce a budget-vs-actual Statement of Activities, that is a system configuration problem that needs to be resolved."
  - title: "Generate fund-level spending report for all active grants"
    content: "Generate a report showing, for each active grant: approved grant budget, year-to-date expenditures charged to the fund, remaining budget, and percentage of budget period elapsed. Most accounting systems can produce this from the fund or class segments - in QuickBooks, run a Profit and Loss by Class; in Sage Intacct, run by dimension. The key comparison is spend rate versus time elapsed: a grant that is 60% through its budget period should have spent approximately 60% of its budget, adjusted for any planned front- or back-loading. Flag any grant with a spend rate more than 15 percentage points ahead of or behind its time elapsed."
  - title: "Calculate donor retention rate for the period if quarterly board meeting"
    content: "For quarterly board meetings, calculate the donor retention rate for the period: donors who gave in the same period last year who have also given this year, divided by total donors from the same period last year. Run this from your donor management system. A retention rate above 60% is acceptable; above 70% is strong; below 50% requires board attention and explanation. Include a comparison to the prior two periods so the board can see the trend, not just the current number. Monthly board meetings do not require the full retention calculation; a gift count and total by month is sufficient."
  - title: "Calculate operating reserve balance as months of operating expenses"
    content: "Calculate the current operating reserve balance: take unrestricted net assets from the Statement of Financial Position, subtract any board-designated funds and fixed assets, and divide by the average monthly operating expense (prior 12 months of total expenses divided by 12). The result is the operating reserve in months. Compare to the board's adopted reserve policy. If the reserve is below the policy minimum, that is a board-decision item, not a staff-communication item - include it in the summary as an action item, not a footnote."
  - title: "Flag any restricted fund within 10% of its budget ceiling"
    content: "Review the fund-level spending report from Step 2. Flag any grant fund where actual expenditures have reached 90% or more of the approved grant budget. A fund at 90% of budget needs either a budget modification (if there is unspent scope), a spending slowdown (if the budget period has significant time remaining), or a planned closeout conversation with the program staff. These flags are a compliance matter, not just a financial reporting matter - overspending a grant budget without prior funder approval is a compliance violation."
  - title: "Flag any grant with a reporting deadline within 45 days"
    content: "Cross-reference the grant portfolio against the current calendar. Flag any grant with a financial or programmatic report due within 45 days. For each flagged grant, note the report type, the due date, the staff lead, and the current completion status (not started, in progress, ready for review). This information belongs in the board report only if board action is needed - a routine report due in 30 days that is on track does not need board attention. A report due in 14 days that has not been started does."
  - title: "Write a three-paragraph narrative: what the numbers show, what the board should decide, and what staff needs"
    content: "The narrative is three paragraphs, each answering one question. Paragraph one answers 'what do the numbers show this period?' - summarize financial performance in three sentences, naming specific variances larger than 10% and their cause. Paragraph two answers 'what decisions does the board need to make?' - list any items requiring board vote or formal direction. Paragraph three answers 'what does staff need from the board?' - specific asks: a contact introduction, authorization for a budget modification, approval to draw from reserves. If there are no action items for the board, say so explicitly."
  - title: "Prepare the one-page summary for board members without finance background"
    content: "Build a one-page summary that presents four numbers: year-to-date net income or deficit against budget, current cash balance, operating reserve in months, and number of active grants with reporting deadlines in the next 45 days. Include one sentence of plain-language context for each number. Do not use accounting terminology on this page - 'Statement of Activities' becomes 'income and expenses,' 'net assets' becomes 'reserves.' This page goes on top; the full statements go in an appendix. Board members who want the detail have the appendix; board members who need the summary have it without searching."
  - title: "Distribute five business days before the meeting with a brief cover email"
    content: "Send the full board financial package - one-page summary, full statements, narrative - five business days before the board meeting. The cover email is three sentences: what is attached, whether there are action items, and whether the treasurer has pre-reviewed. Five business days gives board members time to read the materials, form questions, and contact the ED or treasurer if something needs clarification before the meeting. Board meetings where financial materials arrive the day before produce the least useful financial conversations."
  - title: "Bring full reports as backup documents, not primary board presentation material"
    content: "At the board meeting, present from the one-page summary. Answer questions from the full reports as backup. Do not walk the board through the Statement of Activities line by line - they have it in the packet and can read it. A financial presentation that takes more than 15 minutes is using board meeting time inefficiently. Reserve extended financial discussion for the finance committee, which meets separately and has the technical background for detailed review. The board's job is governance decisions, not bookkeeping review."
faqs:
  - q: "Does the full board need to see the detailed financial statements, or just the summary?"
    a: "Both, but in the right roles. The one-page summary is the primary document for the board meeting presentation. The full statements (Statement of Activities, Statement of Financial Position, fund-level grant report) are distributed in advance as appendices for board members who want the detail. The finance committee reviews the full statements in depth at its separate meeting before the full board meeting. This structure ensures the full board can govern effectively without requiring every board member to have a finance background."
  - q: "How should we handle a month where the numbers are bad?"
    a: "Report it accurately and lead with the explanation in paragraph one of the narrative. A finance function that delays, softens, or buries bad financial news produces a board that cannot make timely decisions. The board's job is to govern through adversity; it cannot do that job without accurate information. Presenting difficult numbers clearly, with an explanation of the cause and the proposed staff response, is more credible than presenting softened numbers that the board has to decode."
  - q: "What if the accounting month-end close isn't finished in time for the board meeting?"
    a: "Send preliminary financials with a clear 'PRELIMINARY - SUBJECT TO ADJUSTMENT' label. Note what is incomplete and why. A preliminary financial report with a clear label is more useful than a delayed one. Do not cancel the financial presentation because the close is not complete - the board needs to know the approximate financial position even if the numbers are not finalized. Recurring close delays are a systems problem that the ED and finance staff need to resolve."
  - q: "How many months of data should the board see?"
    a: "Year-to-date actual vs. budget is the primary view. For the Statement of Financial Position, the current period compared to the same period in the prior year. For the operating reserve, a 12-month rolling calculation. Board members should not need to dig into month-by-month detail to understand trends - the year-to-date view absorbs monthly volatility and shows the trajectory."
  - q: "Who should present the financial report to the board?"
    a: "The treasurer or finance committee chair presents the financials, not the finance director. The finance director or CFO is in the room as a technical resource. This structure reinforces board accountability for financial oversight and prevents the presentation from becoming a staff briefing. The treasurer's presentation should be no more than 10 minutes, followed by questions."

The most common board financial reporting failure is not inaccuracy - it is inaccessibility. Boards that receive 15-page financial packets with no summary, no narrative, and no explicit action items spend their financial review time asking clarifying questions about the numbers instead of making governance decisions. The workflow here solves the inaccessibility problem without sacrificing accuracy or completeness.

## When to run this workflow

Run this workflow monthly, beginning the morning after month-end close. For organizations with quarterly board meetings, run the full workflow quarterly; run a lighter version (Steps 1, 4, 7, and 8 only) for interim months to give the finance committee and ED the financial picture without generating the full board package.

## Common pitfalls

**Presenting from the full statements rather than the summary.** Walking the board line by line through a Statement of Activities is a 45-minute meeting tax that most board members endure rather than find useful. Present from the one-page summary and trust board members to read the full statements in advance.

**Not naming the cause of variances.** A revenue variance of ($45,000) against budget is not informative without an explanation. 'Revenue is ($45,000) below budget because the spring gala was postponed from March to May' is actionable. The variance without the explanation produces confusion and debate about numbers instead of decisions about responses.

**Skipping the 45-day grant deadline flag.** The board financial report is also a compliance monitoring document. Grants with imminent deadlines should appear in the report - not buried in a spreadsheet that only the grants manager reviews.

**Using the same reporting format for every meeting.** Monthly and quarterly board meetings serve different governance functions. Monthly reports should be brief and focused on current-period performance. Quarterly reports should include trend analysis, donor retention, reserve trajectory, and grant pipeline. Build separate templates for each cadence.
