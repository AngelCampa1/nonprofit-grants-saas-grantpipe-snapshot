Functional expense allocation is where many clean-looking nonprofit financial statements get fragile.

The organization may have good programs and honest staff. But if shared costs are split by habit, copied from last year, or assigned to whichever grant has budget left, the allocation can be hard to defend.

This workflow gives finance teams a practical way to choose and document the method.

## Step 1: List shared costs

Start by naming the costs that benefit more than one function:

- rent and utilities
- shared software
- accounting and payroll
- executive leadership time
- insurance
- office supplies
- shared administrative staff
- development systems used for both fundraising and grant reporting

Do not allocate costs that clearly belong to one function. If a cost only supports one program, charge it there. Allocation is for shared benefit.

## Step 2: Build cost pools

Group similar costs into cost pools. For example:

- occupancy
- shared technology
- finance and administration
- executive leadership
- development and communications

Each pool should have one allocation basis. Do not mix rent, payroll, and software in one pool unless the same basis makes sense for all of them.

## Step 3: Choose the basis

The allocation basis should reflect benefit. Common bases include:

- square footage for occupancy
- headcount for some shared people costs
- time records for staff effort
- transaction counts for finance work
- direct cost totals when allowed and supportable
- program activity measures when they explain usage

The easiest basis is not always the best basis. A flat 70/20/10 split may be simple, but it needs support.

## Step 4: Write the policy

The policy should say:

1. Which costs are allocated.
2. Which costs are direct charged.
3. Which basis applies to each pool.
4. Where support is stored.
5. How often the basis is reviewed.
6. Who approves changes.

GrantPipe helps by keeping restricted funds, grant budgets, supporting documents, and reporting views together. That makes allocation review less dependent on one spreadsheet that only finance understands.

## Step 5: Tie to grant rules

Functional allocation and grant cost allocation are related but not identical. Financial statements need functional categories. Federal awards need costs to be allocable, allowable, reasonable, and consistently treated.

If a shared cost is charged to a federal award, the method must also fit the award terms and Uniform Guidance cost principles. Do not let the statement allocation become a shortcut for grant charging.

## Step 6: Review annually

Review the methodology before audit prep. Also review it when:

- a program starts or closes
- staff roles change
- office space changes
- a major grant begins
- a new fundraising channel launches
- software usage changes

Keep the review notes. Auditors do not need a novel. They need to see that the method was considered, supported, and applied consistently.

## What to avoid

Avoid percentages with no support. Avoid changing methods to improve ratios. Avoid charging shared costs to a grant just because the grant has budget left. Avoid having one method in the policy and another method in the books.

Good allocation is boring. It should be documented, repeatable, and close enough to reality that leaders can explain it in plain language.

## Give the board the plain-English version

The board does not need every allocation formula. It does need to understand the policy well enough to govern financial reporting.

Include a short summary in the finance committee packet once a year. Show the major cost pools, the basis used for each one, and any method changes. If program ratios moved because of a method change, say so directly. If ratios moved because activity changed, say that too.

This protects leadership from false stories. A change in program expense percentage may reflect a real shift in activity, a new facility, or a better allocation method. The board should not have to guess which one happened.
