A corrective action plan is not a place for vague promises. It is a work plan for fixing the finding.

2 CFR 200.511 requires an auditee to prepare a corrective action plan for each audit finding. The plan should name the contact person, the action planned, and the expected completion date.

The best responses are calm and specific. They do not argue with the auditor unless there is a real factual reason. They also do not say "management will review procedures" and stop there.

## Step 1: break down the finding

Read the finding in parts. Identify the condition, criteria, cause, effect, questioned costs, and recommendation.

The condition is what happened. The criteria is the rule or policy that was not met. The cause explains why. The effect explains the risk or result.

If the finding is still in draft form, check facts quickly. If dates, amounts, or process details are wrong, send support to the auditor before the report is final.

## Step 2: decide whether you agree

Management can agree, partly agree, or disagree. If you disagree, explain why and cite support. Keep the tone factual.

Do not disagree just because the finding is uncomfortable. Also do not agree to facts that are wrong.

If the finding is valid, say what will change. The response should focus on the fix.

## Step 3: find the root cause

The root cause is usually not "staff error." Ask why the error happened.

Common root causes include no checklist, unclear owner, weak review, missing training, old policy, system access problems, poor file naming, or a deadline that lives in one inbox.

The action plan should address that root cause. If the finding came from a missing procurement file, the fix may include a procurement checklist, approval gate, file review, and staff training. See [test a procurement file before audit](/workflows/test-procurement-file-before-audit) for one example.

## Step 4: write the action

The action should be concrete. Use plain verbs: update, assign, train, review, reconcile, test, approve, save.

A strong action names the control. For example: "Finance will add a monthly grant cash reconciliation checklist. The controller will review and sign it by the tenth business day."

That is stronger than: "Finance will improve oversight."

## Step 5: name the owner and date

Every action needs one owner and one expected completion date. A team can help, but one person owns the result.

Choose a realistic date. If the fix needs board approval, system changes, or policy review, say so.

If the finding has multiple actions, list each one with its own owner and date.

## Step 6: define proof of completion

Decide what evidence will prove the fix happened. This may include an updated policy, training roster, signed checklist, system report, reconciliation packet, or sample test.

Save that proof in one place. The next auditor or pass-through reviewer may ask for it.

This proof also helps leadership track whether the issue is truly closed.

## Step 7: track until resolved

Add the plan to a tracker. Review it at least monthly until complete. If an action misses its date, update the tracker and explain why.

After implementation, test the new control. If the same issue appears again, the action did not fix the root cause.

Connect the plan to the [audit readiness guide](/resources/guides/nonprofit-audit-readiness) and the [SEFA support binder](/workflows/build-sefa-support-binder) so audit prep reflects the lesson learned.

## Keep the tone factual

A corrective action plan is read by auditors, pass-through entities, federal agencies, and sometimes board members. Keep the tone clear and factual.

Do not blame one staff member. If one person made the mistake, still ask what control should have caught it. Good plans fix systems, not just people.

Do not overpromise either. If the fix will take three months, say that. If a system change depends on a vendor, say that. A realistic plan is stronger than a fast promise the team cannot meet.

## Prepare for follow-up

Many findings come back during the next audit if the organization cannot prove the fix worked. Plan the follow-up now.

Decide who will test the new control, what sample they will review, and when they will report the result. Save the test evidence with the plan.

If the finding involved questioned costs, track the agency decision and any repayment, adjustment, or appeal. The plan is not complete until both the control fix and the cost issue are resolved.
