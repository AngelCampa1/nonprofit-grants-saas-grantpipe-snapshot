A grant go no go decision protects staff time. It also protects the organization from awards that cost more to manage than they provide.

The decision should happen before writing starts. Once a proposal is in motion, it gets harder to say no. Staff may keep working because the deadline is close or because the funder name looks good. A simple scorecard makes the choice less personal.

This workflow is for nonprofit operating decisions. It does not replace funder rules, legal review, or accounting advice. Use it to surface risk early.

## Step 1: create the opportunity record

Start with one place for the opportunity. Include the funder, program name, due date, amount, grant period, link to the notice, and staff owner.

Add the core reason the grant is being considered. A good reason is specific. "Funds our after-school tutoring program for the next school year" is useful. "Looks like a good opportunity" is not enough.

GrantPipe can keep the opportunity, deadlines, funder notes, budget comments, and later award tasks connected. That helps the team see the full path from prospect to report.

## Step 2: check eligibility

Read the applicant rules first. Check nonprofit status, location, service area, program type, registration, past award limits, partner rules, and required attachments.

If the organization is not eligible, stop. Do not rely on a workaround unless the funder confirms it in writing.

If eligibility is unclear, mark the decision as pause. Assign someone to ask the funder. Save the answer with the opportunity record.

## Step 3: score mission and program fit

Ask whether the grant funds work the organization already plans to do. A strong grant supports the mission without forcing a program into a shape that staff do not believe in.

Use plain scores:

- Strong fit
- Possible fit
- Weak fit

Write one sentence for the score. If the score is weak, do not bury it. A weak fit can lead to vague outcomes, odd budget lines, and hard reports.

## Step 4: test the budget

Finance should review the budget range before writing starts. Check salary, fringe, supplies, travel, partner costs, equipment, indirect costs, and unrecovered costs.

Ask whether the grant pays up front or by reimbursement. A reimbursement grant may create a cash gap. Ask whether the organization can carry the cost until payment arrives.

Also check whether indirect costs are allowed. If the grant limits indirect costs, leadership should know the true cost before approval.

## Step 5: review match and cost share

If the grant needs match, name the source. Do not list match as a general promise.

For cash match, identify the fund or budget line. For in-kind match, name the source record and valuation method. For volunteer time, check the funder rules before relying on it.

If the team cannot support the match, the decision should be no or pause.

## Step 6: check reporting burden

Read the reporting section. Look for financial reports, outcome reports, demographic data, site visits, reimbursement packets, closeout files, and audit requests.

Then ask who will do the work. A small grant can still be a bad fit if reports require data the program does not collect.

Be direct about capacity. If the same person must write, manage, report, and reconcile the grant, record that risk.

## Step 7: check restrictions and systems

Ask how the money will be tracked if awarded. The answer may be a grant code, class, fund, project, or other field. It should be clear before submission.

Also ask where award files will live. The team should know where to save the notice, budget, agreement, reports, amendments, and funder emails.

If the team cannot separate restricted funds from general funds, the grant may create reporting risk.

## Step 8: choose go, pause, or no

Use three decisions.

Go means the team will write the proposal now.

Pause means the grant may fit, but one or more gaps need answers first.

No means the team will not pursue it.

Do not treat no as failure. A clear no saves time. It also helps the team focus on funders that fit the work.

## Step 9: save the decision

Save the scorecard, decision, approver, date, and notes. If the team chooses go with risk, write the risk. Examples include tight match, low indirect cost recovery, hard reporting, or short timeline.

If the team chooses no, record the reason. Later, this history can show patterns. Maybe the organization keeps finding grants with match it cannot support. Maybe it needs better data before applying for outcome based funds.

The best decision record is short. It should help the next person understand why the team acted.
