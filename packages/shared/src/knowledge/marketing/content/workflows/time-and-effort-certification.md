steps:
  - title: "Identify all employees with federal grant charges"
    content: "Pull a list of every employee whose salary or wages were charged to a federal award during the certification period. Include employees who split time between federal awards, between federal and non-federal activities, and employees fully funded by a single award. This list drives the certification requirement - if the name is on the payroll allocation, a certification is required."
  - title: "Determine the appropriate certification frequency for each employee"
    content: "Under 2 CFR 200.430(i), employees working solely on a single federal award may use semi-annual certification - every six months. All other employees - those on multiple federal awards, or whose time is split between federal and non-federal activities - must certify more frequently. The regulation does not specify monthly for the latter group, but the after-the-fact confirmation principle is most reliably satisfied with monthly certification. Quarterly creates too much opportunity for inaccurate recall."
  - title: "Prepare the certification forms or records"
    content: "A time-and-effort certification must, at minimum, reflect an after-the-fact distribution of the employee's actual activities, be signed by the employee or a responsible supervisory official with firsthand knowledge of the work performed, and cover the specific period. The certification can be a simple time sheet, a signed activity report, or a dedicated certification form - the format is flexible, but the content and signature are not."
  - title: "Distribute certifications to employees for completion"
    content: "Send certification forms to employees promptly after each pay period or month-end closes. The longer the gap between the period and the certification request, the less reliable the employee's recall of time distribution. Best practice is to distribute within two weeks of period end and set a one-week deadline for return. Chasing certifications after 60 days produces responses that are guesses, not after-the-fact records."
  - title: "Collect and review completed certifications"
    content: "Collect completed certifications and review for completeness before filing: every employee on the list has a certification, the period is correctly identified, the signature is present, and the time percentages add to 100% (or correctly account for any non-grant time). An unsigned or undated certification is not compliant. Do not accept certifications that state only the employee name and grant number without any indication of actual time distribution."
  - title: "Reconcile certification percentages to posted payroll"
    content: "Compare the time distribution on each employee's certification to the payroll allocation percentage posted to the general ledger for the same period. They must match. If an employee's certification shows 50% on Grant A and the payroll was posted at 55%, the posting is incorrect by 5%. Document the comparison and initiate a correcting payroll entry for the difference. This reconciliation step is the control that makes the entire system work."
  - title: "Process any payroll allocation corrections"
    content: "For any employee where the certification percentage does not match the posted allocation, prepare and post a correcting journal entry. The entry must cite the time record supporting the correction. Notify the grants manager of corrections that affect budget-vs-actual comparisons - a multi-month series of corrections in the same direction suggests the allocation estimate was systematically off and may need revision."
  - title: "File certifications in audit-ready order"
    content: "File completed certifications in a way that supports rapid audit sampling: by employee, by period, clearly labeled. Auditors testing time-and-effort compliance will request certifications for a sample of employees and pay periods. An auditor who cannot locate the certification for a sampled employee is likely to expand the sample or issue a finding for the missing documentation. Organized filing prevents that outcome."
faqs:
  - q: "What is the after-the-fact confirmation principle?"
    a: "Under 2 CFR 200.430, payroll charges to federal awards must reflect actual time worked - confirmed after the fact by the employee or a supervisor with direct knowledge. The principle prohibits using budgeted or projected time distributions as the basis for payroll charges without subsequent reconciliation to actual time. Budget-based allocations that are never reconciled to actual time violate this principle."
  - q: "Can a supervisor sign time-and-effort certifications instead of the employee?"
    a: "Yes, under 2 CFR 200.430(i). The supervisor must have direct knowledge of the work performed - not just awareness of the assignment. A supervisor who was not present and did not directly observe the work cannot reliably certify based on firsthand knowledge. In practice, self-certification by the employee is more defensible and easier to explain to auditors."
  - q: "What if an employee refuses to certify their time?"
    a: "Any payroll charges to federal awards without a supporting time certification are questioned costs. The organization is responsible for ensuring certifications are completed. Management should establish a clear policy and escalation path for employees who do not submit certifications on schedule. Chronic non-compliance by specific employees is a compliance risk that management must address, not work around."
  - q: "Do we need certifications for employees on indirect cost pools?"
    a: "No - indirect cost certifications are handled through the indirect cost rate proposal and negotiation process, not through individual time certifications. Employees whose time is in the indirect pool are not charging direct costs to federal awards; their certifications are not required under 2 CFR 200.430. Verify which employees are in the direct pool versus the indirect pool."
  - q: "What is the difference between a time sheet and a time-and-effort certification?"
    a: "A time sheet records hours worked on specific days. A time-and-effort certification (also called an effort report or activity report) distributes an employee's total time across cost objectives as a percentage. Both are compliant if properly signed and completed. The percentage-based activity report is more common in nonprofits with complex multi-award environments; the detailed time sheet is more common in research settings."

Time-and-effort certification is the documentation control that makes payroll allocation credible. Without it, every salary charge to a federal award is an assertion unsupported by evidence. With it, the allocation has a paper trail an auditor can test. The certification workflow itself is not complicated - it is the discipline of running it every month that most organizations struggle with.

## When to run this workflow

Run this workflow each month, aligned to your payroll and grant close cycle. Certifications should be distributed to employees within two weeks of month-end and returned within a week of distribution. The entire cycle - distribution, completion, collection, and reconciliation to payroll - should close before you submit any monthly financial progress reports to funders.

For employees on semi-annual certification (those working 100% on a single award), run the cycle at the six-month marks of the grant year and at fiscal year-end. Confirm annually that the list of semi-annual certifiers still qualifies - an employee who picked up a small second-award assignment no longer meets the single-award criterion.

## Common pitfalls

**Applying semi-annual certification to employees who do not qualify.** An employee working 80% on one federal award and 20% on administrative duties is not a single-award employee - the 20% is a separate cost objective. Monthly certification is required. Auditors know this rule and test for it by looking at how employees are classified and whether the certification frequency matches.

**Distributing certifications late.** Certifications requested 90 days after the period ends are not credible after-the-fact records. They are reconstructions from memory, payroll reports, and email review. Auditors can tell the difference. Build the distribution into the close calendar so it happens automatically within two weeks of period end.

**Filing certifications without reconciling to payroll.** The certification and the payroll allocation must agree. Many organizations collect certifications and file them without comparing the percentages to what was posted. The comparison is the control - the filing without comparison provides no assurance.

**Accepting certifications that cover the wrong period or lack a date.** A certification for "April" that does not specify the year, or one that covers "the grant period" rather than a specific calendar period, is not compliant. Certifications must identify the specific period covered and be signed by the certifier with a date.

## Audit trail requirements

For each pay period or month, maintain:

- A complete list of employees with federal grant charges for the period
- Completed and signed certifications for each employee, covering the correct period
- The reconciliation worksheet comparing certification percentages to posted payroll allocations
- Any correcting journal entries with the supporting certification basis
- Notation if any employee's certification was late, with the date received

Store by employee and by period so auditors can sample in either dimension. A common audit approach: select a sample of employees, request all certifications for the audit year; then select a sample of months, request all certifications across all employees. Your filing system must support both.
