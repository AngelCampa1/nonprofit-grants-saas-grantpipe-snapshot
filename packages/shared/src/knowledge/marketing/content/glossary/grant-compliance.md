Grant compliance is the accountability structure for public and philanthropic trust. Grants are funds given for a specific purpose, under specific conditions, with the expectation that the recipient will demonstrate responsible stewardship. Compliance is how that demonstration happens - through documentation, reporting, and the ongoing systems that connect spending to purpose.

## Federal vs. Private Funder Compliance

The compliance frameworks differ significantly by funder type.

**Federal grants** are governed by 2 CFR Part 200 (Uniform Guidance), which establishes standardized requirements across all federal agencies. The Uniform Guidance covers cost principles (what is allowable and how to document it), administrative requirements (financial management, procurement, property), and audit requirements (the single audit threshold of $1,000,000 in federal expenditures, the reporting timeline, and the Federal Audit Clearinghouse). On top of the Uniform Guidance, each federal program has its own requirements described in the OMB Compliance Supplement.

**State pass-through grants** bring both the state's own requirements and the underlying federal program requirements. Organizations receiving state funds that originated as federal awards are bound by 2 CFR Part 200 even if the state is the immediate funder.

**Private foundation grants** are governed by the grant agreement. Requirements vary widely by foundation. Most foundations require narrative and financial reporting, restrictions on how funds are spent, and some form of record retention. Very few conduct compliance audits in the way federal agencies do, but foundations share information among themselves and foundation grants can become the subject of IRS scrutiny in an audit of the nonprofit's form 990.

## The Key Compliance Areas

Across all grant types, the core compliance areas are:

**Restricted use of funds.** The grant was given for a specific purpose. Funds may only be spent on activities and costs that directly support that purpose, within the approved budget categories.

**Financial reporting.** Funders require periodic and final financial reports demonstrating how funds were spent. For federal grants, the SF-425 is the standard form. The report must reconcile to the accounting system and must be submitted on time.

**Documentation.** Every expenditure must be supported by documentation that could allow an independent person to verify what was purchased, that it was necessary for the program, and that it was a reasonable cost.

**Personnel cost documentation.** When salaries are charged to a grant, there must be evidence - contemporaneous records, not reconstructed estimates - that the charged time reflects actual work on the grant.

**Record retention.** Grant records must be retained for a specified period after closeout. For federal grants, the minimum is three years from submission of the final expenditure report.
