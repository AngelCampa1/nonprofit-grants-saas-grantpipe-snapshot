# Nonprofit grant readiness self assessment

A grant can help fund work that already has a plan. It can also expose weak records fast. A self assessment helps a nonprofit see the gap before a funder asks for budgets, reports, policies, or proof.

Use this assessment before you write a proposal. It is not a legal or accounting opinion. It is a working check for leaders who need to know whether the team can apply, accept, track, and report on a grant.

## Check basic eligibility

Start with the funder rules. A nonprofit may need active tax exempt status, state registration, a SAM.gov record, a Unique Entity ID, a board list, recent financial statements, or proof that it serves a specific place or group.

Record the answer to each question:

- Is the organization eligible for this funder?
- Is the nonprofit in good standing with the IRS and state?
- Does the award require federal registration?
- Does the work fit the funder's stated purpose?
- Can the organization meet the deadline?
- Can the board accept the terms if funded?

If any answer is unclear, pause. A strong program idea does not fix an eligibility problem.

## Review finance records

Finance readiness is more than having a budget. The nonprofit needs records that show how grant money will move.

Check whether finance can show:

- Current chart of accounts
- Fund or project codes
- Recent bank reconciliations
- Prior year financial statements
- Current operating budget
- Payroll allocation method
- Approval rules for expenses
- Process for reimbursement grants
- Method for tracking restricted funds

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.302 describes financial management standards. The system should be able to identify awards, source and use of funds, obligations, unobligated balances, assets, expenses, and records that support charges.

Small teams can meet this standard with simple tools if the process is clear. The problem is not size. The problem is when the nonprofit cannot trace a cost from grant budget to invoice, payment, report, and file.

## Test program readiness

A proposal should be built on work the team can do and measure.

Ask the program owner:

- What will change because of the grant?
- Who will be served?
- How will people be counted?
- What records prove service happened?
- What outcomes will be reported?
- Who owns the data?
- How often will data be reviewed?

Do not wait until the first report to define these rules. If staff count clients one way in case notes and another way in the funder report, the team will spend time cleaning data instead of managing the program.

## Review budget and match capacity

A grant budget should fit the work and the rules. If the grant requires cost share or match, the source should be real and trackable before the proposal is sent.

Check each budget line for:

- Need
- Amount
- Basis for the estimate
- Allowability
- Grant period
- Match source if used
- Staff owner
- Evidence needed later

For federal grants, costs generally need to be necessary, reasonable, allocable, consistent, and supported. Funder rules can be stricter. Build the budget with those rules in view.

## Score reporting capacity

Many nonprofits focus on winning the grant. The harder work starts after award.

A ready team can answer:

- Who reads the award terms?
- Who sets report dates?
- Who tracks spending?
- Who collects program data?
- Who reviews reports before submission?
- Who saves proof of submission?
- Who handles funder questions?

If the answer to several questions is the same overloaded person, record that as a risk. The fix may be a calendar, a weekly handoff, or clearer ownership.

## Build the grant file before award

Create a folder before submission. Save the funder notice, draft budget, final budget, budget notes, narrative, required attachments, board approvals, and submission proof.

If the award is funded, add the award letter, contract, reporting dates, budget revisions, reimbursement files, and closeout records. GrantPipe can help connect these records to grants, funds, tasks, and compliance dates so staff do not have to hunt through email.

## Use a simple readiness score

Score each area from 1 to 3.

1 means not ready. The team lacks required records or ownership.

2 means partly ready. The team has the pieces but needs cleanup.

3 means ready. The team can show the record and name the owner.

Score these areas:

- Eligibility
- Finance records
- Program plan
- Outcome data
- Budget support
- Match tracking
- Grant file
- Reporting calendar
- Staff capacity
- Board awareness

Do not average the score and move on. Look for any area scored 1. That is the work to fix before applying.

## Decide what to do next

Use the assessment to make a short action list. Assign one owner and due date to each gap.

Good next steps sound like this:

- Finance will add project codes before the proposal is submitted.
- Program will define client counting rules by Friday.
- Grants will create the grant file and save the final instructions.
- The executive director will confirm board approval rules.

Grant readiness is not about perfect systems. It is about knowing what the funder will ask for and being able to answer with records the team trusts.
