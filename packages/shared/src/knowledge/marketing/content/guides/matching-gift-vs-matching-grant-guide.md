# Matching gift vs matching grant guide

Matching gifts and matching grants both involve more than one source of support. That is where the similarity ends.

A matching gift often starts with a donor. A donor gives, and an employer, sponsor, or campaign funder matches that gift. A matching grant often starts with an award agreement. The nonprofit must raise, spend, or document other resources to meet the grant terms.

The difference matters because the records, deadlines, restricted funds, and reporting work can be very different.

## What a matching gift is

A matching gift is usually tied to a contribution. Common examples include:

- Employer matching gift programs
- Board challenge matches
- Major donor challenge gifts
- Campaign matches
- Event matches

The donor CRM should show the donor, gift, match source, expected match amount, status, and follow-up tasks. Finance should show the gift record, receipt support, restrictions, and deposit.

IRS charitable contribution substantiation rules may apply to donor acknowledgments. This guide is not tax advice. Use the IRS guidance and your advisor for receipt questions.

## What a matching grant is

A matching grant is usually tied to an award. The funder agrees to provide support if the nonprofit meets certain match terms.

The match may be:

- Cash
- In-kind goods
- Volunteer time
- Donated space
- Partner costs
- Other non-federal funds

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.306 covers cost sharing and matching. The award terms matter. Some match must be verifiable from records. Some sources cannot be counted. Some match must be necessary and reasonable for the work.

## Compare the work

Use this simple distinction:

- Matching gift: donor and gift workflow first.
- Matching grant: award and compliance workflow first.

A matching gift may need campaign tracking and donor follow-up. A matching grant may need budget review, restricted fund tracking, source records, reporting deadlines, and audit evidence.

Both can affect finance. Both can be restricted. Both should be reviewed before staff promise anything public or submit a report.

## Watch restriction language

Do not assume a match is unrestricted. Read the donor note, campaign terms, employer program rules, grant agreement, or funder instructions.

Ask:

- Can funds be used for any purpose?
- Must funds support a named program?
- Is there a time limit?
- Is the match conditional?
- What happens if the match target is not met?
- What records prove the match?

If the funds are restricted, finance should track them as restricted funds. Development should still keep relationship history in the donor CRM.

## Track matching gifts

For matching gifts, track:

- Original donor
- Original gift amount
- Match source
- Expected match amount
- Request date
- Approval status
- Payment status
- Restriction
- Receipt or acknowledgment status

Do not count a pending match as received cash. Keep pledge, receivable, and contribution treatment aligned with your accounting policy and professional guidance.

## Track matching grants

For matching grants, track:

- Award amount
- Match amount
- Match ratio
- Eligible match sources
- Ineligible sources
- Match deadline
- Report deadline
- Source records
- Valuation support
- Finance reviewer

If in-kind match is allowed, save the valuation basis. If volunteer time is allowed, save time records and rate support. If another grant is used as match, confirm it is allowed.

## Avoid double counting

The same dollar should not be promised as match for multiple awards unless all rules clearly allow it. The same volunteer hour should not be counted twice.

Create a match register with:

- Source
- Amount
- Award or campaign used for
- Date
- Evidence
- Reviewer

This protects the nonprofit when reports overlap.

GrantPipe can help connect donor CRM records, grant compliance requirements, restricted funds, deadlines, and audit evidence. That matters when a match starts in development but ends in a grant report.

## Pick the right owner

Development should usually own matching gift follow-up. Grants or finance should usually own matching grant compliance. Program staff may own service records or in-kind support.

Write the owner down. Match work crosses teams, so unclear ownership is a common failure point.

The safest approach is simple: read the terms, record the source, track the restriction, save the evidence, and report only what the records support.
