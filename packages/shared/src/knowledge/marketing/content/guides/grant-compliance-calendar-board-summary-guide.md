A grant compliance calendar is useful to staff. A board summary needs a different job. It should help the board see whether grant obligations are under control.

Board members do not need every task. They need deadlines that affect compliance, cash, renewals, audit risk, or board decisions. They also need a plain view of who owns the work and what needs attention.

## Start with the board's role

The board is not there to manage each report. It is there to govern. That means oversight, risk review, financial health, and decisions that require board approval.

A good summary filters the calendar through that role. It shows the board where the organization is on track and where leadership needs support or action.

The IRS compliance guide for public charities points boards toward active oversight. Grant compliance is one place where that oversight can be concrete.

## Use a one page summary

Keep the summary short. A strong format includes:

- reports due in the next 30 to 90 days
- late or at risk items
- grants with cash or match concerns
- budget variance notes
- decisions needed from the board
- owner and next action

Put the full calendar in the staff system. The board summary should be a decision tool, not a task dump.

## Show deadlines by risk

Group deadlines by status. Use simple labels like on track, watch, late, and needs board action.

A deadline due next week is not always risky if the report is done. A deadline due in two months may be risky if program data is missing.

The status should reflect readiness, not only the due date.

## Connect dates to owners

Every board-level item should have an owner. The owner may be the grants manager, finance lead, program director, or executive director.

Do not write "staff" as the owner. That hides accountability. The board needs to know who is driving the next step.

If an item has no owner, mark it as a risk. Missing ownership is often the real problem behind late grant reports.

## Include report and cash effects

Grant deadlines often affect money. A late reimbursement request can slow cash. A missed report can delay payment. A budget variance can affect renewal.

Show the cash effect when it matters. For example, "Q2 reimbursement request due July 31. About $42,000 in costs depends on timely submission."

Finance should review these notes before the board packet goes out.

## Escalate match and restriction risk

Boards need to know when match, cost share, or restricted fund rules are at risk. These items can become audit findings or funding problems if ignored.

Use plain language. "Match documentation is short by $8,500" is better than "match tracking needs improvement."

For federal grants, performance reports and financial controls connect to 2 CFR 200.329 and 200.303. Private grants may use different terms, but the risk pattern is similar.

## Keep detail available

The board summary should link back to detail. That may include the report calendar, budget-to-actual, risk register, fund balance report, or grant file.

In GrantPipe, the board summary can be built from grant report dates, status, owners, and fund records so the board packet reflects current work.

Avoid copying data by hand when possible. Hand copying creates version problems.

## Add management notes

For each risk item, add a short management note. Use this format:

- issue
- effect
- next action
- owner
- date

Example: "The youth program report is at risk because attendance data from two sites is incomplete. Program will finish the review by July 12. Grants will submit by July 25."

That gives the board enough detail to ask useful questions.

## Name board decisions

Some items need board action. Examples include approving a policy, accepting a grant with match, authorizing a budget change, approving a contract, or reviewing a risk response.

Put these items in a separate section. Include the decision date and what happens if the decision is delayed.

Do not hide board decisions in the calendar table.

## Review the summary before the packet

Before the board packet goes out, review the summary with grants, finance, and the executive director. Check due dates, owners, amounts, status, and decisions.

After the meeting, save the final summary and any board actions in the grant file. The summary then becomes evidence of oversight, not only a meeting handout.
