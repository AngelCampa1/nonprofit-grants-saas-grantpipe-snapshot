# Grant renewal risk review guide

A renewal request should not start with a blank proposal. It should start with the truth about the current award.

Before asking for continued support, review whether the nonprofit did what it promised, spent funds as planned, met deadlines, and kept evidence. This is not about hiding problems. It is about finding them early enough to fix or explain them.

Use this review 90 to 120 days before a renewal ask. Use it sooner for large awards, federal funds, or grants with late reports.

## Start with the award record

Pull the current grant record before drafting the renewal. Do not rely on memory.

Gather:

- Award letter or agreement
- Approved budget
- Report schedule
- Submitted reports
- Budget changes
- Funder notes
- Program outcome data
- Expense detail
- Reimbursement status
- Evidence folder

If the file is split across email, shared drives, accounting, and a donor CRM, list each source. That list shows where renewal work may stall.

GrantPipe can help connect donor CRM history, grant compliance tasks, restricted funds, deadlines, audit evidence, and reporting work. The review still needs human judgment, but the record should not be scattered.

## Review promised outcomes

Compare the proposal, award terms, and reports. What did the nonprofit promise? What has happened so far?

Build a simple table:

- Promised outcome
- Current result
- Source record
- Owner
- Status
- Explanation needed

Use source records, not broad claims. If the grant promised 100 workshop participants, the source might be attendance logs. If the grant promised follow-up coaching, the source might be case notes or service records.

Do not turn weak data into confident language. If the data is incomplete, say that internally. Then decide whether to fix the data, change the renewal scope, or explain the limit.

## Check spending and restricted funds

A funder may like the program and still worry about the money. Review the budget to actual report before drafting.

Ask finance:

- How much has been spent?
- How much remains?
- Are costs posted to the right grant?
- Are restricted funds reconciled?
- Are reimbursements current?
- Are any costs waiting on approval?
- Are any costs outside the grant period?
- Will the grant close over or under budget?

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.302 covers financial management records, and 2 CFR 200.329 covers performance reporting. Private funders may have their own terms. In either case, renewal planning should include clean budget status.

If spending is low, ask whether work is delayed or whether costs are missing from the record. If spending is high, ask whether the team needs a budget revision, unrestricted support, or a smaller renewal scope.

## Review deadlines and report history

Late reports create renewal risk. Missing reports create larger risk.

Check:

- Interim reports submitted
- Final report due date
- Financial reports submitted
- Performance reports submitted
- Attachments sent
- Funder questions answered
- Site visits completed

If a report was late, write the reason and the fix. A renewal memo should not pretend the issue did not happen. It should show that the team knows what changed.

## Review the funder relationship

Open the donor CRM or relationship record. Look for calls, emails, visits, feedback, and board connections.

Ask:

- Who is the main funder contact?
- When was the last contact?
- What feedback did the funder give?
- Did staff share wins during the year?
- Did the funder raise concerns?
- Is there a known strategy shift?
- Who should make the renewal ask?

Relationship notes should be factual. Do not write guesses about what a funder thinks. Write what was said, who said it, and when.

## Name open risks

Make a short risk list. Use plain language.

Examples:

- Report was submitted 10 days late.
- Outcome data is missing for March.
- Spending is 42 percent with two months left.
- Payroll allocation support is incomplete.
- Funder contact changed in May.
- Program scope changed after a staff vacancy.

Then add the next step, owner, and due date. A risk without an owner is only a note.

## Decide the renewal ask

The renewal ask should match the evidence. If the current grant went well, the request may stay the same or grow. If the grant had delays, the request may need a tighter scope.

Review:

- Requested amount
- Program scope
- Budget changes
- Outcome targets
- Reporting plan
- Match or other funding
- Staff capacity

Do not promise more outcomes just to make the request sound stronger. A renewal built on unsupported promises creates future reporting problems.

## Write the internal renewal memo

Keep the memo short enough for leadership to read.

Include:

- One paragraph on current grant status
- One paragraph on outcomes
- One paragraph on budget and restricted funds
- One paragraph on relationship status
- Risk list
- Recommended renewal ask
- Open decisions

Attach the budget report, outcome evidence, and report history. The executive director should not have to search for proof.

A strong renewal process helps the nonprofit ask with clarity. It also helps the team protect the next grant from the same issues that made the current one hard.
