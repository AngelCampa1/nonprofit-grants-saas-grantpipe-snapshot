# Grant application go no go checklist

A grant opportunity can look good at first glance. The deadline is close. The award amount fits a budget gap. The funder name feels useful. That is when teams need a go no go check.

The point is not to make the process slow. The point is to avoid chasing grants that cost more than they help. Use this checklist before staff write the full proposal.

## Start with mission fit

Ask whether the work is already part of the plan. A grant should fund the mission, not pull the nonprofit into side work.

Use these questions:

- Does the funder support work we already do or plan to do?
- Does the award fit our service area?
- Does the target population match our program?
- Would we still want this work if the grant were smaller?
- Would this work harm another program by taking staff time?

If the answer is weak, stop before budget work begins. A poor fit can lead to strained programs, weak reports, and costs that are hard to support.

## Confirm eligibility

Read the funder instructions. Do not rely on a short summary or an old grant listing.

Check:

- Entity type
- Tax status
- Location
- Program type
- Minimum or maximum budget
- Prior award rules
- Required registrations
- Required partners
- Board or audit requirements

For federal opportunities, applicants may need registration steps before submission. Grants.gov explains the applicant registration process. If registration is missing and the deadline is close, the opportunity may need to wait.

## Review the money

Finance should review the budget rules before the proposal team invests time.

Ask:

- Is the award amount worth the work?
- Does it pay for staff time?
- Are indirect costs allowed?
- Is match required?
- Is the grant paid as an advance or reimbursement?
- Can the nonprofit carry costs before payment?
- Are any cost categories banned?
- Will the grant require a new fund or project code?

For federal awards, 2 CFR Part 200 cost rules matter. Costs should be allowable, reasonable, allocable, and supported. Some funders add tighter rules.

## Test match and cost share

Do not say yes to match because it sounds doable. A match promise becomes a tracking job.

For each match source, name:

- Source
- Amount
- Cash or in-kind
- Valuation method
- Owner
- Proof
- Timing
- Restrictions

If the team cannot name the source and proof, mark the opportunity as high risk.

## Check reporting work

A grant is not done when the money arrives. Read the reporting rules before applying.

Look for:

- Financial reports
- Program reports
- Client counts
- Outcome data
- Backup documents
- Site visits
- Audit rights
- Closeout report
- Record retention period

Ask the program team if the data exists now. If not, ask whether the team can collect it without changing the program in a harmful way.

## Check staffing and timing

Proposal work has a cost. Management work has a cost too.

Name the owner for each phase:

- Application
- Budget
- Attachments
- Submission
- Award intake
- Spending review
- Reporting
- Closeout

Then check the calendar. If the deadline conflicts with audit prep, year end close, a major event, or another large proposal, decide what gets paused.

## Look for approval needs

Some grants need board approval, executive approval, partner signatures, or finance committee review. Some awards require the nonprofit to accept terms that affect insurance, data sharing, cash flow, or record access.

Do not wait until the final day to ask for signatures. A go decision should include the approval path.

## Use a simple decision note

Write a short note for every opportunity, even the ones you decline. This keeps the grant pipeline clean.

Include:

- Opportunity name
- Funder
- Amount
- Deadline
- Program fit
- Eligibility result
- Budget risk
- Match risk
- Reporting burden
- Staff owner
- Decision
- Reason

GrantPipe can help keep this decision history tied to the grant pipeline, calendar, documents, and tasks. That matters when leaders ask why a team skipped one grant and pursued another.

## Decide yes, no, or wait

Use three final choices.

Go means the opportunity fits, the team can apply, and the organization can manage the award if funded.

No go means the opportunity does not fit or carries risk the team should not accept.

Wait means the opportunity may fit later, but a missing record, registration, partner, budget plan, or approval blocks this cycle.

Strong grant teams say no often. That is not a lack of ambition. It is how they protect staff time and keep funded work clean.
