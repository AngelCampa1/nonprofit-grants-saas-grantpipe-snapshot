# Grant deadline calendar buying checklist

The broader grant calendar requirements guide defines what a grant calendar should track. This checklist is for the buying moment: demos, vendor questions, user acceptance testing, and the final go or no-go decision.

A grant deadline calendar fails when it behaves like a personal reminder list. Grant work involves funder terms, finance data, program data, documents, review steps, and proof of submission. The buying process should test that work, not only ask whether the product has reminders.

Use this with the [grant calendar system requirements guide](/resources/guides/grant-calendar-system-requirements-guide) and the [quarterly grant calendar workflow](/workflows/build-quarterly-grant-report-calendar).

## Link each deadline to an award

Every deadline should belong to a grant or proposal record. The calendar item should show funder, award title, award period, report type, owner, status, and required files.

If the calendar item stands alone, staff still need to search for the award terms. That search wastes time and creates mistakes.

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.329 covers performance reporting. The software should make those report tasks visible as part of post-award management.

## Track funder dates and internal dates

The funder due date is the last date. It is not the only date.

Useful fields include:

- funder due date
- internal review date
- finance data due date
- program data due date
- leadership review date
- submission date
- closeout date

Internal dates protect the team. A report due on Friday may need finance data two weeks earlier.

## Assign one owner

Each deadline needs one owner. Other staff may provide data or review, but one person should be accountable for moving the task.

Add supporting roles for finance, program, development, and leadership. These roles help staff know who needs to act without turning ownership into a group label.

If ownership changes, the calendar should keep the old note and show the new owner.

## Show status without jargon

A calendar should use plain status values:

- not started
- gathering data
- drafting
- under review
- ready to submit
- submitted
- accepted
- late

Avoid private shorthand. A new grants manager or executive director should be able to scan the calendar and know where help is needed.

## Attach or link required documents

A deadline record should point to the files needed to complete the report. That may include the award letter, approved budget, prior report, expense backup, outcome data, and portal instructions.

It should also store proof after submission. A saved receipt or confirmation email can prevent audit and renewal problems later.

Record retention matters. Under 2 CFR 200.334, many federal award records are kept for three years after final report submission, with exceptions. Deadline software should not make old proof hard to find.

## Support useful alerts

Alerts should arrive early enough for staff to act. Same-day reminders are too late for most grant reports.

Use a default alert pattern, then allow exceptions. A routine quarterly report may need 30, 14, 7, and 1 day alerts. A board-reviewed report may need 60 days. A closeout task may need alerts tied to final invoices and obligations.

Alerts should go to the owner and the supporting role when their part is due. Do not send every alert to everyone.

## Include renewal windows

Renewal work often starts before final reporting. The calendar should show renewal planning, funder meetings, draft dates, board approvals, and application windows.

This matters because performance reports and renewal asks affect each other. A late report can weaken a renewal. A renewal deadline can change how staff prioritize final data.

## Include closeout tasks

Grant closeout has its own deadline risk. 2 CFR 200.344 covers federal closeout and final reporting timing. The calendar should track final performance report, final financial report, final invoice cutoff, remaining balance review, and closeout letter.

Closed grants should remain searchable. Staff may need them for audits, renewal context, or board questions.

## Provide different views

One calendar view will not serve every user.

Grants staff need task detail. Finance needs financial report and reimbursement dates. Program staff need outcome and narrative dates. Leadership needs a risk view. Board treasurers may need a read-only summary.

Ask vendors to show these views using the same sample grant. If each view needs a manual export, the calendar may not be enough.

## Add escalation rules

Escalation should be clear and calm. A late internal draft may notify the grants manager. A report due in three days with no finance review may notify leadership.

The rule should show why the alert fired and what action is needed. Escalation without context just creates noise.

## Test with a real grant

Before choosing software, test one grant with a real report schedule. Enter the award, report dates, internal dates, owners, files, reminders, and proof of submission. Then ask each role to use its view.

The test should answer five questions. Can staff see what is due next? Can they find the support? Can finance see its dates? Can leadership see risk? Can the team prove submission later?
