Donor advised fund grants can move fast. A check or ACH arrives from a sponsor. The letter may mention an advising donor. The gift may be meant for a campaign, program, scholarship, or general support. If staff code it too quickly, the nonprofit can lose the restriction trail.

The safest rule is plain: read the sponsor letter before posting the gift. Then record both the donor relationship and the accounting treatment without mixing them up.

This guide is for nonprofit finance and development teams. It is not tax or legal advice. Use it as a review aid and confirm unusual gifts with your adviser.

## Identify the donor of record

For a donor advised fund grant, the sponsoring organization is usually the donor of record. The person who recommended the grant may be important for stewardship, but the nonprofit should follow the sponsor letter for receipt language and recognition rules.

That distinction matters. Development may want to thank the adviser. Finance needs to post the gift from the sponsor. The donor CRM should preserve both facts without turning the adviser into the tax donor for that grant.

## Read the restriction language

The sponsor letter may say the grant is unrestricted. It may also name a program, campaign, fund, or time period. Treat that language as the starting point for coding.

Do not assume a donor advised fund grant is unrestricted just because the sponsor is a public charity. Do not assume it is restricted because the advising donor has a history with a campaign. The document controls the first pass.

If the letter is unclear, ask the sponsor for clarification before posting a final restriction. Save the reply in the gift file.

## Match development notes to finance coding

Development records often hold useful context: the adviser name, relationship manager, campaign name, appeal code, soft credit, or public recognition preference. Finance records need the sponsor, amount, restriction, fund code, revenue account, and release plan.

Both views matter. A clean process gives each team what it needs without making either team guess.

Use a small intake checklist:

- Sponsor name
- Advising donor name if allowed
- Amount and date
- Sponsor grant ID if available
- Restriction language
- Fund and campaign code
- Receipt or acknowledgment rule
- Release trigger
- File owner

## Be careful with receipts and thanks

Many donor advised fund grants should not receive a second charitable tax receipt to the advising donor. The donor may have received a deduction when contributing to the fund. The nonprofit can still send a thank-you note when sponsor rules allow it.

Keep the wording simple. Thank the adviser for recommending support if the sponsor permits that. Thank the sponsor for the grant in the accounting record. Do not add unsupported tax language.

Save the acknowledgment copy. It becomes part of the audit evidence and donor history.

## Code the gift before deposit close

The best time to catch restrictions is before the deposit is closed. Once the cash hits a general revenue account, the correction can take more time.

Finance should review the deposit packet and assign the right fund. If a gift is split, show the split by amount. If part is unrestricted and part is restricted, write that clearly.

If the grant supports a government or foundation match, link it to the related grant record. A donor advised fund gift can affect deadlines, reporting, and match support.

## Plan the release

A restricted donor advised fund grant still needs release support. If the restriction is purpose based, release it when qualifying costs or activity support the release. If the restriction is time based, release it when the time period is met. If both apply, review both.

The release file should include the sponsor letter, ledger detail, qualifying costs or date support, approval, and journal entry.

Do not release the gift just because the budget needs unrestricted dollars. The release should follow the restriction.

## Report the balance plainly

Board and finance reports should make donor advised fund grants understandable. Show material restricted balances, recent receipts, expected releases, and open questions.

Avoid labels that hide the source. "DAF" may be clear to staff but not to a board member. Use "donor advised fund grant restricted for youth program" when space allows.

The Form 990 source and FASB not-for-profit reporting sources are useful reminders that public reporting and financial statement reporting have their own rules. The day-to-day control is still local: save the letter, code the gift, and prove the release.

## Watch for common failures

Common failures include using the adviser as the legal donor, sending the wrong receipt, missing the restriction, coding the whole gift to one fund when the letter splits it, and losing the sponsor letter after deposit.

Another failure is storing the relationship note apart from the finance record. That creates friction later when a donor asks how the grant was used or when finance prepares audit support.
