# Bank reconciliation audit evidence guide

Bank reconciliations are one of the first places auditors look because cash touches every part of the nonprofit. A clean reconciliation proves that the bank statement, general ledger, and timing items agree. It also shows whether someone reviewed the work before the audit.

For grant-funded nonprofits, cash can be more complex. One bank account may hold unrestricted cash, restricted funds, grant advances, and reimbursement receipts. The reconciliation needs to tie cash without pretending that bank balance equals restricted balance.

## Keep every month, not only year-end

Auditors may ask for all monthly reconciliations, not just the final month. Monthly records show whether cash controls worked during the year.

Each month should include:

- Bank statement
- Reconciliation report
- General ledger cash detail
- List of outstanding checks
- List of deposits in transit
- Adjusting entries
- Preparer signoff
- Reviewer signoff
- Review date

The review date matters. If all reconciliations are signed the week before fieldwork, the file may show that review happened late. Complete and review reconciliations during normal close.

## Tie bank balance to book balance

The reconciliation should show the path from the bank statement ending balance to the book cash balance. It should not be a screenshot with no support.

Check that the adjusted bank balance equals the adjusted book balance. If it does not, stop and fix the reconciliation before it goes to the auditor.

For each reconciling item, include enough detail to explain why it exists. "Misc difference" is not enough. Use specific labels such as outstanding check, deposit in transit, bank fee, interest, transfer timing, or correction entry.

## Review old items

Old reconciling items need attention. An outstanding check from many months ago may need follow-up. A deposit in transit that does not clear quickly may mean the date or amount is wrong. A recurring adjustment may point to a process problem.

Add an aging column for reconciling items. Use it to flag items older than the normal clearing period. Assign an owner and due date for follow-up.

Before audit fieldwork, review all old items. Keep notes that explain the result. If a check was voided after year-end, keep the void record. If a deposit cleared after year-end, keep the clearing proof.

## Connect cash to restricted funds

Restricted funds need a separate reconciliation. Cash is part of the story, but it may not equal the full restricted balance. A grant may have a receivable. A donor restriction may be partly spent but not yet released. A reimbursable grant may have expenses before cash arrives.

For audit evidence, link the bank reconciliation to:

- Restricted fund rollforward
- Grant receivable schedule
- Cash receipt detail
- Release entries
- Grant or donor agreement
- Board or funder reports

## Prepare bank confirmation support

Auditors may confirm bank balances directly with the bank. Staff should still prepare the account list, signer list, year-end statements, and reconciliations.

Include closed accounts that were active during the year. Include new accounts. If the nonprofit has sweep accounts, investment accounts, or restricted accounts, make sure they appear in the cash listing.

The account list should tie to the trial balance and financial statements. If an account is not on the ledger, explain why.

## Show reviewer independence

A strong reconciliation control separates preparation and review. The reviewer should be someone who can question errors, not the same person who prepared the reconciliation.

The review should be visible. A signature, workflow approval, or dated review note can work. The evidence should show what was reviewed and when.

If the nonprofit is small and staffing limits separation, document the compensating review. For example, the executive director or treasurer may review the monthly bank package. Ask your auditor what evidence they expect.

## Keep support for unusual activity

Auditors may ask about large transfers, returned deposits, bank fees, wire payments, or unusual withdrawals. Keep support with the month it happened.

For transfers between accounts, show both sides. For wire payments, include approval and recipient support. For bank errors, include bank correspondence. For fraud or suspected fraud, involve leadership and professional advisors.

## Close the loop after fieldwork

After the audit, review all bank reconciliation follow-up. If old items caused repeated questions, tighten the monthly close checklist. If reviewer signoff was missing, add a required review step. If restricted cash was hard to explain, improve the restricted fund reconciliation.

Bank reconciliation evidence should be simple. It proves the cash balance, shows the timing items, documents review, and gives auditors a clear path from bank statement to ledger.
