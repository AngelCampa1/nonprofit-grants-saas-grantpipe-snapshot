# Foundation fit checklist for nonprofit grants

A foundation grant can look close enough at first glance. The foundation funds your issue area. The award size looks useful. The deadline is still open. That is not enough to start writing.

Foundation fit means the funder, program, budget, timing, and reporting needs all make sense together. A checklist protects staff time. It also helps the executive director see why a grant is worth pursuing or why it should wait.

Use this review before drafting. Keep the notes in your donor CRM or grant record so the next person can see the decision.

## Check mission and program area

Read the foundation's own language first. Do not rely on a one-line database summary. Look for:

- Stated funding areas
- Program exclusions
- Population served
- Age range served
- Geography served
- Type of support
- Recent grant examples

Write down the one program you would propose. If you cannot name it in one sentence, the fit is not ready.

A strong fit sounds like this: "This foundation funds youth mental health programs in our county, and our school counseling program serves that group now."

A weak fit sounds like this: "They fund community work, and our work is in the community." That is too broad.

## Review giving history

Candid's foundation profile resources can help teams understand foundation research. A foundation's website, annual report, and public filings may show past grants. The IRS also provides Form 990 resources. Private foundation returns can help staff see who the foundation has funded and at what size.

Look for:

- Grants to similar nonprofits
- Grants in your city or state
- Average grant size
- Multi-year grants
- First-time grantees
- General operating support
- Program support only

Do not treat one old grant as proof. Giving patterns change. Use the history as evidence, then check the current guidelines.

## Check relationship history

Foundation fit is not only data. It is also relationship context.

Review your donor CRM for:

- Program officer notes
- Board connections
- Past proposals
- Declines and reasons
- Site visits
- Event attendance
- Prior gifts or sponsorships

If there is no history, decide whether an inquiry is allowed. Some foundations welcome a short contact before applying. Others ask applicants not to call. Follow the funder's process.

GrantPipe can help keep foundation contacts, grant records, deadlines, restricted funds, and reporting tasks connected. That helps staff see both the relationship and the award work in one place.

## Test budget fit

Do not wait until the budget attachment is due. Review budget fit during prospect research.

Ask:

- Does the foundation fund the cost type you need?
- Does it allow staff time?
- Does it allow indirect costs?
- Does it fund equipment?
- Does it require a match?
- Does it cap request size?
- Does it require other committed funds?

If the grant would create restricted funds, finance needs to know early. The award may need its own fund, project code, budget line, or report package. A foundation grant can be simple, but it still needs clean records.

## Check deadline and process fit

Foundation processes vary. Some ask for a short letter first. Some use a portal. Some require a board-approved budget, audited financials, Form 990, logic model, or partner letter.

Build a process checklist:

- Inquiry required
- Portal account needed
- Board approval needed
- Budget attachment ready
- Latest audit ready
- Form 990 ready
- Program outcomes ready
- Letters ready
- Executive review date set

If any required item is missing, assign an owner before drafting starts. A deadline problem found after the narrative is written wastes time.

## Review reporting duties

Foundation reporting can be light or detailed. It may include a narrative report, budget update, outcome data, photos, stories, or final spending report.

Before applying, ask:

- What report dates are listed?
- Who owns each report?
- What outcome data is needed?
- What budget data is needed?
- What documents should be saved?
- Are site visits expected?

This is where program and finance staff should speak up. If the team cannot track the promised outcome now, do not promise it without a plan.

## Use a pass, wait, or pursue decision

End the checklist with one of three decisions.

Pursue means the fit is clear, the deadline is workable, and the budget and reporting duties are manageable.

Wait means the funder may fit, but a relationship step, missing document, or unclear program scope should happen first.

Pass means the grant does not fit the nonprofit's work or would create more risk than value.

Record the reason in plain words. The best reason is short enough for a future staff member to understand.

## Keep the checklist current

Review the foundation fit note before each new cycle. Update it when the funder changes guidelines, staff have a new conversation, or the nonprofit's program changes.

Good fit work reduces rushed writing. It also helps the nonprofit avoid restricted grants that do not match real costs, deadlines, or evidence capacity.
