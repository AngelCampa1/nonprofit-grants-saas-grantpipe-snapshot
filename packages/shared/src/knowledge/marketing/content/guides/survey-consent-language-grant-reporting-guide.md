# Survey consent language for grant reporting

Survey data can help a nonprofit explain program results. It can also create risk when people do not know how their answers will be used. Consent language keeps the survey clear before the first answer is given.

This guide is not legal advice. It is a working checklist for program and grants teams. Use it with your award terms, privacy policy, and counsel when needed.

## Start with the report purpose

Write down why the survey exists. Many grant surveys support a report, renewal request, evaluation plan, or board update. Some surveys also help staff improve the program.

The consent text should name the purpose in plain words. A participant should not have to guess whether the survey affects service, payment, eligibility, or case work.

A simple opening can say: "We ask these questions to learn how the program is working and to report summary results to funders."

That line is plain. It does not promise more than the team can prove. It also tells the person that the answers may leave the program team in summary form.

## Say what data may be used

List the type of answers the survey collects. Keep the list short, but be specific enough for trust.

Common survey data includes:

- Attendance feedback
- Satisfaction scores
- Pre and post skill ratings
- Demographic answers
- Service needs
- Open text comments
- Contact information for follow up

If the survey asks sensitive questions, say that clearly. If the survey stores names or case numbers, say that too. A hidden identifier can still make the data identifiable.

For grant reporting, staff often want summary data. Summary data can still carry risk when a group is small. If only three people are in a report group, a quote or rare answer may point back to one person. Review small groups before the report is sent.

## Explain who may see the data

Do not promise that no one will see survey answers unless that is true. Most grant reporting work involves more than one person.

The consent text can say:

"Program staff, grants staff, and approved reviewers may see survey answers. Reports to funders will use summary results when possible."

That is safer than saying "your answers are private" with no limit. Privacy is not the same as secrecy. Staff may need to review answers to run the program, fix data, or support a funder report.

If a funder, evaluator, auditor, or partner may see the data, name the role. You do not need a long list in every case, but the text should not hide known use.

## Make the choice clear

The survey should say whether the person can skip questions or decline the survey. If services do not depend on the survey, say so.

Plain language can say:

"You may skip any question. Your choice will not change the services you can receive."

Only use that line if it is true. Some programs have required intake or eligibility questions. In that case, separate required intake from optional feedback. People should know which questions they must answer and which ones help the report.

## Keep the reading level low

Consent text is not the place for dense policy language. Use short sentences. Use common words. Define hard terms.

Avoid:

- "Data may be disseminated to stakeholders."
- "Participation is voluntary unless otherwise mandated."
- "Information may be used for programmatic evaluation."

Better:

- "We may share summary results with funders."
- "You can skip this survey."
- "We use answers to see what is working."

The goal is respect. People should understand the request before they answer.

## Save the approved version

The consent text is part of the evidence file. Save the exact version used for each survey period. If the text changes, keep the date of the change.

Save:

- Survey form
- Consent text
- Approval note
- Date used
- Program owner
- Data owner
- Report period
- Privacy review notes

This matters when a funder asks how data was collected. It also helps new staff understand why a survey question exists.

## Match the report language to the consent

Do not use survey data in a way the consent text did not describe. If the survey said answers would be used for program improvement, think carefully before using named quotes in a public report.

For grant reports, use summary data when possible. If you include a quote, remove names and details that identify the person unless you have clear permission.

GrantPipe can help teams keep survey files, report tasks, due dates, and evidence notes near the grant record. That makes it easier to check whether the report language matches the source.

## Review before each report

Before the report is sent, ask:

- Did we use the current consent text?
- Did people know the survey purpose?
- Did we remove private details from the report?
- Did we use summary data when possible?
- Did the report match the survey date range?
- Did program staff approve the meaning?
- Did grants staff save the source?

Survey consent does not need to be long. It needs to be honest. Good language tells people what you are asking, why you are asking, how the answers may be used, and what choice they have.
