# Nonprofit program budget template guide

A program budget should show what the work really costs. A grant budget often shows only the part a funder may pay. Nonprofits need both.

Use this guide to build an internal program budget that can support grant proposals, board review, and monthly budget checks.

## Start with the program plan

Do not start with last year's spreadsheet. Start with the work.

Ask program staff:

- What services will be delivered?
- How many people will be served?
- Where will work happen?
- Which staff roles are needed?
- Which partners or vendors are needed?
- What supplies, travel, or space are needed?
- What outcomes must be tracked?

The budget should follow the plan. If the plan changes, the budget should change too.

## Build revenue lines

List each expected revenue source.

Include:

- Government grants
- Foundation grants
- Corporate grants
- Individual donations
- Earned revenue
- Sponsorships
- Released restricted funds
- Unrestricted support

Mark each source as confirmed, pending, or planned. Also mark whether it is restricted. A program can look fully funded while still having a cash or restriction problem.

## Build personnel lines

Personnel is often the largest cost. It should be clear and tied to work.

For each role, list:

- Position title
- Annual salary or hourly rate
- Percent of time
- Months charged
- Salary cost
- Fringe rate
- Fringe cost
- Funding source

Avoid hiding staffing needs in one large line. Grant reviewers and leaders need to see how the program will be staffed.

## Add direct non-personnel costs

Direct costs are costs tied to the program.

Common lines:

- Supplies
- Travel
- Training
- Contract services
- Participant support
- Equipment
- Printing
- Occupancy
- Software
- Evaluation

For each line, add an assumption. An assumption explains how the amount was calculated. For example, 40 training kits at $18 each is clearer than a flat supplies estimate.

## Add shared and indirect costs

Programs use shared support. A budget that omits shared costs can make a program look cheaper than it is.

Shared costs may include:

- Finance staff
- HR staff
- Executive oversight
- Rent
- Insurance
- Technology
- Audit costs
- Data systems

Some costs may be charged as indirect costs. Some may be allocated as direct shared costs. Finance should choose a consistent method that fits policy and funder rules.

For federal awards, allocable costs are addressed in 2 CFR 200.405. Cost rules in 2 CFR 200.403 also matter.

## Track match and unfunded costs

A program budget should show what the grant will not pay.

Add columns for:

- Total cost
- Requested from funder
- Match
- Other grant support
- Unrestricted support
- Unfunded amount

This view helps leaders see whether a grant will cover the work or shift cost to the organization.

## Add budget notes

Budget notes should explain the numbers in plain words.

Good notes answer:

- What is included?
- How was the amount calculated?
- Which source will pay?
- What rule affects the line?
- What assumption could change?

These notes become useful later during budget to actual review. They also help if a staff member leaves.

## Review before using it in a proposal

Before copying numbers into a funder form, review the internal budget.

Ask:

- Does the budget match the work plan?
- Are all costs allowed by the funder?
- Are costs in the right period?
- Is match real and supported?
- Are indirect costs treated correctly?
- Are shared costs allocated fairly?
- Does the program need cash before reimbursement?

GrantPipe can help connect program budgets to grants, restricted funds, documents, and reporting dates. The internal template still needs finance judgment and program review.

## Keep one source of truth

Save the approved internal budget. If a funder asks for a different format, keep that funder version too. Do not let versions drift without notes.

When the grant is funded, compare the approved award budget to the internal program budget. This shows what changed, what is funded, and what the nonprofit still needs to cover.

A good program budget helps the team say yes to the right grants and no to grants that leave too much cost behind.
