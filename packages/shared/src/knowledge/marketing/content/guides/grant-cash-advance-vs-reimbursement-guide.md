# Grant cash advance vs reimbursement guide

Grant payment terms affect cash, records, and risk. A grant that pays in advance can create spending control issues. A grant that pays by reimbursement can create cash flow pressure.

Finance should review payment terms before the award is signed and before costs begin.

## Know the basic difference

A cash advance sends money before all costs are paid. The nonprofit must spend the funds for the approved purpose and keep support.

A reimbursement grant repays costs after the nonprofit records or pays them. The nonprofit submits a request with backup, waits for review, and receives payment later.

Some grants use both methods. For example, a funder may send an initial advance and then switch to reimbursement.

## Review advance rules

Advance funds still need controls. The nonprofit should not treat the cash as unrestricted.

For each advance, record:

- Amount received
- Date received
- Approved purpose
- Spending period
- Bank or fund account
- Budget lines
- Reporting dates
- Interest rule if any
- Unspent balance
- Closeout rule

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.305 includes payment standards. Award terms may explain timing, drawdown, and cash management rules.

## Review reimbursement rules

For reimbursement grants, finance should map the request process before costs start.

Record:

- Cost period
- Request deadline
- Request form
- Required backup
- Proof of payment rule
- Review contact
- Expected payment time
- Minimum or maximum request
- Signature rule

If payment takes 30, 60, or more days, the nonprofit needs to know whether operating cash can carry the cost.

## Compare cash flow risk

Advance risk is usually control risk. The cash is in hand, but the nonprofit must prove it was used correctly.

Reimbursement risk is usually timing risk. The nonprofit must pay or record costs before cash arrives.

Ask:

- How much cash comes in first?
- How much cash goes out first?
- How long until payment?
- Which costs are payroll?
- Which costs are large vendor bills?
- What happens if the funder rejects a cost?

A grant can be fully awarded and still cause a cash squeeze.

## Tie payment terms to the budget

Do not review payment terms apart from the budget.

For each budget line, ask:

- Is the cost allowed?
- When will it be incurred?
- When will it be paid?
- When can it be requested?
- What proof is needed?
- What if the funder pays late?

Payroll is often the most sensitive line because staff must be paid on time even if the funder review is slow.

## Build controls for advances

Advance controls should show that money was used for approved work.

Use:

- Grant code
- Restricted fund tracking
- Budget to actual review
- Approval workflow
- Spending deadline
- Report calendar
- Unspent balance review
- Closeout checklist

If advance funds are pooled with other cash, accounting records still need to show the grant balance and use.

## Build controls for reimbursements

Reimbursement controls should make requests complete and timely.

Use:

- Expense coding review
- Backup checklist
- Payroll support review
- Proof of payment if required
- Request log
- Aging report
- Funder question log
- Payment posting review

GrantPipe can help connect reimbursement requests, cash status, grant budgets, and backup documents so finance can see what is requested, paid, and still open.

## Watch for rejected costs

If a funder rejects a cost, decide how to handle it. The cost may need to move to another fund, be replaced with another allowed cost, or be excluded from the request.

Save the funder response and the correction. Do not resubmit the same cost without fixing the issue.

Also check whether the rejected cost changes the cash forecast. A denied reimbursement may leave unrestricted cash covering work that the budget expected the grant to pay.

## Use payment terms in go no go decisions

Before applying, ask whether the nonprofit can manage the payment model.

A large reimbursement grant may need reserve cash. A large advance may need stronger monthly review. Either way, payment terms should affect the decision to apply.

Learn the payment model before the proposal goes out. If that did not happen, review it during award intake, before spending starts.
