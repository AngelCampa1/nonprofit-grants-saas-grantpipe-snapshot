The board does not need every grant detail. It does need to know when grants could change the organization's choices.

That is the purpose of an executive director grant risk briefing. It gives the board a short, clear view of grant risks that matter to governance: cash, compliance, reporting, delivery, reputation, and future funding.

Use this guide with the [board grant dashboard guide](/resources/guides/nonprofit-board-grant-reporting-dashboard) and the [grant compliance guide](/resources/guides/grant-compliance-101-for-nonprofits).

## Start with the risk, not the activity

Many board updates sound like staff reports. They list applications sent, reports drafted, calls held, and meetings attended.

That activity can matter, but it is not the briefing.

Start with a short risk statement:

"Three active grants need board attention this quarter. One creates cash strain because it pays by reimbursement. One is behind spending plan. One has a report due before the next meeting and depends on partner data."

That opening tells the board where to listen.

## Use five risk buckets

Keep the briefing stable by using the same five buckets each time.

### 1. Cash timing

Explain whether grants pay in advance or by reimbursement. Show receivables that are late. Name any payment tied to a report or invoice.

Cash timing is a board issue when it affects reserves, payroll, vendor timing, or program delivery.

### 2. Restricted use

Explain what funds can and cannot be used for. Do not let a large bank balance hide restricted cash.

If a grant is restricted to one program, the board should not treat that money as general support.

### 3. Spending pace

Show awards that are far ahead or behind plan. Explain why.

Underspending may mean hiring delays, partner delays, or slower service volume. Overspending may mean coding errors or costs that will not be covered.

### 4. Reporting and compliance

Show reports due soon, late items, funder questions, and any audit or monitoring issue.

Federal awards may require internal controls and financial management systems under 2 CFR Part 200. Do not turn the board briefing into a rulebook. Name the duty and the risk.

### 5. Renewal and relationship risk

Tell the board if a grant is ending, at risk, delayed, or tied to a funder concern. This connects finance and fundraising.

A clean report history can support renewal. A missed report or weak closeout can hurt it.

## Name the board action

Every risk does not need a board vote. But the briefing should say what the board is being asked to do.

Use simple labels:

- "For awareness"
- "Finance committee follow-up"
- "Board approval needed"
- "Decision needed next month"

Examples of board actions:

- approve reserve use during a reimbursement gap
- approve a budget change
- approve acceptance of a high-risk award
- approve a contract above policy limits
- approve a corrective action plan

The [board minutes grant approval checklist](/resources/guides/nonprofit-board-minutes-grant-approvals-checklist) can help document formal action.

## Keep the briefing short

A useful briefing fits on one page or five minutes of meeting time. Use backup only when needed.

Suggested format:

1. Top risk sentence.
2. Grant risk table.
3. Cash and receivable note.
4. Restricted fund note.
5. Reports due before next meeting.
6. Board action list.

The grant risk table can include grant name, risk level, issue, owner, next date, and action needed.

## Do not soften real problems

Plain language is better than comfort language. If a report is late, say it is late. If a grant is behind plan, say why and what staff are doing. If staff capacity is the issue, name it.

The board cannot help with a hidden problem.

At the same time, do not make every small issue sound like a crisis. Reserve board attention for issues that affect cash, policy, compliance, delivery, or future funding.

## Bring finance and program together

The executive director should not build the briefing alone. Finance should supply balances, receivables, restricted fund detail, and spending pace. Program leaders should supply delivery status and staffing issues. Development should supply funder context and renewal risk.

The executive director's role is to join those facts into one clear board story.

## Final check

Before the meeting, ask:

- Can the board see the top three risks?
- Does each risk have an owner?
- Does each risk have a next date?
- Is the board action clear?
- Are restricted funds shown plainly?

If yes, the briefing is ready.
