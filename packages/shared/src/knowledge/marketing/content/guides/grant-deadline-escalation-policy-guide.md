A grant deadline escalation policy helps staff act before a missed date becomes a cash, compliance, or funder problem. It tells the team when normal follow-up is not enough and who must be told next.

Most deadline problems start small. A program count is late. A receipt is missing. A budget question is open. A funder portal will not accept a file. A board vote is needed before the next meeting. Without a policy, staff may wait too long or tell the wrong person.

The policy should be short, direct, and easy to use. It should cover proposals, reports, reimbursements, renewals, amendments, board approvals, and closeout dates.

## Define the deadlines covered

Start by naming the deadline types the policy covers.

Include:

- proposal submission dates
- letter of intent dates
- award acceptance dates
- report due dates
- reimbursement request dates
- budget revision dates
- prior approval dates
- match documentation dates
- renewal dates
- closeout dates
- board or committee approval dates

If a date can affect money, compliance, funder trust, or board action, include it.

Do not write a policy only for final due dates. Many grant deadlines have internal due dates. A report due to a funder on Friday may need program data by Monday and finance review by Wednesday.

## Set internal warning points

Use warning points before the final due date. The point is to create time for action.

A simple model:

- 30 days out: owner confirms scope, data, budget, and support needed.
- 14 days out: owner confirms drafts or files are in progress.
- 7 days out: owner flags any missing item.
- 3 days out: manager escalation if any required item is still missing.
- 1 day out: executive escalation if submission is at risk.

Adjust the timing for large grants. A federal report or major proposal may need a 60 or 90 day start.

## Name escalation triggers

The policy should list triggers. A trigger is a reason the issue must move up.

Good triggers include:

- required data is missing at the internal due date
- finance review is blocked
- a report may be late
- reimbursement may be delayed
- a funder portal issue is not resolved
- a budget change needs approval
- match support is incomplete
- staff capacity threatens the deadline
- board approval is needed before submission
- the funder asks a question staff cannot answer

Do not rely on people to guess what counts as serious. Write the triggers down.

## Use clear escalation paths

Not every deadline goes to the executive director. Create paths based on risk.

Program data issues may go first to the program director. Budget or reimbursement issues may go to the finance director. Proposal scope issues may go to the development director. Funder relationship issues may go to the executive director or development lead. Board approval issues may go to the executive director and board chair.

For each path, name the first role, second role, and final role. Keep it simple.

Example: "If required program data is missing seven days before a report due date, the grants manager notifies the program director. If still missing three days before due date, the grants manager notifies the executive director."

## Decide when to contact the funder

The policy should say who can contact the funder about a deadline risk. This avoids mixed messages.

Some funders want early notice if a report may be late. Others have strict portal rules. The award terms matter. This guide is not legal advice. Read the grant agreement and involve the right advisor when needed.

The policy can say that funder deadline extension requests must be approved by the executive director or development director before they are sent.

If the deadline affects reimbursement, finance should be part of the decision. A late report can delay cash.

## Document escalations

Every escalation should be documented. Keep the note short.

Record:

- grant name
- deadline
- issue
- trigger
- person notified
- date notified
- decision
- new owner or next step
- final result

Store the note in the grant record. Do not leave it only in email.

GrantPipe can help by tying deadline tasks, owner changes, notes, documents, and report status to the grant record. That makes it easier to see why a deadline moved and who handled it.

## Protect staff from false emergencies

A good policy does not turn every task into a crisis. It does the opposite. It tells staff which issues are normal and which issues need help.

For example, a draft that is still in review 14 days before a report may be normal. Missing source data three days before the due date is not normal.

Use the policy to reduce drama. People should know what happens next.

## Review after missed or close deadlines

After any missed deadline or near miss, review the policy. Ask:

- Was the trigger early enough?
- Was the owner clear?
- Did the escalation reach the right person?
- Did staff know who could contact the funder?
- Was the final proof filed?

Use the answers to improve the policy. Do not wait for audit prep or a funder complaint.

## Starter policy language

Use plain language:

"Grant deadlines must have an owner, internal due date, and final due date. If a required item is missing seven days before the final due date, the deadline owner must notify the responsible department lead. If the item is still missing three days before the final due date, the owner must notify the executive director. Any request to change a funder due date must be approved before it is sent."

Keep the final policy short enough that staff will use it.
