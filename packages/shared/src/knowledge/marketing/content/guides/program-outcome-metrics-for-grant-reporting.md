# Program outcome metrics for grant reporting

Grant reports get harder when outcome metrics are chosen too late. If the proposal promised a result that staff did not track, the report becomes a scramble. If the metric is vague, the team may write around the gap.

Choose metrics before the grant starts. For active grants, review them before the next report. The best metrics are simple enough to collect and strong enough to answer the funder's question.

## Separate outputs from outcomes

Outputs count activity. Outcomes show change.

Outputs include:

- People served
- Classes held
- Meals delivered
- Coaching sessions completed
- Applications submitted
- Referrals made

Outcomes include:

- Skills improved
- Housing retained
- Employment gained
- Attendance improved
- Compliance issue resolved
- Health measure changed

Both can matter. A funder may ask for both. Problems start when a nonprofit reports an output as if it proves an outcome. Serving 200 people does not prove that 200 people improved. It proves reach.

## Start with the funder requirement

Read the award terms and report form. Grants.gov describes reporting as part of post-award work. For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.301 covers performance measurement, and 2 CFR 200.329 covers performance reporting.

List each required measure:

- Exact wording
- Reporting period
- Source record
- Owner
- Due date
- Review step
- Notes

If a required measure is unclear, ask the funder before the report is due. Do not wait until staff are drafting the narrative.

## Choose metrics staff can support

A metric is only useful if the nonprofit can support it with records.

For each metric, ask:

- What source record proves it?
- Who enters the data?
- When is it entered?
- Who checks it?
- What errors are common?
- What privacy limits apply?
- Can the data be pulled for the grant period?

If the answer is "we can probably estimate it," the metric is not ready for a funder report. Use a safer measure or create a real collection step.

## Define each metric in plain words

Write a metric definition that staff can follow.

Weak definition: "Participants improved."

Better definition: "Participants who completed the post-session survey and answered at least four of five knowledge questions correctly."

Weak definition: "Families stabilized."

Better definition: "Families that remained housed for 90 days after receiving rental support."

Definitions should include the numerator, denominator, time period, source record, and exclusions. This helps staff collect the same thing each month.

## Link metrics to finance and restrictions

Outcome metrics do not live apart from finance. If a grant pays for a specific program, the report should connect activity, outcomes, and spending.

Ask:

- Which restricted fund supports this work?
- Which budget lines pay for the activity?
- Are costs posted to the right grant?
- Are outcomes tied to the funded period?
- Does the report need budget notes?

GrantPipe can help connect program reporting, grant compliance, restricted funds, deadlines, and audit evidence. That connection helps when a funder asks why spending changed or why an outcome shifted.

## Build a monthly data check

Do not wait for the report deadline. Review outcome data each month.

A monthly check should ask:

- Is the source data complete?
- Are dates inside the grant period?
- Are duplicates removed?
- Are staff using the same definition?
- Are privacy rules followed?
- Are low or high results explained?
- Is evidence saved?

The goal is not to punish staff. The goal is to find data problems while they can still be fixed.

## Use careful language in the narrative

The narrative should match the strength of the data.

If the data shows attendance, say attendance. If the data shows survey change, say survey change. If the data does not prove long-term impact, do not claim it does.

Better language:

- "The program served 84 participants during the period."
- "Of the 61 participants who completed both surveys, 48 improved their score."
- "Staff could not collect follow-up data from 12 participants."

This kind of writing may feel less dramatic, but it is easier to support.

## Review metrics before renewal

Before a renewal request, compare promised metrics to actual records.

Ask:

- Which metrics were easy to report?
- Which metrics caused manual cleanup?
- Which metrics did the funder value?
- Which metrics should be changed?
- Which targets were too high or too low?

Do not copy old metrics into a new proposal just because they are familiar. Keep the ones that help explain the work and can be supported.

Good outcome metrics make grant reporting more honest. They help program staff explain real work, finance staff connect costs, and leadership make better renewal choices.
