A restricted gift agreement can help a donor and nonprofit share the same intent. It can also create work the team does not expect. The safest time to find that out is before the agreement is signed.

This checklist gives development, finance, and leadership a shared review path. It is not legal advice. Use counsel for unusual terms, naming rights, endowment language, repayment rights, real estate, large pledges, or anything the team does not understand.

## Confirm the donor and gift facts

Start with the basic record. The agreement should clearly name the donor, organization, gift amount, payment timing, and purpose.

Check:

- Legal donor name
- Nonprofit legal name
- Gift or pledge amount
- Payment schedule
- Contact names
- Date of agreement
- Source of any related emails or pledge forms

If the agreement uses a nickname, campaign name, or old legal name, fix it before signing. Small naming errors can slow deposits, receipts, and audit review.

## Read the restriction out loud

The restriction should be clear enough that finance and program staff can follow it. If people interpret it differently, the wording needs work.

Ask:

- What program, purpose, site, or period is named?
- Can the funds pay staff time?
- Can they pay overhead or shared costs?
- Is there a deadline to spend the funds?
- What happens if the project changes?
- Can unused funds be redirected?

Do not accept vague terms just to close the gift. A phrase like "for community impact" may not tell finance how to track the money.

## Look for conditions

Some agreements include conditions. A condition may require the nonprofit to take a specific action before it is entitled to the funds. FASB ASU 2018-08 is the main accounting source finance may use when reviewing contribution conditions.

Watch for:

- Matching requirements
- Milestones before payment
- Donor right of return
- Barriers that must be met
- Multi-year promises tied to future events

Conditions can affect revenue timing. Finance should review these terms before the gift is recorded.

## Check reporting promises

Many restricted gifts include reports. The agreement should say what the nonprofit must send and when.

Capture:

- Report due dates
- Financial detail needed
- Outcome data needed
- Story or photo rules
- Meeting or site visit promises
- Final report requirements

GrantPipe can help track reporting deadlines, restricted funds, audit evidence, and donor CRM notes together. That matters when a gift is managed by both development and finance.

## Review receipt language

The gift acknowledgment should be factual. IRS written acknowledgment rules may apply. The receipt or thank you note should not add promises that are not in the agreement.

For example, if the agreement restricts the gift to a scholarship fund, the thank you should not promise a specific student outcome unless that promise is approved and supported.

Keep tax advice out of the receipt unless it has been reviewed by the right advisor.

## Plan accounting setup

Before signing, finance should know how the gift will be tracked.

Decide:

- Fund, project, class, or grant code
- Release method
- Budget owner
- Report owner
- Evidence storage location
- Month end review process

If the accounting system cannot track the restriction, do not ignore that. Set up the tracking before the gift is deposited or choose a manual control with a named owner.

## Name approval owners

The agreement should have a review record. At minimum, development confirms donor intent, finance confirms tracking and accounting review, and leadership approves risk.

Large or unusual gifts may also need board review, legal review, or gift acceptance policy review.

Save the approval record with the agreement. It should show who reviewed the final version and when.

## Save the final packet

The final packet should include the signed agreement, source emails, pledge records, finance review, receipt copy, deposit support, and setup notes.

If the restriction is later released, the release entry should point back to this packet. That link helps finance prove why the money was restricted and why it later became available for use.

A restricted gift agreement should make the work clearer, not harder. The review protects the donor, the program, and the finance team before the organization says yes.
