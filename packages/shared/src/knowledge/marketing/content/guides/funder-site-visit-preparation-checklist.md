A funder site visit is part relationship meeting and part evidence review. The funder wants to see the work, meet the people, and understand whether the grant is on track.

Good preparation keeps the visit useful. Staff know who will speak. Finance can answer budget questions. Program staff can explain results. The grants team can show reports and support without scrambling.

Use this checklist for foundation, corporate, state, local, or federal funder visits. Adjust it to the funder's stated purpose.

## Confirm the purpose

Ask why the funder is visiting. The visit may support a new proposal, renewal, monitoring review, board education, or problem solving.

The purpose shapes the agenda. A renewal visit needs progress, outcomes, and future plans. A monitoring visit needs compliance evidence. A first visit needs context and a clear explanation of the program.

Write the purpose at the top of the prep document so everyone works from the same plan.

## Build a short agenda

A useful agenda includes welcome, program overview, service walk-through, staff discussion, finance or reporting review, questions, and next steps.

Keep time for the funder to ask questions. Do not fill every minute with presentations.

Name an owner for each agenda section. The executive director does not need to answer every question. The person closest to the work often gives the clearest answer.

## Prepare the program story

Program staff should explain who the program serves, what happens during service, what changed during the grant period, and what results are measured.

Use plain facts. Avoid broad claims that cannot be tied to records. If the program is early, say what has been completed and what is still in progress.

If client stories are used, check consent and privacy rules first. Do not share names, case notes, health information, immigration details, or other sensitive facts unless the organization has a clear right and reason to share them.

## Prepare the finance story

Finance should review the approved budget, actual spending, remaining balance, match, reimbursement status, and any variance.

The funder may ask why spending is low, why a line item changed, or whether cash timing is a problem. Prepare answers before the visit.

If the grant is federal, remember that financial records and internal controls under 2 CFR 200.302 and 200.303 may be part of monitoring. Even for private funders, the same habits help.

## Gather evidence

Prepare a concise evidence folder. Include the award summary, proposal goals, report history, budget-to-actual, outcome support, key policies, staff roles, and open questions.

Do not overwhelm the funder with every document. Keep deeper support available if they ask.

## Brief staff

Hold a short prep meeting. Review the agenda, funder purpose, likely questions, sensitive topics, and who answers what.

Tell staff it is fine to say, "I need to confirm that and follow up." Guessing during a funder visit creates cleanup work later.

Also review what not to share. Staff should avoid private client details, unsupported promises, and internal disagreements that do not help the funder understand the grant.

## Prepare for hard questions

List the questions the team hopes the funder will not ask. Then prepare honest answers.

Common topics include delayed hiring, lower enrollment, budget underspending, turnover, data quality, late reports, match, or a change in program design.

A calm answer should include what happened, what it affected, what the organization is doing, and when the funder will get an update.

## Plan the walk-through

If the visit includes a tour, check the route. Make sure spaces are safe, accessible, and appropriate for visitors.

Tell staff and participants when visitors will be onsite. Respect program flow. Do not turn service delivery into a performance.

If photos are possible, review consent first.

## Track follow-up

During the visit, assign one person to capture questions, commitments, requested files, and deadlines.

Send a follow-up note within a few business days. Thank the funder, answer open questions, attach promised files, and list next steps.

Save the agenda, evidence folder, notes, and follow-up in the grant file. The site visit then becomes part of the grant record, not a memory held by one staff member.
