An executive director grant pipeline review helps the organization decide which grants deserve staff time and which grants create too much risk. The review is not only a fundraising meeting. It is a leadership check on mission fit, money, staff capacity, compliance, and board choices.

Many nonprofits treat the pipeline as a list of possible revenue. That is too narrow. A grant can look attractive and still create a cash gap, a reporting burden, a weak program fit, or costs the award does not cover.

The executive director does not need to write every proposal or review every receipt. But the executive director should know which grants shape the budget, affect the program plan, or need board attention.

## Review the full pipeline, not just open proposals

Use a pipeline with stages. Good stages include research, qualify, plan, write, submitted, awarded, declined, active, renewal, closeout, and paused.

Each stage answers a different leadership question.

Research asks whether the funder might fit. Qualify asks whether the grant is worth time. Plan asks whether the budget and program design are real. Submitted asks what staff must prepare if the award comes through. Awarded asks whether the terms are acceptable. Active asks whether the work is on track. Renewal asks whether the relationship and results support another ask. Closeout asks whether records are complete.

Do not hide paused or declined grants. They are useful. They show where staff time was saved or where the organization learned a funder was not a good fit.

## Start with mission fit

The first check is simple: does this grant pay for work the organization should do?

Ask:

- Does the grant fit the current strategic plan?
- Does the program already exist, or would this create a new project?
- Would the work continue after the grant ends?
- Who will own the program if funded?
- Does the funder require work that changes the mission?

Money can pull an organization off course. A clear pipeline review helps the team say no before the proposal consumes staff time.

## Test the real cost

The grant budget should show more than direct program costs. It should show staff time, fringe, supplies, travel, contracts, equipment, shared costs, indirect cost treatment, and reporting work.

Ask whether the award pays the full cost. If it does not, name the gap. The gap may be covered by unrestricted donations, another funder, reserves, or board-approved support. It should not be hidden.

Federal awards may bring cost rules under 2 CFR Part 200. Foundation awards may have their own budget limits. This guide is not legal or accounting advice. It is a prompt to read the award terms and involve qualified help when needed.

Use the [grant proposal budget review checklist](/resources/guides/grant-proposal-budget-review-checklist) before staff submit a budget.

## Check cash timing

Some grants pay in advance. Others reimburse after costs are paid. Some pay only after a report is accepted. The executive director should know which grants can affect payroll, vendors, or reserves.

For each large grant, ask:

- When will cash arrive?
- What must be submitted before payment?
- How old are current receivables?
- Could a delay affect operations?
- Does the board need to approve reserve use?

Use the [cash flow forecast guide for reimbursement grants](/resources/guides/cash-flow-forecast-reimbursement-grants) when a grant may strain cash.

## Match staff capacity to grant load

The pipeline should show who will do the work. A grant can fail because the budget is fine but the team is full.

Review proposal writing time, program delivery time, finance review time, reporting time, and document support. Check whether the grant depends on a vacant role, a partner, or a contractor.

If staff are at capacity, decide what moves. Do not let every good idea become an urgent deadline.

## Look at reporting before the award

Reporting is part of the cost. Ask what the funder will require before the proposal is submitted.

Check outputs, outcomes, demographic data, financial reports, receipts, match support, client counts, partner reports, and closeout duties. If the organization does not collect the data now, name the work needed to collect it.

The [grant reporting calendar template](/resources/guides/grant-reporting-calendar-template) can help turn reporting duties into dates and owners.

## Separate board issues from staff issues

The executive director should flag pipeline items that need board or committee action.

Board issues may include accepting a high-risk award, approving a budget change, using reserves, changing a policy, approving a contract above limits, or committing match.

Staff issues may include drafting the proposal, collecting letters, building the budget, or updating the calendar. Keep those in the staff plan.

Use the [nonprofit board grant risk register guide](/resources/guides/nonprofit-board-grant-risk-register-guide) for items that rise to board attention.

## Add renewal and relationship risk

The pipeline should not end at award. Renewal risk matters because many grants become part of the operating plan.

Ask:

- Is the funder happy with reports and contact?
- Are outcomes on track?
- Has spending pace changed?
- Are there late reports or unresolved questions?
- What would happen if the grant does not renew?

GrantPipe can help connect award records, reports, contacts, tasks, and renewal notes. That gives the executive director one place to see the pipeline and the follow-through after award.

## Run the review with a fixed agenda

Use the same agenda each time:

1. New opportunities to qualify.
2. Proposals due in the next 30 days.
3. Awards waiting for acceptance.
4. Active grants with cash or compliance risk.
5. Reports due before the next review.
6. Renewals due in the next 90 days.
7. Items needing board or committee action.
8. Grants to pause, decline, or close.

End with decisions. Each decision should name the grant, owner, next step, and due date. A pipeline review should reduce confusion, not create more meeting notes.
