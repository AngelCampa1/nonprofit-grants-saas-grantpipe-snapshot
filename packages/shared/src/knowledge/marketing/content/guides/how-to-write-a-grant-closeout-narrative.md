steps:
  - title: "Locate the reporting requirements in the grant agreement"
    content: "Before writing anything, confirm exactly what the funder requires for final reporting: a specific form or template, a page limit, required sections (narrative, financial, data tables, attachments), the submission method (portal, email, postal mail), and the deadline. Federal agencies often provide final report templates or SF-PPR (Performance Progress Report) forms. Private foundations may have a template, may accept a free-form narrative, or may specify only broad sections. Do not write a 15-page closeout narrative if the funder expects a two-page summary."
  - title: "Gather the program data before writing"
    content: "The narrative must be built from data, not memory. Pull the program records from the full grant period: participant counts by month, services delivered by activity type, outputs documented (workshops held, materials distributed, referrals made), and any outcome data collected (follow-up surveys, assessments, third-party records). If data collection was ongoing throughout the grant, the closeout narrative is an assembly task, not a reconstruction. If data was not consistently collected, the closeout narrative becomes a painful exercise in reconstruction from incomplete records."
  - title: "Write the activities and outputs section"
    content: "Document what was done: the specific activities carried out, the schedule on which they occurred, the populations served, and the outputs produced. This section should match the work plan from the original grant application. Where activities were modified during the grant period (changed timeline, adjusted target population, added or removed an activity), note what changed and why. Funders expect honest documentation of what was planned versus what was delivered - unexplained gaps between the application and the closeout are more concerning than acknowledged variances with explanation."
  - title: "Write the outcomes section"
    content: "Outcomes are the changes in knowledge, behavior, or condition that resulted from the activities. Outputs are what you produced; outcomes are what changed because of it. Distinguish clearly between them. Use the outcome measures from the original logic model or evaluation plan. Report the data you actually have, not aspirational numbers. If outcome data collection had limitations - low survey response rates, difficulty reaching the target population for follow-up - acknowledge them. A closeout narrative that reports only positive outcomes with no caveats reads as incomplete."
  - title: "Write the financial reconciliation summary"
    content: "The financial section reports budget-vs-actual for the full period of performance: every budget category, the approved amount, the actual expenditure, the variance, and an explanation for any variance above the reporting threshold. The numbers in the narrative financial section must match the final financial report (SF-425 for federal grants, or the funder's required financial form) exactly. Write the financial section from the final reconciled accounting records - not from memory or from an interim budget-vs-actual."
  - title: "Explain unspent funds"
    content: "If any approved budget line has an underspent balance, explain it. Do not leave underspent funds unexplained in a closeout narrative. The explanation should cover: why the funds were not spent, what the organization's intent is for the remaining funds (return to funder per grant agreement, carry forward if permitted, apply to a related allowable cost in the liquidation period). For federal grants, any unspent federal funds must generally be returned to the awarding agency - this is not optional. For private foundation grants, the grant agreement specifies whether unspent funds revert to the funder."
  - title: "Describe lessons learned"
    content: "A well-written lessons-learned section demonstrates organizational learning and enhances the relationship with the funder for future funding. What worked better than expected? What was harder than anticipated? What would the organization do differently if implementing this program again? This section is also useful internally - it becomes the institutional memory that informs the next grant application to this funder or a similar one."
  - title: "Compile supporting documentation"
    content: "Federal funders typically require specific attachments: final financial reports (SF-425), equipment inventory if applicable, and data tables showing performance against targets. Private funders vary widely - some want no attachments, others want program photos, participant sign-in sheets, or copies of materials produced. Confirm what attachments are required in the grant agreement. Do not attach unsolicited materials that inflate the submission without adding required substance."
bluf: "A grant closeout narrative has two core components: a programmatic section documenting what was accomplished (activities, outputs, outcomes) and a financial section reconciling the approved budget against actual expenditures. The narrative must be built from data collected during the grant period - not reconstructed from memory at closeout. Unexplained variances between planned and actual work, or between the budget and actual spending, are the most common weaknesses in closeout narratives."
faqs:
  - q: "What is the difference between outputs and outcomes in a closeout narrative?"
    a: "Outputs are what you produced: 120 workshops held, 1,400 participants served, 80 referrals made, 6 curricula developed. Outcomes are what changed as a result: 78% of participants demonstrated improved financial literacy scores, 40% of referred participants secured employment within 90 days. Both belong in a closeout narrative, but they serve different purposes. Outputs demonstrate that the funded activities were implemented. Outcomes demonstrate that the implementation produced the intended effects."
  - q: "How do federal and private funder closeout narratives differ?"
    a: "Federal funder closeout narratives are more standardized. Most agencies require specific forms (SF-PPR for performance reporting, SF-425 for financial reporting), specific sections, and numeric performance data tied to measures established in the award. The financial section must reconcile to the accounting records with a high degree of precision. Private foundation narratives are more variable - some funders want two pages, some want fifteen, some provide templates and some do not. The financial expectations are typically less granular than federal requirements, though well-structured budget-vs-actual tables are always useful."
  - q: "Can I use data from after the grant period in the closeout narrative?"
    a: "Generally no. The closeout narrative reports on activities and outcomes from within the period of performance. Data collected after the end date, even if it relates to program participants served during the grant, is typically not appropriate for the closeout report (it may be appropriate for a separate follow-up communication to the funder). Exception: some federal awards allow a brief post-closeout period for data collection when outcome measurement inherently requires longer follow-up - check the award terms."
answers:
  - question: "What happens if my outcomes did not meet the targets in the original application?"
    answer: "Report what actually happened, with explanation. Do not adjust the targets retroactively, and do not omit outcome data because it fell short. Funders have seen hundreds of closeout reports - they can recognize a report that smooths over underperformance. An honest assessment of what was achieved, what was not, and why is more credible and more valuable to the funder relationship than a narrative engineered to make the numbers look like success. If external factors (pandemic disruptions, policy changes, unexpected population needs) affected outcomes, document them."
  - question: "How do I structure the financial section if there was a budget modification?"
    answer: "Present the approved budget as amended, not the original approved budget. If you received prior approval to rebudget funds between categories during the grant period, the final closeout narrative financial section should show the amended approved budget alongside actuals. Include a note indicating when the modification was approved and the original and amended amounts for any changed lines. This creates a clear audit trail connecting the final financial picture to the approved framework."

The closeout narrative is the last impression your organization makes on a funder for a specific grant. Programs that were well-implemented and well-documented produce closeout narratives that almost write themselves. Programs that were implemented without systematic data collection produce closeout narratives that require weeks of painful reconstruction - and often still have gaps.

## The Difference Between a Well-Documented and a Reconstructed Closeout

Organizations that maintain consistent program documentation throughout the grant period arrive at closeout with the narrative ingredients already assembled. Participant counts are in the program database. Session notes and attendance records are filed. Outcome survey results are tallied. The financial data reconciles cleanly because budget-vs-actual monitoring was ongoing, not retroactive.

Organizations that did not maintain systematic documentation spend the closeout period trying to reconstruct from memory, email threads, and scattered records. They discover that the follow-up survey was administered to only 40% of participants because nobody was tracking completion. They find that some months have attendance records and some do not. They realize that the financial report will not match the accounting system without a week of reconciliation.

The closeout narrative is a reflection of how the grant was managed throughout the period - not just how it was documented in the final two weeks.

## What Funders Are Actually Reading For

Program officers reading closeout narratives are not reading to verify that activities happened. They are reading to assess whether the organization was honest, whether the program produced the intended effects, and whether it is worth funding again.

**Honest documentation of variances.** A closeout narrative that shows perfect alignment between the application and the final report is almost never credible. Grant programs encounter delays, unexpected challenges, and changes in the population being served. Funders know this. A narrative that acknowledges what changed and explains why is more credible than one that presents only success without acknowledging difficulty.

**Evidence of actual impact.** The outcomes section is where the narrative either earns its credibility or loses it. Outputs (workshops held, people served) demonstrate activity. Outcomes (knowledge changes, behavior changes, conditions improved) demonstrate impact. Program officers who fund multiple organizations on the same issue area compare outcome numbers across closeout reports. Vague outcome claims - "participants were empowered" or "the community benefited significantly" - do not meet the standard.

**A clear financial picture.** The financial section should leave the program officer with no questions about how the money was spent. Budget-vs-actual by line item, with brief explanations for material variances, is the standard. Do not bury financial information in narrative paragraphs - use a table.

## Federal vs. Private Funder Expectations

**Federal funder requirements** are defined and mandatory. The Notice of Award specifies the final report form, the required data elements, the submission portal, and the deadline. Deviating from the required format - submitting a narrative when a specific form was required, omitting required performance measures - creates a compliance finding. For federal reports, use the prescribed format and fill in every required field.

**Private foundation requirements** vary. Some funders provide detailed templates with specific sections and word counts. Some provide only a brief description of what they want and leave the format to the grantee. Some have moved to online submission portals with required fields; others still accept a Word document sent by email. The safest approach: use the funder's template if one exists, and if it does not, structure the narrative with clear sections (program activities and outputs, outcomes, financial summary, lessons learned) rather than submitting a long undifferentiated document.

A small number of private funders ask only for a brief letter summarizing what was accomplished and what, if anything, is outstanding. Even for these, include a one-page financial summary showing budget-vs-actual - it signals financial accountability without requiring the funder to ask for it.
