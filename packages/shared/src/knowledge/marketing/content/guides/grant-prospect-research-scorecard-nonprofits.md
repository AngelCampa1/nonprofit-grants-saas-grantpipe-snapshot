# Grant prospect research scorecard for nonprofits

Grant prospect research should stop weak opportunities early. A funder may look good because the award amount is large. That does not mean the grant fits your work, your budget, or your staff capacity.

A scorecard gives the team one place to compare options. It helps keep the conversation out of inbox threads and old spreadsheets. It also creates a record of why you chose to apply, wait, or pass.

Use the scorecard before staff start a full proposal. The goal is not to make the choice automatic. The goal is to make the tradeoffs visible.

## Score mission and program fit first

Start with the funder's stated purpose. For federal grants, read the opportunity notice and eligibility rules. For foundation grants, review the foundation profile, recent grants, program areas, geography, and any application notes.

Score fit from 1 to 5:

- 5 means the grant matches your current program.
- 3 means the grant may fit with a clear scope.
- 1 means the grant would pull work away from your plan.

Do not raise the score because the funder is well known. Do not raise it because the board likes the name. Fit should come from the program, the people served, the place served, and the allowed work.

Write one plain sentence that explains the score. Example: "The funder supports after-school youth work in our county, and this program already runs there."

## Check eligibility before excitement builds

Eligibility is a hard gate. Grants.gov explains that grant eligibility depends on the applicant type and the funding opportunity. Some grants are only for states, schools, tribes, local governments, or specific nonprofit types.

Review:

- Eligible applicant type
- Required registrations
- Geographic limits
- Required partners
- Minimum or maximum budget size
- Match or cost share rules
- Prior award limits

If the grant requires a match, ask finance how the match would be tracked. A matching requirement can turn a simple proposal into restricted funds work. The match may need source records, valuation support, payroll detail, or board approval.

Mark any uncertain rule as a question. Assign one owner to confirm it before proposal work starts.

## Rate budget fit

The budget should fit the work you already plan to do. A grant that pays for a narrow part of a program can still be useful. A grant that forces the team to invent a new budget under deadline is risky.

Score budget fit by asking:

- Does the grant pay for the real cost of the work?
- Are indirect costs allowed?
- Are payroll costs allowed?
- Are equipment or supplies allowed?
- Are there unallowable costs that matter?
- Would the grant create cash pressure before reimbursement?
- Would the award need a new restricted fund?

Federal awards require financial records that identify the source and use of funds. Even for private grants, finance needs a clean way to track restricted funds. If the budget needs manual workarounds, lower the score or name the control needed.

## Score deadline risk

A short deadline is not always a reason to pass. It is a reason to be honest.

Review:

- Due date and time zone
- Required attachments
- Board approvals
- Partner letters
- Budget review
- Portal setup
- Fiscal sponsorship needs
- Executive review

Use a simple risk rating:

- Low risk: all source material exists.
- Medium risk: one or two items need owner follow-up.
- High risk: core facts, budget, or approvals are missing.

For high risk grants, require the executive director to approve the choice before staff spend nights or weekends on the proposal. Urgency can hide poor fit.

## Include reporting and evidence work

Prospect research should include the work after the award. Grants.gov notes that reporting can include financial and performance reports. A foundation grant may also require outcome updates, narrative reports, site visits, or budget notes.

Score the reporting load:

- 5 means the team already tracks the needed data.
- 3 means the team can track it with one clear change.
- 1 means the team would need a new process.

Ask program staff what outcomes can be supported with current records. Ask finance what expenses can be tied to the grant. Ask operations where audit evidence would live.

GrantPipe can help teams connect donor CRM records, grant deadlines, restricted funds, documents, and reporting tasks. The scorecard still matters because staff must decide which opportunities deserve that setup work.

## Use a weighted score

Not every factor should count the same. A simple nonprofit scorecard can use these weights:

- Mission fit: 30 percent
- Eligibility: pass or fail
- Budget fit: 20 percent
- Deadline risk: 15 percent
- Reporting burden: 15 percent
- Relationship fit: 10 percent
- Evidence readiness: 10 percent

Keep the math simple. A scorecard that takes more time than a staff meeting will not get used.

## Set review rules

Decide what each score means before the team reviews prospects.

Example rules:

- 80 to 100: pursue if staff capacity exists.
- 60 to 79: discuss with finance and program first.
- Under 60: pass unless leadership documents a reason.
- Any eligibility fail: do not apply.
- Any high deadline risk: get executive approval.

Save the scorecard in the grant record. If the nonprofit applies and wins, the notes help set up the award. If the nonprofit passes, the record stops the same prospect from being reopened every month.

## Review the score after the outcome

After each decision, compare the score to what happened. If you applied, was the proposal painful? If you won, did the reporting work match the score? If you passed, did a better grant appear?

The best scorecard gets better with use. It should help your team protect time, choose stronger grants, and avoid awards that create more compliance work than value.
