Boards often see one large cash number and one large net asset number. That can cause bad decisions. A nonprofit may have $800,000 in the bank and still have only $90,000 available for payroll, rent, and urgent needs. The rest may be tied to grant periods, donor purposes, or capital work.

A good restricted net assets board report fixes that problem. It answers one plain question: what money can we use, and what money must stay tied to a promise?

## Start with a one-page summary

The first page should be short enough to read before the meeting. Use the same structure every time:

- Beginning net assets with donor restrictions
- New restricted grants and gifts
- Releases from restriction
- Ending net assets with donor restrictions
- Cash available for general use
- Restricted funds that need action

This format follows the way ASC 958 presents net assets with and without donor restrictions. It also connects to the board's duty to oversee resources. The board does not need to post journal entries, but it does need to know when restricted balances are growing, when grant delivery is behind, and when unrestricted liquidity is tight.

For more background, link the report to your internal guide on [restricted fund accounting basics](/resources/guides/restricted-fund-accounting-basics). If board members need the mechanics, point them to [net asset release workflow](/resources/guides/net-asset-release-workflow).

## Separate cash from availability

Cash is not the same as available cash. This is the most useful board lesson in restricted fund reporting.

Show a simple bridge:

Total cash and investments

Less cash restricted by donors or grantors

Less cash held for future grant periods

Less board designations, if your board treats them as unavailable

Equals cash available for general use

This bridge helps the treasurer explain why a strong bank balance may not mean strong liquidity. ASC 958 requires nonprofits to disclose liquidity and availability in audited financial statements. A board report should use the same logic in simpler form.

## Show releases as program progress

A release from restriction is not just an accounting entry. It is proof that restricted work moved forward. If a $200,000 grant had $60,000 released this quarter, the board should see what drove that release.

Use a table with these columns:

- Fund or grant name
- Opening restricted balance
- New restricted revenue
- Released this period
- Ending restricted balance
- Percent of grant period elapsed
- Percent of budget spent
- Notes

The last two percent columns matter. A grant that is 80% through its grant period but only 35% spent needs attention. A grant that is 40% through the period but 90% spent may need a budget check or a no-cost extension plan.

## Use plain risk flags

Do not make the board hunt through numbers for risk. Add a plain status for each material restricted fund:

On track means spending and timing look reasonable.

Watch means the fund needs staff review before the next meeting.

Action needed means the board, finance committee, or executive director must decide something.

Examples of action items include a budget revision, a cash bridge, a grant extension request, or a policy decision about temporarily using unrestricted cash before reimbursement arrives.

Keep the language factual. Do not write that a grant is "safe" unless the data supports it. Write what is known: invoices are pending, payroll coding is incomplete, a report is due, or a funder approval is needed.

## Tie the report to Form 990 and audit needs

The board does not need an audit binder every month. Still, the report should line up with future audit and Form 990 work.

Use the same net asset categories that appear in the financial statements. Use grant names that match award letters. Keep support for each release. That support may include invoices, payroll records, time records, funder emails, and journal entries.

IRS Form 990 asks for financial statement information and board review practices. Auditors often ask how the board monitors restricted funds. A steady board report gives a clean answer: the board sees restricted balances, releases, liquidity, and risks on a regular cadence.

## Add a short narrative

Numbers need a few words. Add a short finance note after the table. Keep it direct:

"Restricted net assets increased by $140,000 because two new foundation grants were awarded in June. Releases were lower than planned because the youth program hiring date moved to July. General cash available for use is $112,000 after removing donor-restricted cash."

That kind of note gives the board the story without hiding the details.
