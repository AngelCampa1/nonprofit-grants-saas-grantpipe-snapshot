A nonprofit cost allocation policy tells staff how to charge costs when more than one grant, fund, program, or department benefits. Without a written policy, the team may split costs by habit, budget room, or the preference of whoever prepares the entry.

That is risky. Reviewers need to see why a cost was charged to a grant. Program staff need a fair method. Finance needs a method that can be repeated. Leaders need to know whether each program is carrying its share of cost.

This sample outline is not legal or accounting advice. It gives nonprofit teams a structure to adapt with their CPA, auditor, grant terms, and board policy.

## 1. Purpose

Start with a plain purpose statement.

The policy should say that the organization allocates shared costs based on benefit, uses consistent methods, keeps support for each allocation, and reviews methods on a set schedule.

Avoid a purpose statement that only says the policy exists for compliance. The better reason is operational control. A clear policy helps staff post costs correctly, prepare reports faster, and answer funder questions with evidence.

GrantPipe can help keep grant budgets, fund codes, approvals, and allocation support connected. The policy still needs to define the rules people follow.

## 2. Scope

Name what the policy covers. Include grants, restricted funds, unrestricted programs, administrative departments, fundraising, and shared services if those areas use the same accounting records.

The scope should also say which costs are outside this policy. For example, payroll may have a separate time certification policy. Capital purchases may have separate approval rules. Fundraising event costs may follow another procedure.

Clear scope prevents staff from using the wrong method just because the policy is nearby.

## 3. Definitions

Define the terms staff will use. Keep the definitions short.

Useful terms include direct cost, shared cost, indirect cost, cost pool, allocation basis, allocation rate, benefiting activity, period of performance, restricted fund, grant code, fund code, and functional expense category.

Define the terms in the same way your accounting system and chart of accounts use them. If a word means one thing in the policy and another in the ledger, staff will make mistakes.

## 4. General principles

List the principles that guide every allocation.

A good policy usually says costs must be necessary, reasonable, allowable, allocable, consistently treated, and documented. For federal grants, these ideas appear in 2 CFR 200.403 and 2 CFR 200.405. Private grants may use different words, but the review logic is similar.

The policy should also say that a grant cannot be charged only because it has budget left. Budget room is not proof of benefit.

## 5. Direct charging rules

Explain when a cost should be charged directly.

Direct charging fits when the cost benefits one grant, fund, program, or activity and the source record supports that link. Examples include supplies bought only for one grant, a consultant hired for one award, travel for one funded event, or a staff position that works only on one grant and has support for that work.

The policy should say what support is required. That may include an invoice, purchase approval, timesheet, contract, agenda, participant list, or program manager confirmation.

## 6. Shared cost rules

Explain when costs must be allocated.

Shared costs may include rent, utilities, software, insurance, shared staff, training, supplies, equipment use, data systems, and professional services. The policy should tell staff not to force these costs into one grant when more than one activity benefits.

For each common cost type, name the allowed allocation basis. Staff time may use certified hours. Rent may use square footage. Software may use licensed users. Supplies may use units, participant counts, or program usage. A shared service contract may use service tickets, cases, or agreed program usage.

## 7. Indirect cost treatment

The policy should explain how indirect costs are treated. Some nonprofits use a negotiated indirect cost rate. Some use the de minimis rate when allowed. Some charge administrative costs through approved direct allocation methods.

Do not mix these methods without review. A cost should not be charged once as a direct cost and again through an indirect pool. The policy should state how the organization prevents duplicate recovery.

If federal grants are involved, review 2 CFR 200.414 and award terms before setting this section.

## 8. Documentation

Every allocation needs a support file. The policy should require the source cost, allocation basis, calculation, grant or fund codes, reviewer approval, and posting reference.

For recurring allocations, keep the approved method and the current period support. A method from last year may be stale if staff, space, programs, or grant mix changed.

Documentation should let a reviewer trace the cost from invoice or payroll report to ledger entry.

## 9. Approval roles

Name who prepares, reviews, and approves allocations.

Program staff should confirm benefit when finance cannot see it from the invoice. Finance should prepare or review the calculation. Grants staff should check award terms. A supervisor or controller should approve the final method.

For high risk or high dollar allocations, require a second review. The threshold should be written in the policy.

## 10. Review cadence

Set a review schedule. Monthly review works for recurring allocations. Annual policy review works for the full method.

Also require review when something changes. New grants, ended grants, staff turnover, new office space, new software, new programs, or changed funder rules can all make an old basis wrong.

## 11. Exceptions

No policy covers every case. Create an exception process.

The exception record should explain the cost, why the normal method did not fit, which method was used, who approved it, and whether it is one time or recurring.

Do not let exceptions become the normal method without updating the policy.

## 12. Examples

End the policy with examples. Examples help staff apply the rule.

Include a payroll example, rent example, shared software example, supplies example, consultant example, and cost that should not be allocated to a grant. Show the basis and support for each.

Examples are especially useful for new grants managers and program directors. They turn the policy from a control document into a working tool.
