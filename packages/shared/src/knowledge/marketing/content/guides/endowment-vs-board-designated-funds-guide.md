Nonprofits often use the words endowment, reserve, restricted fund, and board designated fund in the same meeting. They are not the same thing. The difference matters because it affects who can use the money, who can change the limit, and how finance reports the balance.

This guide gives a plain way to separate donor-restricted endowment funds from board designated funds. It is not legal or accounting advice. Endowments can involve state law, donor agreements, investment policy, and accounting rules. Use qualified advisors for specific decisions.

## Start with who created the limit

The first question is simple: who created the limit on use?

If the donor created the limit, the funds may be donor restricted. If the board created the limit using unrestricted money, the funds are board designated.

That source matters. A board can often change its own designation through a new board action. A donor restriction usually cannot be changed by staff or the board alone unless the donor terms, law, or a legal process allow it.

## Understand donor-restricted endowments

An endowment is meant to support the organization over time. Some endowments are donor restricted. A donor may say the original gift must be invested and only a spending amount can be used. The donor may also restrict the use to scholarships, a program, a building, or general support.

Finance should save:

- Gift agreement
- Donor letters
- Board acceptance
- Investment policy
- Spending policy
- Restrictions on use
- Release or appropriation records

FASB ASU 2016-14 addresses nonprofit financial statement presentation, including net assets with and without donor restrictions. Endowments may also be affected by state law such as UPMIFA, which many states have adopted in some form.

## Understand board designated funds

A board designated fund starts with funds the organization could otherwise use. The board sets them aside for a purpose. Common examples include operating reserves, building reserves, strategic projects, or future equipment.

The board should document:

- Amount designated
- Purpose
- Date of vote
- Source of funds
- Use rules
- Review date
- How designation can change

Because the limit comes from the board, it is usually reported as net assets without donor restrictions. The board designation should still be visible in internal reports. It helps leaders avoid spending money they have set aside for a reason.

## Do not mix donor limits and board choices

A fund can become confusing when donor restrictions and board decisions sit in the same account. For example, a donor-restricted building gift should not be treated like a board building reserve just because both relate to the same project.

Use separate tracking for donor restrictions and board designations. If the board adds extra limits to donor-restricted money, keep both records. The donor terms still matter.

## Compare release rules

Restrictions and designations end in different ways.

A donor restriction is released when the donor's purpose or time limit is met, based on accounting review. A board designation changes when the board votes or follows its own policy.

Keep evidence for both. For donor restrictions, save expense support, dates, release entries, and donor terms. For board designations, save board minutes, policy references, and votes.

## Show both in board reports

Board reports should avoid one large cash number. Cash can include restricted funds, board designated funds, operating reserves, and money needed for payroll.

A useful report shows:

- Cash total
- Donor-restricted balances
- Board designated balances
- Available operating cash
- Major upcoming uses
- Open reporting or release issues

This helps board members understand why cash in the bank is not the same as cash available to spend.

## Review terms each year

Review endowment and board designated funds at least annually. Confirm the balances, restrictions, policies, spending draws, and board actions. Update owners and save the review notes.

For endowments, check donor agreements, investment reports, spending policy, and any legal or accounting updates. For board designated funds, check whether the purpose still fits and whether the board wants to keep, change, or release the designation.

## Keep the language clean

Use the right name in reports. Do not call a board reserve "restricted" unless donor terms restrict it. Do not call a donor-restricted endowment "board designated" because the board likes the purpose.

Clear language protects decision making. It also helps finance answer audit questions without rebuilding old board and donor records.

The short rule is this: donor restrictions come from donors, board designations come from the board. Treat them with different evidence and different approval paths.
