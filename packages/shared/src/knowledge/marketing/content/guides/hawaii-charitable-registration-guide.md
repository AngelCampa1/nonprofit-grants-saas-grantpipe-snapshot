## BLUF

Nonprofits soliciting contributions in Hawaii or holding Hawaii charitable assets must file Charity Registration with the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division. The current filing fee is no initial fee; renewal fees scale with revenue, and the renewal cycle runs before soliciting; renewed annually through the AG's online portal. Most rejections come from missing IRS Form 990 attachments, fee miscalculation, or filings submitted past the due date without an extension.

## TL;DR

- Authority: Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467B.

## What Charity Registration is

Charity Registration is the filing the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division uses to oversee charitable activity in Hawaii. The form registers a charitable organization soliciting contributions in Hawaii. It exists under Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467B. For most operating nonprofits, this is the recurring state-level filing that sits next to the federal IRS Form 990 in the annual compliance cycle.

Hawaii uses an online-only system. The state accepts the IRS Form 990 in lieu of a separate annual financial report. Audit requirements scale with gross revenue.

## Who must file

The registration obligation is broad. If your organization solicits contributions from Hawaii residents, holds charitable assets in Hawaii, or conducts charitable programs in the state, you almost certainly fall within scope. The trigger is solicitation activity, not physical presence. An organization headquartered in another state that runs a donation page reachable by Hawaii residents has typically triggered registration.

Common categories that must file:

- 501(c)(3) public charities incorporated in Hawaii
- Out-of-state charities that solicit Hawaii donors by any channel (direct mail, phone, email, online, events)
- Organizations applying for grants from Hawaii-based funders or government agencies
- Charitable trusts holding assets in Hawaii

Common exemptions (verify each, since exemption is fact-specific):

- Religious organizations operating exclusively for religious purposes
- Accredited educational institutions soliciting from their own communities
- Hospitals and certain healthcare entities under defined statutory exemptions
- Small organizations under defined gross revenue thresholds

Even when an exemption applies, many states require an exemption claim to be filed. Confirm with the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division rather than assuming. ## Filing deadline and fee

The deadline is before soliciting; renewed annually through the AG's online portal. Missing the deadline triggers late fees and deficiency notices. Repeat lapses can result in administrative revocation of the right to solicit in Hawaii, which has downstream effects on grant eligibility and donor confidence.

The current filing fee is no initial fee; renewal fees scale with revenue. Always check the agency's current fee schedule before paying; fee tables change and out-of-date checks are a common rejection reason. Plan for ancillary costs: CPA review or audit fees if your gross revenue meets the financial-statement threshold, paid preparer fees if you outsource the filing, and late penalties if a previous year was missed.

## Required attachments and documentation

The Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division typically requires the following alongside Charity Registration:

- A complete copy of the most recent IRS Form 990, 990-EZ, or 990-PF (organizations filing only the 990-N postcard generally still need to provide state-level financial detail)
- The IRS determination letter establishing tax-exempt status
- Articles of incorporation and any amendments
- Current bylaws
- A list of officers and directors with addresses and dates of appointment
- Audited or reviewed financial statements when gross revenue meets the Hawaii threshold
- A list of states where the organization is registered, when applicable

## How to file

The five-step sequence below covers the core mechanics. Each step maps to one of the structured filing steps in the frontmatter, so the content compiles into both narrative prose and HowTo schema.

**1. Confirm registration trigger.** Determine whether Hawaii law requires your organization to register. Review Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467B for the activities that trigger registration: direct mail, online giving forms accessible to Hawaii residents, paid solicitors, grant solicitations, and asset holdings. If any apply, you must file.

**2. Gather supporting documents.** Pull the most recent IRS Form 990 (or 990-EZ/990-PF), IRS determination letter, articles of incorporation, bylaws, list of current officers and directors, and audited or reviewed financials if your gross revenue triggers the Hawaii financial-statement requirement.

**3. Complete the Charity Registration.** Complete every line. Charity Registration registers a charitable organization soliciting contributions in Hawaii. Use figures from your most recently filed Form 990 to keep state and federal data consistent. Inconsistencies between state and federal filings are a frequent deficiency notice trigger.

**4. Calculate and submit the fee.** The current filing fee is no initial fee; renewal fees scale with revenue. Verify the fee against the agency's current schedule before submitting; fee schedules change. Pay through the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division online registration system or by check made payable to the entity specified on the current form instructions.

**5. File and retain confirmation.** Submit through the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division online registration system. Save the confirmation number, payment receipt, and a complete copy of the filed form with attachments. Store the package alongside your federal Form 990 in your compliance file.

## Common mistakes

After reviewing the published deficiency reasons across state charity bureaus, the same handful of errors come up year after year:

1. **Missing Form 990 attachment.** The state expects the complete federal return, not a summary. Partial uploads are rejected.
2. **Inconsistent revenue figures between state and federal filings.** Use the same fiscal-year data in both places. If the federal return is amended, amend the state filing too.
3. **Wrong fee.** Fee tables change. Verify the current schedule on the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division website before submitting payment.
4. **Missing signatures.** Most state forms require two officer signatures, often the chief executive and the chief financial officer. One signature is a common reject.
5. **Late filing without an extension.** A federal Form 990 extension does not always extend the Hawaii filing automatically. Confirm before relying on it.
6. **No audit when one is required.** State audit thresholds are independent of the federal $1,000,000 single audit threshold under 2 CFR 200.501. An organization can need a state audit but no federal one, or vice versa.
7. **Stale officer and director information.** Directors who rolled off the board still appearing on the filing is a frequent point of state follow-up.

## What happens if you miss the Charity Registration deadline

The first consequence is mechanical: the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division issues a deficiency notice and assesses late fees. The second consequence is operational: until the registration is current, the organization is not legally permitted to solicit contributions in Hawaii. That status flows through to grant eligibility (state and many private funders verify charitable registration before disbursing), donor receipts (deduction language tied to Hawaii-registered status becomes problematic), and online giving platforms (some platforms verify registration before processing).

The third consequence is reputational. Lapses appear in public registries. Donors who research the organization on the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division website see the lapse. Board members who run their own organizations notice. For an organization that depends on institutional grants, a lapse can disqualify a proposal in the eligibility-screening stage before the substance ever gets reviewed. The math on prevention is simple: the cost of a calendar reminder and a monitored deadline is far less than the cost of a missed grant cycle.

## FAQ

**Who must file the Charity Registration in Hawaii?** Any organization soliciting charitable contributions from Hawaii residents, holding charitable assets in Hawaii, or otherwise meeting the registration trigger under Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467B must file. Religious organizations, accredited educational institutions, and certain small organizations may qualify for an exemption, but exemption is not automatic; confirm status with the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division before skipping the filing.

**When is the Charity Registration due?** The Charity Registration is due before soliciting; renewed annually through the AG's online portal. Missing the deadline typically triggers late fees, deficiency notices, and (in repeat cases) administrative dissolution or revocation of the right to solicit in Hawaii.

**What does it cost to file the Charity Registration?** The current filing cost is no initial fee; renewal fees scale with revenue. Additional costs can include CPA fees for required reviews or audits, registered agent fees, and late penalties if the organization missed a prior renewal.

**Can I file the Charity Registration online?** Yes. Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division accepts filings through the Hawaii Attorney General Tax & Charities Division online registration system. Online filing reduces the most common rejection reasons (missing signatures, math errors on fee tables, attachments not received) and produces a date-stamped confirmation.

**What happens if my Hawaii registration lapses?** A lapsed registration means the organization is not legally permitted to solicit contributions in Hawaii until the registration is reinstated. The state may impose late fees, require back filings for missed years, and pursue civil penalties under Hawaii Revised Statutes Chapter 467B. Donors who give during a lapse may not be able to claim deductions tied to the Hawaii-registered status.
