Payroll is often the largest grant cost. That makes staff time certification one of the most important grant controls.

The goal is simple: payroll charged to a grant should match work performed for that grant. The file should show who worked, when they worked, what grant benefited, how the amount was calculated, who reviewed it, and how corrections were handled.

This guide is for nonprofit finance, grants, and program teams. It focuses on practical records, not legal advice. Use award terms, labor rules, and your accounting policy for final decisions.

## Start with the grant role

Before certifying time, know what the staff role is supposed to do under the grant. Review the approved budget, job description, award terms, workplan, and any staffing changes.

If the grant pays for a program coordinator, the certification should support program coordinator work. If the staff member moved to another program halfway through the period, the allocation should change.

GrantPipe can help connect staff assignments, budgets, tasks, files, and grant periods. The time record still needs to reflect the actual work performed.

## Do not rely on the budget alone

A budget is a plan. Time certification supports actual payroll cost.

If a grant budget says an employee is 50 percent funded, that does not prove the employee worked 50 percent on the grant every pay period. The team needs a record that supports the charge. That may be a timesheet, activity report, personnel activity record, certified allocation, or other system record allowed by policy and award terms.

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.430 addresses standards for compensation and records. The core idea is that payroll charges need support from the organization's records and internal controls.

## Choose the right time record

Different roles need different records.

Hourly staff usually need timesheets that show hours worked. Salaried staff may need activity records or certifications if they work across grants or cost objectives. Staff working on one grant may still need a periodic certification, depending on policy and funder rules.

The record should be clear enough for a reviewer to understand the work. "Admin" may be too vague. "Client intake for Grant A program" is stronger.

## Match time to the period

Payroll records, timesheets, grant periods, and accounting periods should line up. A staff member may work in one month and be paid in another. The certification should make the relationship clear.

Check that the work happened inside the grant period. If the payroll period crosses the grant start or end date, split the time correctly.

Closeout periods need care. Work done after the grant ends may be allowed only for closeout activity if the award terms allow it.

## Confirm grant benefit

The reviewer should confirm that the work benefited the grant. This is easy when the employee works only on one funded program. It is harder when staff serve multiple programs, grants, or unrestricted work.

Use calendars, case notes, attendance records, work orders, program logs, deliverable trackers, or supervisor review to support the benefit when needed.

If the work benefits more than one grant, use a supported allocation basis. Do not split payroll by remaining budget.

## Review coding and allocation

Payroll charges should use the right employee, pay period, grant code, fund code, program, department, natural account, and restriction code.

For split funded staff, the allocation worksheet should show hours or percentages, total pay, fringe treatment, grants charged, and reviewer approval. If fringe follows salary, say so in the policy. If fringe uses another method, document it.

Compare the current split to prior periods. A large change may be correct, but it should have a reason tied to staffing or work.

## Certify with the right reviewer

The reviewer should know enough to confirm the work. That may be the employee, supervisor, program director, grant owner, or other responsible official named in policy.

Finance should also review payroll coding and math. Grants staff may need to review award terms, budget fit, and reporting categories.

The certification should be dated. It should show the period, employee, grant, amount or hours, reviewer, and approval decision.

## Handle corrections clearly

Errors happen. Staff may code time to the wrong grant, miss a timesheet, change roles, or report late.

A correction should have support. Save the reason, corrected amount, original entry, new entry, approval, and date. Avoid vague notes like "cleanup." A better note says "moved 12 hours from Grant A to Grant B based on approved timesheet correction dated July 8."

Late corrections near closeout need extra review because they can affect reports and reimbursement.

## Keep the audit file

The payroll support file should include the payroll register, timesheet or activity record, allocation worksheet, certification, approval, grant budget check, and correction records.

Keep records according to award terms and document retention rules. For federal awards, retention rules can continue after closeout, and some circumstances extend the period.

The file should let a reviewer trace payroll from employee work to grant charge to report or reimbursement request.

## Monthly certification checklist

Each month or pay period, confirm:

- staff assignment matches the grant budget or approved change
- time records cover the right period
- work descriptions support grant benefit
- allocation basis is current
- payroll and fringe math are correct
- grant, fund, and account codes are correct
- supervisor or responsible official approval is saved
- corrections are documented
- reports and reimbursement requests match payroll support

This process does not need to be heavy. It does need to be consistent. A short, repeated review is easier than rebuilding payroll evidence at audit time.
