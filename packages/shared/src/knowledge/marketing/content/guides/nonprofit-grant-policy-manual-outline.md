A nonprofit grant policy manual gives staff one place to find the rules for grant work. It explains who can pursue grants, who reviews budgets, who signs awards, how records are kept, and how reports and closeouts are handled.

The manual does not need to be long. It does need to be clear. A small team can start with a simple outline and add detail over time.

This guide is not legal or accounting advice. Federal grants, state grants, city contracts, and foundation awards can all have different terms. Use the manual to define your internal process, then check each award for specific rules.

## Section 1: purpose and scope

Start with the purpose. Explain why the manual exists.

Example: "This manual sets the organization's process for pursuing, accepting, managing, reporting, and closing grants."

Then define scope. Say which grants are covered. Include government grants, foundation grants, corporate grants, restricted gifts that function like grants, and pass-through awards if they apply.

Also say what is not covered. For example, the manual may not cover small unrestricted donations or event sponsorships unless they include formal grant terms.

## Section 2: roles and authority

List the roles involved in grant work. Use roles before names.

Include executive director, development, grants, finance, program, data, compliance, board treasurer, finance committee, and full board when those roles apply.

Define who can approve:

- grant pursuit
- proposal submission
- budget submission
- award acceptance
- funder agreement signature
- budget amendments
- reserve use
- match commitments
- closeout certification

Use the [grant team roles and responsibilities matrix](/resources/guides/grant-team-roles-and-responsibilities-matrix) to make this section easier to maintain.

## Section 3: opportunity review

Set the process for deciding whether to pursue a grant.

The policy should require review of mission fit, program fit, staff capacity, true cost, cash timing, reporting load, restrictions, match, and risk.

Define who can say yes, who can pause the opportunity, and who can decline it.

This section protects the organization from chasing grants that look good but do not fit.

## Section 4: proposal budget review

The manual should require finance review before a budget is submitted.

List budget items finance should review: salaries, fringe, consultants, supplies, travel, equipment, indirect costs, shared costs, match, and reimbursement timing.

Also define how the organization handles budgets that do not cover full cost. A grant that leaves a gap may still be worth pursuing, but leadership should see the gap before submission.

Use the [grant proposal budget review checklist](/resources/guides/grant-proposal-budget-review-checklist) as a procedure under this policy.

## Section 5: award acceptance

An award notice should not move straight into revenue without review.

The manual should require review of award amount, period, restrictions, payment method, reporting dates, match, budget changes, signature authority, insurance, procurement, and closeout terms.

Define when board or committee approval is needed. Examples include high-risk terms, large match commitments, reserve use, contracts above limits, and major new program obligations.

Use the [grant award acceptance checklist](/resources/guides/grant-award-acceptance-checklist) as the operating tool.

## Section 6: financial management and controls

This section should connect grant work to finance controls.

Cover grant codes, restricted funds, budget to actual review, reimbursement requests, receivables, cost allocation, payroll allocation, procurement, approvals, and documentation.

Federal awards may require financial management and internal control standards under 2 CFR Part 200. The manual should not paste long rules. It should say how the organization meets its duties and where staff find the details.

## Section 7: reporting and deadlines

Define how report dates are captured, reviewed, drafted, approved, and submitted.

Each report should have an owner, internal due date, final due date, data source, finance support, reviewer, and proof of submission.

The manual should also define escalation rules when a report may be late. Link to the [grant deadline escalation policy guide](/resources/guides/grant-deadline-escalation-policy-guide).

## Section 8: records and document retention

The manual should state where grant files live and what belongs in them.

Include proposal, award, amendments, approved budget, reports, receipts, reimbursement support, match support, approvals, correspondence, monitoring notes, closeout proof, and retention notes.

For federal awards, record retention requirements may apply. Use the award terms and applicable rules. The [grant compliance file index template guide](/resources/guides/grant-compliance-file-index-template-guide) can help structure files.

GrantPipe can support this section by keeping grant records, documents, reports, notes, and dates together instead of spreading them across inboxes and folders.

## Section 9: monitoring, findings, and corrective action

Define how the organization responds to funder monitoring, audit requests, management letters, and findings.

Name who receives requests, who gathers files, who reviews responses, who approves corrective action, and who tracks closure.

Do not let findings disappear after one meeting. Track them until proof of closure exists.

## Section 10: closeout and policy review

Closeout should have a required checklist. Include final reports, final reimbursement, receivable review, restricted balance review, inventory, match, file completeness, and funder confirmation.

Review the manual at least once a year. Also review it after staff changes, new grant types, audit findings, or funder rule changes.

A manual only works if people use it. Keep it plain, keep it current, and connect it to the tools staff use every week.
