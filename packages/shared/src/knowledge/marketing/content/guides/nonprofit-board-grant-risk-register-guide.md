A nonprofit board grant risk register helps leaders see grant problems before they become meeting surprises. It is not a staff task list. It is not a full grant tracker. It is a short board view of risks that could affect cash, compliance, delivery, reputation, audit findings, or future funding.

The board has an oversight role. Staff manage the daily grant work. The register should respect that line. It should give the board enough detail to ask good questions and make good choices without pulling trustees into day-to-day work.

Use this guide with the [executive director grant risk briefing guide](/resources/guides/executive-director-grant-risk-briefing-guide) and the [finance committee grant oversight checklist](/resources/guides/finance-committee-grant-oversight-checklist). Those pages help turn the register into a meeting update.

## Use a small set of risk types

Do not build a register with twenty labels. Staff will stop using it, and the board will stop reading it. Use a short set that maps to real governance concerns.

Good risk types include cash timing, restricted use, spending pace, reporting, match, staffing, document support, audit or monitoring, funder relationship, renewal, and closeout.

Cash timing covers reimbursement delays, late receivables, advances that may need to be returned, and reports that hold payment. Restricted use covers money that can only be used for a purpose, period, or program. Spending pace covers grants that are far ahead or behind budget. Reporting covers late reports, weak source data, or funder questions. Match covers required cost share and in-kind support. Staffing covers vacancies that affect delivery or reporting.

The point is simple: the board should see the type of risk and why it matters.

## Decide what belongs on the register

Not every issue belongs. A typo in a file name does not need board attention. A missing report that delays payment does.

Put an item on the register when it meets one of these tests:

- It could affect cash within the next quarter.
- It could require board or committee approval.
- It could create a funder concern.
- It could lead to an audit or monitoring finding.
- It could affect service delivery.
- It could affect a renewal or future award.
- It crosses finance, program, and development teams.

This keeps the register focused. If staff add every small task, the board loses the signal.

## Give each risk a plain owner

Every risk needs one owner. That does not mean one person does all the work. It means one person answers for the next step.

For example, finance may own a reimbursement delay. Program may own late service data. Development may own a funder call. The executive director may own a board decision about reserve use.

Avoid vague owners like "team" or "staff." Use a role. Good examples are finance director, grants manager, program director, development director, executive director, and board treasurer.

## Use simple status levels

Use three or four status levels. More levels invite debate instead of action.

A plain model works well:

- Watch means the risk is real, but no action is late.
- Action needed means staff must act before the next meeting.
- Escalated means a committee or board decision is needed.
- Closed means the issue has proof of resolution.

Do not use color alone. Add words. A red dot without a sentence does not help the board understand the issue.

## Add dates that matter

Each register row should have at least one date. Use the date that drives risk: report due date, award end date, cash need date, board decision date, funder meeting date, closeout date, or expected payment date.

Dates help the board see urgency. They also stop the same risk from staying open for months without movement.

If a risk has no next date, ask whether it belongs on the board register. Most real grant risks have a clock.

## Write the risk as a sentence

Short risk titles are useful, but the board needs a sentence too.

Weak title: "Late report."

Better sentence: "The Q2 report for the youth services grant is due July 15, and program attendance data is still missing for two sites."

Weak title: "Reimbursement issue."

Better sentence: "The city reimbursement request is 45 days old, and the delay could require a reserve draw if payment does not arrive before payroll week."

Clear sentences make the register useful for trustees who do not live in the grant files.

## Connect the register to board action

The register should say what the board needs to do. Use labels such as awareness, finance committee follow-up, board vote, policy review, or executive session.

Many risks only need awareness. Some need committee work. A few need formal action.

Examples of board action include approving reserve use, approving a budget amendment, accepting a high-risk award, approving a corrective action plan, or changing a policy.

Use the [nonprofit board minutes grant approvals checklist](/resources/guides/nonprofit-board-minutes-grant-approvals-checklist) when a vote is needed. The register should point to the action, not replace the minutes.

## Keep support outside the register

The register is a summary. Store backup in the grant file. That backup may include award terms, reports, invoices, emails, meeting notes, budget schedules, and closeout proof.

The board does not need every document in the packet. It does need to know the support exists and who owns it.

GrantPipe can help by keeping award terms, report dates, notes, tasks, documents, and restricted fund context in one place. That makes it easier to update the board register from current records instead of chasing facts across folders.

## Review and close risks with proof

A risk should not close just because someone says it is handled. Close it when the proof is in the file.

Examples of closure proof include a paid reimbursement, submitted report receipt, approved budget amendment, updated policy, accepted corrective action plan, or funder email confirming the issue is resolved.

At each review, ask four questions:

- Is the risk still real?
- Has the level changed?
- Is the owner still right?
- What proof will close it?

This habit keeps the register alive. It also helps the board see whether grant risk is improving or getting worse.

## A simple register layout

Use these columns:

- grant or funder
- risk type
- plain risk sentence
- status
- owner
- next date
- board action
- closure proof

Add notes only when needed. If the register needs long paragraphs, move the details to backup. The board version should fit on one or two pages.

Start with the top five risks. Add more only when the board can use them.
