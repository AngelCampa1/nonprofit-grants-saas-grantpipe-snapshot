# Donor CRM vs grant management system

Nonprofits often ask one tool to do two different jobs. A donor CRM helps staff manage people and gifts. A grant management system helps staff manage awards and obligations. The difference matters because a missed donor note and a missed federal report create different risks.

For a wider buying frame, read the [unified donor and grant management guide](/resources/guides/unified-donor-grant-management-guide). If you are still defining the terms, start with [what is grants management](/resources/guides/what-is-grants-management).

## What a donor CRM is for

A donor CRM is the relationship hub. It should show who gave, who asked, what was said, what was promised, and what should happen next.

Common CRM work includes:

- donor records
- gift history
- pledge tracking
- appeal lists
- thank-you notes
- moves management
- event participation
- segmentation

This work is important for foundation relationships too. A program officer may be a funder contact, an event guest, and a board connection. That history belongs in a CRM.

## What a grant system is for

A grant management system is the award hub. It should show what was awarded, what limits apply, what has been spent, what reports are due, and what evidence supports the file.

Common grant system work includes:

- award setup
- approved budgets
- restriction terms
- report calendars
- budget changes
- document files
- closeout tasks
- record retention

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.302 requires financial records that identify the award and source of funds. 2 CFR 200.329 covers performance reporting. 2 CFR 200.334 covers record retention. These rules make grant tracking more than a contact management task.

## Where the tools overlap

The overlap is the funder. A foundation may be a donor, a grantmaker, and a relationship your executive director manages. The CRM view should help staff understand the relationship. The grant view should help staff manage the award.

Problems start when teams confuse the two. A gift note does not prove a restricted cost was allowed. A grant budget does not tell development staff when the funder last spoke with the executive director.

The best setup lets each workflow keep its job while sharing the same funder context.

## Where donor CRMs usually fall short

Many CRMs can store a grant record. That does not mean they manage grants well.

Watch for these gaps:

- no approved budget categories
- no restricted balance view
- no report packet support
- no audit evidence folder
- no closeout checklist
- no connection to finance data

These gaps may be fine for one or two simple foundation grants. They become costly when the team manages several restricted awards.

## Where grant tools usually fall short

Some grant tools are strong after award but weak for donor work. They may not support gift acknowledgments, donor lists, pledge schedules, event history, or appeal results.

That matters because foundations are relationships, not just files. If the grant system hides relationship history, staff may miss context before a renewal ask.

## Use a real workflow test

Pick one funder that has both relationship history and an active award. Then ask each system to show:

- the main contact
- past gifts or awards
- open tasks
- award restrictions
- current spend status
- next report date
- documents needed for review

If the CRM can show relationships but not award control, it is not enough by itself. If the grant system can show reports but not relationship history, it may still need a CRM beside it.

## Choosing a stack

Small nonprofits may start with a CRM and a careful grant spreadsheet. That can work when grants are simple and staff review the spreadsheet each month.

Mid-sized nonprofits with several active restricted grants need stronger controls. They can use separate tools with a clear handoff, or they can choose a platform that connects donor and grant records.

Large nonprofits may need separate systems because the workflows are deep and teams are specialized. In that case, integration and data ownership matter more than buying one tool.

## Watch the reporting burden

The deciding factor is often reporting. If reports are simple thank-you updates, a CRM task and document folder may be enough. If reports require budget-to-actual numbers, restriction releases, program outcomes, attachments, and approval steps, a grant system becomes more important.

Ask staff how long it took to prepare the last three reports. Also ask where the numbers came from. If the answer involves copying from accounting, checking a spreadsheet, and searching shared drives, the current stack is hiding work.

That time should be part of the buying decision. Software does not need to remove every manual step, but it should reduce repeat work and make review easier.
