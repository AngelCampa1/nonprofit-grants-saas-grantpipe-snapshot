## Starting a Nonprofit in Nashville: The Full Process

Nashville is in the middle of a nonprofit boom. The city's healthcare industry - anchored by HCA Healthcare and dozens of health services companies - generates corporate philanthropy and health-focused nonprofit creation at a rate that outpaces most cities of similar size. The music and entertainment industry creates its own charitable ecosystem, from artist-led foundations to music education organizations. And Nashville's rapid population growth creates more demand for social services, education, and community development organizations.

This growth means more people are starting nonprofits in Nashville. It also means more people are making avoidable mistakes in the process. The steps below walk through the full sequence - from Tennessee incorporation through federal tax exemption to local tax exemptions - with Nashville-specific context.

## Step 1: Plan Before You File

Before filing any paperwork, answer three questions:

**Is a new nonprofit the right structure?** Nashville already has thousands of nonprofit organizations. If an existing organization serves your mission, partnering with or joining that organization may be more effective than creating a new entity. The [Community Foundation of Middle Tennessee](https://www.cfmt.org) maintains a directory of Nashville-area nonprofits that can help you assess whether your mission is already being served.

**Can you sustain the organization financially?** A nonprofit corporation has ongoing legal obligations - annual reports, financial filings, tax returns - regardless of revenue. Starting an organization with no realistic funding plan creates a legal entity with obligations but no resources to meet them.

**Do you have a qualified board?** Tennessee requires at least one director for a nonprofit corporation, but the IRS expects a functioning board of at least three unrelated individuals. Recruit board members before filing, not after.

## Step 2: Incorporate in Tennessee

File articles of incorporation with the Tennessee Secretary of State. The filing fee is $100, and online filing is available through the Secretary of State's business filing portal.

Your articles of incorporation must include:

- The corporation's name (must include "Corporation," "Incorporated," or an abbreviation, and must be distinguishable from other registered Tennessee entities)
- A statement of nonprofit purpose
- The registered agent's name and Tennessee street address
- The incorporator's name and address
- Language required by the IRS for 501(c)(3) eligibility: a purpose clause limiting activities to those described in Section 501(c)(3), and a dissolution clause directing assets to another 501(c)(3) organization upon dissolution

Get the IRS language right in the articles of incorporation. Fixing it after filing requires an amendment, which costs additional time and money.

## Step 3: Obtain an EIN

Apply for an Employer Identification Number (EIN) from the IRS immediately after incorporation. The application is free and can be completed online through the IRS website. You'll receive the EIN immediately upon completion. You need the EIN before you can open a bank account, apply for 501(c)(3) status, or file for state registrations.

## Step 4: File for 501(c)(3) Status

File [Form 1023](/resources/guides/501c3-application-step-by-step-guide) (or Form 1023-EZ for smaller organizations) with the IRS. The filing fee is $600 for Form 1023 and $275 for Form 1023-EZ.

**Which form should Nashville organizations use?** Form 1023-EZ is available to organizations expecting less than $50,000 in annual gross receipts and less than $250,000 in total assets. It's faster - typically one to three months versus three to six months for the full Form 1023. But the full application forces you to articulate your organizational structure, programs, finances, and governance in detail. For Nashville nonprofits planning to apply for foundation grants from the Community Foundation of Middle Tennessee or other institutional funders, the organizational clarity that comes from completing the full Form 1023 is worth the additional time.

The IRS determination letter - the document confirming your 501(c)(3) status - is required for virtually every subsequent step: state tax exemptions, charitable solicitation registration, and grant applications.

## Step 5: Register for Tennessee Charitable Solicitation

Tennessee requires organizations that solicit or receive more than $30,000 per year in contributions to register with the Secretary of State's Division of Charitable Solicitations. Organizations below the $30,000 threshold must file an exemption form.

This is the step Nashville founders most often miss. The $30,000 threshold sounds high for a new organization, but a successful first-year fundraising campaign, a single foundation grant, or an effective online giving campaign can push you over that line quickly. Register proactively rather than discovering the requirement after you've exceeded the threshold.

Registration requires:

- The completed registration form
- A copy of the IRS determination letter
- Financial statements (audited financial statements are required for organizations receiving more than $500,000 in contributions)
- A filing fee (varies based on contribution levels)

Registration is annual. Set a calendar reminder - failing to renew creates legal exposure for continued solicitation.

## Step 6: File the Tennessee Annual Report

Tennessee requires nonprofit corporations to file an annual report with the Secretary of State. The report confirms your registered agent, principal address, and officers/directors. The filing fee is $20.

This is a simple filing, but it matters. Failure to file results in administrative dissolution - the state involuntarily dissolves your corporation. Reinstatement is possible but requires additional fees and paperwork. Set a calendar reminder for your annual report due date.

## Step 7: Apply for Davidson County Property Tax Exemption

If your organization owns property in Davidson County, apply for property tax exemption through the Tennessee State Board of Equalization. The property must be:

- Owned by the nonprofit organization (not leased)
- Used exclusively for the organization's exempt purpose
- Not used for commercial activity unrelated to the exempt purpose

Nashville's consolidated city-county government means there is no separate city and county property tax - Davidson County property tax is the only applicable levy. Apply as soon as you acquire property; the exemption is not retroactive.

## Step 8: Apply for Metro Nashville Business Tax Exemption

Nashville Metro government requires businesses operating within Davidson County to have a business tax license. Nonprofits with 501(c)(3) status are exempt from the business tax but must apply for the exemption with the Metropolitan Clerk's office. Bring your IRS determination letter and your Tennessee articles of incorporation.

## Nashville-Specific Context: Healthcare and Music

Nashville's nonprofit sector operates within an economy dominated by two industries that shape the philanthropic environment.

**Healthcare.** Nashville is the healthcare capital of the United States by corporate concentration. HCA Healthcare, Community Health Systems, Envision Healthcare, and dozens of health services companies are headquartered in the metro area. This generates health-focused philanthropy, corporate giving programs, and a steady stream of healthcare professionals who serve on nonprofit boards. If your nonprofit works in health equity, mental health, substance abuse, or community health, Nashville's corporate healthcare infrastructure is both a funder and a partner.

**Music and entertainment.** Nashville's music industry generates philanthropy through artist-led foundations, record label giving programs, and benefit concerts. Organizations working in music education, arts access, and creative workforce development have access to a funding ecosystem that doesn't exist in other cities. The CMA Foundation, Nashville Songwriters Foundation, and individual artist foundations fund music-related programs at scale.

These industry concentrations also create competition. Nashville has a high density of health-focused and arts-focused nonprofits relative to its population. A new nonprofit in either space needs a clear differentiator - a population not currently served, a geographic gap, or a programmatic approach that existing organizations don't offer.

## Common Mistakes in Nashville

**Skipping the full 1023.** Form 1023-EZ is tempting because it's faster and cheaper. But Nashville's institutional funders - the Community Foundation of Middle Tennessee, the HCA Healthcare Foundation, and others - ask questions during due diligence that the full Form 1023 forces you to answer during the application process. Doing the work upfront saves time when your first grant application asks for the same information.

**Ignoring charitable solicitation registration.** A new Nashville nonprofit that raises $35,000 in its first year through a combination of individual gifts and a small foundation grant has exceeded the $30,000 threshold. Soliciting without registration is a legal violation. Register early.

**Letting the annual report lapse.** The $20 annual report fee and simple filing seem trivial. But missing the deadline triggers administrative dissolution, which means your organization legally ceases to exist until you apply for reinstatement. This creates problems with bank accounts, grant agreements, and [donor management](/resources/best/best-nonprofit-crm-nashville) that are disproportionate to the cost of a simple filing.

**Not planning for Nashville's competitive landscape.** Nashville's rapid growth means more nonprofits are starting each year. Funders - particularly community foundations and corporate funders - evaluate new organizations partly based on whether they fill a genuine gap or duplicate existing services. Before filing your articles of incorporation, understand what already exists and how your organization adds value that isn't currently available.
