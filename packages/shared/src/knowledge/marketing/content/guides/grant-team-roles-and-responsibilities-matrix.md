Grant work crosses teams. Development may find and write the grant. Program may design the work. Finance may build the budget and track costs. The executive director may approve the opportunity. The board may approve risk, match, or policy exceptions. Someone still has to submit reports, file documents, and close the award.

A grant team roles and responsibilities matrix makes that work visible. It shows who owns each step, who reviews it, who approves it, who supports it, and who needs to be informed.

The matrix is useful even when the team is small. In a small nonprofit, one person may hold several roles. That makes clarity more important, not less important.

## Use roles before names

Start with roles, not names. Names change. Roles explain the job.

Common roles include executive director, development director, grants manager, finance director, program director, data lead, compliance lead, board treasurer, finance committee, and full board.

If one person holds two roles, list both roles and then add the current name in a note. For example, "grants manager role is currently held by the development director."

This helps during staff transition. It also helps the board and leadership see where too much work sits with one person.

## Map the grant lifecycle

Use the grant lifecycle as the left side of the matrix. This keeps the table practical.

Good lifecycle rows include:

- prospect research
- go or no-go decision
- proposal calendar
- program design
- proposal budget
- narrative review
- submission approval
- award notice intake
- award acceptance
- accounting setup
- restricted fund setup
- report calendar
- reimbursement request
- budget revision
- funder communication
- document filing
- monitoring response
- renewal review
- closeout

You do not need every row on day one. Start with the points where work often gets missed.

## Use simple responsibility labels

Avoid a complex code system if the team will not use it. Plain labels work.

Use owner, reviewer, approver, support, and informed.

Owner means the role makes sure the work gets done. Reviewer checks quality or policy fit. Approver has authority to say yes. Support provides facts or files. Informed receives the update but does not block the step.

Each row should have one owner. It may have several support roles. If a row has no owner, it is a risk.

## Define proposal roles

Proposal work usually needs several roles.

Development may own funder fit, deadlines, and narrative drafting. Program may own the service model, outcomes, staffing plan, and delivery facts. Finance may own the budget, cost allocation, indirect cost treatment, and cash timing. The executive director may approve the request amount and risk level.

For larger grants, the board or finance committee may need to approve match, reserve use, a contract, or a policy exception before submission.

Use the [grant proposal budget review checklist](/resources/guides/grant-proposal-budget-review-checklist) to define the finance review row.

## Define award setup roles

Award setup is where many handoffs break. A grant is not ready just because the notice arrived.

The matrix should assign ownership for award notice review, term review, signature authority, budget setup, grant code setup, report calendar setup, restricted fund review, document filing, and kickoff meeting.

Finance should not discover payment terms after the award is booked. Program should not discover deliverables after the work starts. Development should not own funder promises alone after award.

Use the [grant award acceptance checklist](/resources/guides/grant-award-acceptance-checklist) before the award moves into active management.

## Define reporting roles

Reports often need program, finance, data, and development. The matrix should show who owns each piece.

Program may supply service counts and narrative updates. Finance may supply expenses, budget to actual reports, match support, and reimbursement backup. Data staff may supply outcome files. Development or grants staff may submit the report and manage funder communication.

The owner should confirm that the whole report is complete. Do not assume each team knows what the other team sent.

Use the [grant reporting calendar template](/resources/guides/grant-reporting-calendar-template) to connect roles to dates.

## Define closeout roles

Closeout can expose weak roles from the whole award period. The matrix should assign final financial review, final narrative review, inventory check, receivable check, restriction release review, document retention, and funder confirmation.

The closeout owner should verify that support files are complete before the award is archived. Use the [grant closeout checklist](/resources/guides/grant-closeout-checklist) for the closeout row.

## Include board roles carefully

The board should not be listed as owner for staff tasks. Use board roles for governance duties.

Board or committee roles may include approving high-risk awards, approving reserve use, approving major budget changes, reviewing audit findings, approving policies, and receiving grant risk updates.

The executive director should decide what rises to the board, based on policy and risk.

## Keep the matrix usable

The matrix should fit on a few pages. If it becomes too large, split it into phases: pre-award, active award, reporting, and closeout.

Review the matrix during onboarding, after staff changes, and after any missed deadline or finding. A missed handoff is a signal that the matrix needs an update.

GrantPipe can support the matrix by giving each grant a clear owner, tasks, due dates, documents, notes, and report status. The matrix says who should do the work. The system helps make the work visible.

## Starter matrix fields

Use these columns:

- lifecycle step
- owner
- reviewer
- approver
- support roles
- informed roles
- deadline trigger
- file location
- notes

Keep notes short. If a row needs a long explanation, link to the policy or procedure. The matrix should point people to the right action, not become the whole manual.
