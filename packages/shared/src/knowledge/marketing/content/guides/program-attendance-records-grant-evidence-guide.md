# Program attendance records as grant evidence

Attendance records are one of the first files a grant reviewer may ask for. They show whether a program reached people during the report period. They also help explain spending, staffing, and service volume.

Attendance data can be useful, but it is easy to misuse. A sign in sheet may count visits. A grant report may ask for unique people. A class roster may include no shows. A case system may include people outside the grant period.

This guide helps nonprofits use attendance records as evidence without making the data say more than it can support.

## Know what the funder asks for

Start with the report form, award terms, or proposal. Write down the exact attendance measure.

Common measures include:

- Total visits
- Unique people served
- Sessions held
- People completing a class
- Households served
- Youth attending at least one session
- Participants attending a set number of sessions

These are not the same. A person who attends five times may count as five visits or one unique participant. The right answer depends on the measure.

If the funder does not define the measure, choose a rule and write it down before counting. The rule should stay the same across the report.

## Use the right date range

Attendance evidence must match the report period. If the report covers April 1 through June 30, the source record should use that period.

Check for common date errors:

- The export uses the calendar year instead of the quarter.
- The roster includes future sessions.
- The record uses registration date instead of attendance date.
- The time zone shifts late night records into the wrong day.
- The report includes makeup sessions outside the period.

Save the date range with the evidence. A screenshot or export note can help if the system does not show filters later.

## Define who counts

Attendance records often include people who should not be counted for a grant report. The report rule should say who is included.

Decide how to handle:

- Staff
- Volunteers
- Caregivers
- Guests
- No shows
- Duplicate registrations
- People served by another grant
- People missing required intake data

Do not delete source records just to make the report cleaner. Keep the source, then document the count rule and exclusions.

## Remove duplicates with care

Duplicate checks depend on the report question. For total visits, repeat rows may be correct. For unique people, repeats must be removed.

Use stable identifiers when possible. Names alone can cause mistakes. One person may use two names. Two people may share one name. A case ID, participant ID, or household ID is usually safer.

If you must use names, document the method. Keep a review note for merges or exclusions. Do not store private details in the grant file unless your policy allows it.

## Separate attendance from outcomes

Attendance proves reach. It does not prove change.

A report can say, "Seventy two participants attended at least one workshop." It should not say, "Seventy two participants improved job skills" unless there is separate outcome evidence.

This distinction protects the nonprofit. It also makes the report more useful. The funder can see what happened and what is still being measured.

## Save the support file

The evidence packet should include more than a final number.

Save:

- Source export or roster
- System name
- Export date
- Report period
- Count definition
- Duplicate check
- Exclusion notes
- Program owner approval
- Final report number
- Submission proof

For federal awards, performance reporting and record retention rules may apply. Private funders may have their own file rules. Follow the award terms and your retention policy.

## Protect participant privacy

Attendance records can include names, dates of birth, addresses, case notes, or service needs. Do not copy raw records into a broad grant folder if fewer details would support the report.

Use summary files when possible. Limit access to people who need the record. Remove columns that do not support the count. Store raw files in the approved system.

## Review before submission

Before the report goes out, ask:

- Does the date range match the report?
- Does the count rule match the funder request?
- Were duplicates handled correctly?
- Are no shows excluded when needed?
- Is the source saved?
- Did program staff approve the number?
- Does the narrative avoid overstating outcomes?

Attendance evidence is strongest when it is boring. The source is clear. The rule is clear. The report says what the record proves and nothing more.
