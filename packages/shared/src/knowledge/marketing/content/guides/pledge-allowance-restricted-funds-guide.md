A restricted pledge can make the balance sheet look stronger than the cash account feels. The donor has promised support, but the money may arrive over months or years. Some pledges will be paid late. Some may need an allowance. Some may carry a donor restriction that limits use even after cash arrives.

This guide is not legal or accounting advice. It is a work plan for finance review. Use your auditor or accounting adviser for final treatment when a pledge is large, unusual, conditional, or disputed.

The practical goal is simple. Finance should be able to answer four questions: what was promised, what is likely to be collected, what restriction applies, and what support proves any release.

## Start with the written promise

The pledge file should include the signed pledge card, gift agreement, email, board record, or other written support. The file should show the donor, amount, payment schedule, purpose, restriction, and any conditions.

Do not treat every promise the same. A promise that depends on a future event may need different review than a firm pledge. FASB ASU 2018-08 is a useful source when the team must tell a donor restriction from a condition. If the donor must take back the promise when a barrier is not met, slow down and get accounting review.

GrantPipe can keep donor CRM notes, restricted funds, deadlines, and audit evidence connected to the same record. That helps the finance team avoid a common problem: the pledge sits in one file, the restriction sits in another, and the board report shows only a total.

## Build a pledge aging view

List each open pledge by donor, agreement date, due date, original amount, cash received, open balance, restriction, and fund. Then add aging buckets. Use buckets that match your reporting rhythm, such as current, 1 to 30 days late, 31 to 90 days late, and over 90 days late.

Aging alone does not prove collectability, but it gives the reviewer a starting point. A pledge that is 120 days late needs a note. The note can be simple: staff contacted the donor on June 12, the donor expects to pay in July, or the donor has asked to change the schedule.

Avoid vague notes such as "follow up." Write what happened and who owns the next action.

## Review collectability

The allowance review should use more than one signal. Consider payment history, donor communication, age of the receivable, known changes, and whether the pledge was made by an individual, foundation, company, or donor advised fund sponsor.

Do not write down a pledge just because it is restricted. The restriction explains how the pledge can be used. It does not answer whether the money will be collected.

Do not ignore risk just because the donor is known to the organization. A long donor relationship can support collection, but the review still needs evidence. A board member should not have to guess why the allowance is low or high.

## Separate cash, receivable, and restriction

Restricted pledges create timing differences. The pledge receivable may be recorded before cash arrives. Revenue may be recorded before the nonprofit can spend the cash. A release from restriction may depend on time, purpose, or both.

Your reconciliation should show these pieces in separate columns:

- Gross pledge balance
- Allowance
- Net pledge receivable
- Cash received
- Restricted balance
- Release from restriction
- Remaining restricted amount

This keeps the restricted fund report from turning into a cash report. Cash matters, but it is not the whole balance.

## Decide what goes to the board

The board usually does not need every pledge line. It does need clear risk. Show material pledges, aging, allowance changes, major restrictions, cash expected soon, and balances with open questions.

Use plain labels. "Pledge receivable with donor restriction" is clearer than a ledger code. "Allowance increased because two payments are more than 90 days late" is clearer than "management estimate changed."

If the pledge supports a grant match, capital project, or restricted program, say so. A late pledge can affect deadlines, reporting, and cash timing. It can also affect whether a program has enough unrestricted cash to keep moving while it waits.

## Save the audit evidence

Auditors may ask for the pledge agreement, payment history, allowance method, management review, subsequent cash receipts, and release support. Save those records while the facts are fresh.

The best file tells the story without a meeting. A reviewer can open the file and see the promise, the restriction, the balance, the allowance, the cash status, and the release basis.

## Watch for common errors

Common errors include booking a verbal pledge with weak support, treating a conditional promise as a normal receivable, forgetting an allowance review, releasing restriction without evidence, and reporting restricted pledges as available cash.

Another common error is losing donor CRM context. If the donor has changed a payment date or narrowed a restriction, finance needs that note. GrantPipe is useful when the donor CRM record, restricted fund record, and reporting view point to the same facts.

A pledge allowance review is not meant to be dramatic. It is a monthly discipline. Keep the schedule current, document judgment, and make the board report clear enough that leaders know which dollars are promised, which dollars are collected, and which dollars are still restricted.
