A nonprofit chart of accounts should make reporting easier, not harder. For grant-funded organizations, the chart often breaks because each new award creates new accounts. Staff add Salary Grant A, Salary Grant B, Salary Grant C, and the account list grows until no one can report across grants.

The better design separates the type of cost from the source or purpose of funding.

## Keep natural accounts stable

Natural accounts answer one question: what is this transaction?

Examples include:

- Cash
- Accounts receivable
- Grants receivable
- Contributions with donor restrictions
- Salaries
- Payroll taxes
- Rent
- Supplies
- Travel
- Professional services

These accounts should not change every time a new grant starts. A salary cost is still salary whether it belongs to a federal grant, a foundation grant, or unrestricted operations.

For a deeper restricted fund version, link to [nonprofit chart of accounts for restricted funds](/resources/guides/nonprofit-chart-of-accounts-restricted-funds).

## Use dimensions for grant identity

Grant identity belongs in a separate code. Your system may call it fund, class, project, grant, job, segment, or dimension.

A single salary transaction might carry:

Natural account: Salaries.

Grant or fund: HUD-2026-Housing.

Program: Housing support.

Function: Program services.

Department: Case management.

This structure lets finance report total salaries, all costs for one grant, all costs for one program, or all program service costs without rebuilding the ledger.

## Track restriction status

Under ASC 958, nonprofits report net assets with donor restrictions and net assets without donor restrictions. Your chart and dimensions should support that split.

Restricted grant revenue should be coded so it appears as with donor restrictions when appropriate. Releases from restriction should be tracked clearly. Expenses should connect back to the grant or fund that allowed the release.

Do not use board designations as donor restrictions. If the board sets aside cash for a reserve or project, that is internal. It may be important, but it is not the same as a donor restriction.

For the reporting framework, link to [FASB ASC 958 nonprofit reporting](/resources/guides/fasb-asc-958-nonprofit-reporting).

## Map to funder budgets

Funder budgets often use categories like personnel, fringe, travel, equipment, supplies, contractual, and indirect costs. Your chart may have more detail than that.

Create a budget mapping table. It should show which natural accounts map to each funder budget line. For example, salaries and payroll taxes may both map to personnel and fringe, depending on the funder format.

This mapping helps with reimbursement requests and grant reports. It also helps prevent staff from creating new accounts just to match one funder's form.

For spending reports, use [grant budget tracking](/resources/guides/grant-budget-tracking).

## Keep function separate from program

Program and function are related, but not the same. A program is what the organization does, such as housing, food access, or youth tutoring. A function is how expenses are presented, such as program services, management and general, or fundraising.

Most direct program costs are program service function. But some costs in a program department may still be management and general. Some shared staff may split across functions.

IRS Form 990 Part IX reports functional expenses. Audited financial statements also include functional expense information under ASC 958. A good COA design makes this easier.

## Use account ranges

A clear account range helps staff code consistently:

1000s for assets.

2000s for liabilities.

3000s for net assets.

4000s for revenue.

5000s and 6000s for expenses.

The exact ranges matter less than consistency. Keep enough room for growth, but do not create accounts for every small variation.

## Clean up duplicates

Review the chart at least once a year. Look for duplicate accounts, unused grant accounts, vague names, and old fund codes.

Do not delete accounts that have history unless your accounting system handles it safely. Usually, mark old codes inactive and keep the history.

If you use QuickBooks classes, understand the limits. Classes can help small teams segment activity, but they are not full fund accounting. Link staff to [QuickBooks classes are not fund accounting](/resources/guides/quickbooks-classes-are-not-fund-accounting) if the issue keeps coming up.

When you add a new grant, add the grant or fund code through a short intake step. Finance should confirm the code, restriction class, budget map, and owner before costs are posted. That prevents cleanup later.
