# Auditor confirmation request nonprofit guide

Auditor confirmations can feel outside the nonprofit's control because the auditor sends the request and receives the answer. Staff still have important work to do. They prepare accurate information, identify the right contacts, and help explain any differences.

The goal is simple: make sure outside parties can confirm the same facts the nonprofit has in its records.

## Know what confirmations are for

A confirmation gives the auditor evidence from a party outside the nonprofit. That outside party may confirm a bank balance, debt amount, pledge, grant receivable, legal matter, or other financial fact.

Because the evidence comes from outside the organization, auditors control the process. Staff should not send confirmations on the auditor's behalf or receive the responses directly unless the auditor's process calls for a specific secure method.

Staff can prepare the source schedules and contact lists. They can also follow up with the outside party if the auditor asks them to help with a late response.

## Prepare bank and investment information

Bank confirmations are common. The auditor may ask for bank name, account number, authorized signers, year-end balance, contact information, and online confirmation details.

Before fieldwork, build a list of every bank and investment account. Include closed accounts if they were active during the year. Tie each account to the year-end reconciliation and general ledger.

For each account, save:

- Statement
- Reconciliation
- Ledger balance
- Authorized signer list
- Contact or confirmation platform details
- Notes for closed or new accounts

If the bank confirms a different balance than the ledger, the team needs to explain deposits in transit, outstanding checks, fees, or timing items.

## Prepare debt and lease records

Debt confirmations may cover loans, lines of credit, notes payable, or other obligations. Staff should prepare agreements, payment schedules, interest support, covenant records, and year-end balances.

If the nonprofit has debt tied to property, equipment, or restricted funds, note that in the tracker. The auditor may need to connect the debt to board approvals or grant terms.

Keep lender contacts current. A wrong contact can delay the response and extend fieldwork.

## Prepare grant and pledge receivables

Nonprofits may have grants receivable, pledges receivable, or conditional promises that need review. The auditor may confirm selected balances with funders or donors.

Prepare a receivable schedule that includes name, amount, date, restriction, condition, expected payment date, and source document. Tie the schedule to the general ledger.

Use care with donor contact information. Confirm with the auditor what data is needed and how it will be protected. Do not send donor lists that were not requested.

GrantPipe can help teams keep donor CRM records, grant compliance files, restricted funds, deadlines, audit evidence, and reporting data connected. That makes it easier to trace a receivable back to the agreement, restriction, and report history.

## Prepare legal counsel information

Auditors may request a legal letter from outside counsel. This helps them evaluate claims, litigation, or other matters that may affect the financial statements.

Staff should prepare a list of attorneys used during the year, matters handled, and contact details. The executive director or board chair may need to review the list.

Do not summarize legal matters casually. Work with counsel and the auditor. Legal letters can be sensitive, and the wording matters.

## Track every request

Add confirmations to the audit tracker. Use columns for type, outside party, contact, related balance, date sent, response date, exception, owner, and status.

The tracker should show which confirmations are late. If a response is delayed, ask the auditor whether staff should contact the outside party. Keep the auditor in control of the confirmation process.

## Review exceptions quickly

An exception does not always mean an error. It may be timing, a different cut-off date, a name mismatch, or a balance that includes items not in the ledger.

When an exception arrives, compare the response to the source schedule. Then prepare a short explanation with supporting records. Avoid long narrative answers without evidence.

Examples of support include reconciliations, payment records, award letters, pledge agreements, board minutes, or legal correspondence.

## Keep final support with audit records

Save the tracker, source schedules, and exception explanations with the audit file. The confirmation responses themselves may stay in the auditor's workpapers, but your support should remain available for next year.

Strong confirmation prep is quiet work. It is clean schedules, current contacts, and records that tie out. That is what helps outside evidence match the nonprofit's books.
