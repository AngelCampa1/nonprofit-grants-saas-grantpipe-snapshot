Public support is easy to ignore until Form 990 season. That is the wrong time to discover that one large source changed the organization's support mix.

For many public charities, Schedule A is where the organization shows why it qualifies as a public charity. The details vary by organization type and support test. The operating habit is the same: track revenue by source during the year, not after the year is closed.

This guide is not tax advice. It is an operating checklist for executive directors, development leads, and finance teams so the tax preparer is not handed a mystery.

## Build the support source file

Start with a support source file that lists revenue by source, date, amount, restriction, and category. At minimum, include:

- individual contributions
- foundation grants
- corporate grants
- government grants
- membership dues, if relevant
- program service revenue
- investment income, if relevant
- related-party support
- large one-time gifts or grants

Do not rely on account names alone. A revenue account called "foundation grants" may hide one donor concentration problem or one unusual grant question.

## Watch concentration before year-end

The public support test is not only an accounting issue. It is also a fundraising strategy signal.

If one donor, foundation, or related group is becoming a large share of support, leadership should know before the year closes. That does not mean the organization should reject support. It means the board and staff should understand the public charity status implications and ask the tax preparer how the gift or grant should be treated.

GrantPipe can help here by keeping grant revenue, donor records, restrictions, and board reporting in the same operating view. That makes it easier to see when one funding source is carrying too much of the plan.

## Flag unusual grants early

Schedule A instructions include treatment for unusual grants. Do not assume every large grant qualifies. Keep the award letter, grant purpose, donor history, relationship notes, and board context in one place so the preparer can evaluate it.

The practical rule: if a grant is large enough that leadership mentions it in the board packet, it is large enough to flag for Schedule A review.

## Tie Schedule A to Form 990 review

The public support file should sit next to the Form 990 board review packet. Board members do not need to calculate Schedule A line by line. They do need to understand broad support, revenue concentration, and any judgment calls that could affect public charity status.

Ask these questions before filing:

1. Are large donors and grantors identified clearly?
2. Are government grants separated from private foundation grants?
3. Are related-party sources flagged?
4. Are unusual grants documented?
5. Does Schedule A tie to the financial statements and revenue detail?

If the answer to any question is no, fix the support file before the return is filed.

## Keep fundraising and finance aligned

Development teams often think in relationships. Finance teams think in records. Schedule A needs both.

Development can identify donor relationships, grant history, and whether a gift was expected or unusual. Finance can tie the amounts to the ledger. The executive director can decide when the board needs to see concentration risk.

Public support should not be a surprise buried in tax prep. It should be part of the same revenue review leaders already use to manage the organization.

## Add a board warning threshold

Set a simple internal threshold for review. For example, any source that may represent a large share of support over the measurement period should be flagged for the finance committee. The threshold is not a tax rule. It is a management trigger.

The trigger should prompt three actions. First, confirm the source classification with finance. Second, ask whether the gift or grant may need special Schedule A treatment. Third, decide whether the revenue plan is too dependent on one relationship.

This turns public support from a once-a-year tax worksheet into a board-level risk signal. The board does not need to micromanage donor strategy. It does need to know when a funding pattern could affect public charity status or future fundraising flexibility.
