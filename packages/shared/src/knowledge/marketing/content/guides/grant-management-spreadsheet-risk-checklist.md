# Grant management spreadsheet risk checklist

Spreadsheets are not the enemy. Many nonprofits use them because they are cheap, flexible, and familiar. The risk starts when the spreadsheet becomes the place where grant truth lives, while finance, documents, and reports live somewhere else.

Use this checklist before audit season, before a software search, or when a staff member who owns the file is leaving. For a software comparison, read [Excel grant management vs software](/resources/guides/excel-grant-management-vs-software).

## 1. The spreadsheet does not match the grant agreement

Open one active award. Compare the spreadsheet to the signed agreement and approved budget.

Check:

- award amount
- start and end dates
- budget categories
- match rules
- report dates
- allowed changes
- closeout terms

If the spreadsheet uses old terms, staff may make spending or reporting choices from stale data.

## 2. The spreadsheet does not match finance

2 CFR 200.302 requires financial records that identify the source and use of federal funds. A grant spreadsheet should support finance records, not compete with them.

Reconcile the spreadsheet to the accounting system each month. Compare expenses, remaining budget, restricted balance, and any receivable or deferred revenue treatment. If the numbers differ, write down the reason.

## 3. Formulas are hidden or fragile

Formula errors are hard to see. A copied row, deleted cell, broken range, or manual override can change the result.

Protect formula cells. Keep one version owner. Add a review tab with the date, reviewer, and issues found. If the file drives board or funder reports, a second person should check it.

## 4. Deadlines are split from work

A report date in a spreadsheet is useful only if it connects to the work needed before the due date. Each report should show owner, data source, document needs, internal review date, due date, submission method, and proof of submission.

For federal performance reporting, 2 CFR 200.329 sets the broad reporting rule. Your award may add agency terms. The spreadsheet should link to those terms.

## 5. Documents are not linked

If the spreadsheet says a report was submitted, it should link to the report. If it says a cost was approved, it should link to the approval. If it says a budget was revised, it should link to the amendment.

Use the [grant document management requirements guide](/resources/guides/grant-document-management-system-requirements) to define the file list. A spreadsheet with no document path is only a summary.

## 6. One person knows how it works

This is a serious risk. If one grants manager or finance staff member can explain every tab, formula, and color code, the organization has a person-dependent control.

Write a short file guide. Include tab purpose, data sources, update cadence, protected ranges, and review steps. Store it beside the file.

## 7. Prior versions are missing

Version history matters when numbers change. Keep a copy at month-end close, after budget revisions, after reports, and at closeout. Name files with the grant, date, and status.

Cloud history helps, but do not rely on it as the only record. Make sure the organization can find the version used for each submitted report.

## 8. Access is too broad

Too many editors create quiet risk. Staff who need visibility may not need edit rights. Auditors may need review access only.

Limit edit access. Use read-only access for reviewers. Keep a record of who can change the file.

## 9. Closeout and retention are not tracked

A grant is not done when the program period ends. Final reports, final invoices, property records, and retention rules may continue.

Federal grant records are generally retained for three years after final report submission under 2 CFR 200.334, with exceptions. Your spreadsheet should show the final report date and retention end date.

## 10. Staff rebuild the same answers

If your team rebuilds spend-down, report status, document status, and restricted balances each month, the spreadsheet is costing more than it appears. That time is part of the real cost.

## Make a decision threshold

Do not wait for a failure to decide when spreadsheets are no longer enough. Set a threshold now.

Common thresholds include five or more active grants, more than one federal award, monthly reconciliation taking more than four hours, more than one staff member editing the same tracker, or repeated report rework. Your threshold may be lower if the grants are complex or restricted funds are material to the budget.

When the threshold is crossed, the next step does not have to be a big system purchase. It may be a better template, a locked workbook, a shared review meeting, or a focused grant tool. The point is to make the risk visible before it becomes urgent.

GrantPipe can help teams connect awards, restricted funds, reports, and documents in one place. It is not the only option. The decision point is whether your current spreadsheet can still be checked, trusted, and handed to the next person.
