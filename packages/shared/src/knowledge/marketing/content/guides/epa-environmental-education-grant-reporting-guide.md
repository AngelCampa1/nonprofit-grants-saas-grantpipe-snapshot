# EPA environmental education grant reporting guide

EPA environmental education grants fund projects that help people understand environmental issues and make informed choices. Reporting should show both sides of the award: what the project did and how the money was used.

The best reports are built all year, not at the due date. Keep activity records, participant support, budget support, and partner records in the grant file as work happens.

## Start with the award and work plan

Read the EPA assistance agreement, approved work plan, budget, and terms and conditions. Pull out each reporting duty, due date, measure, and required format. Put those dates in a [grant reporting calendar](/resources/guides/grant-reporting-calendar-template).

The work plan should drive the report. If the work plan promised teacher workshops, student lessons, field events, or community training, the report should show progress against those items.

## Define outputs and outcomes

Outputs are things you can count. Examples include workshops held, participants served, materials produced, partner meetings, and lessons delivered. Outcomes show what changed. Examples include knowledge gained, skills learned, behavior changed, or decisions informed.

Use the same terms in each report. If one report says "attendees" and another says "participants," define whether they mean the same thing. This keeps annual totals clean.

## Keep participant support

Participant counts should tie to source records. Use sign-in sheets, registration exports, rosters, learning platform reports, or partner attendance records. If the project works with minors, follow your privacy and school partner rules. Do not include unnecessary personal data in a grant report packet.

For public events, keep event date, location, topic, facilitator, count method, and materials used. If the count is estimated, label it as an estimate and explain the method. Do not present estimates as exact counts.

## Keep evaluation records

If the work plan includes learning goals, keep the evaluation method and results. That might include pre- and post-session surveys, teacher feedback, quiz summaries, or observation notes. Store the blank tool, the summary, and the method used to calculate results.

Do not overstate outcomes. If the project measured attendance, call it attendance. If it measured knowledge gain with a survey, explain the survey and result. Clear limits make the report more credible.

## Save curriculum and materials

Keep copies of lesson plans, handouts, slides, evaluation tools, outreach emails, and partner-approved materials. If materials were adapted during the project, keep the final version and note the change.

Reports are stronger when they can point to what was delivered. A sentence saying "we educated students" is weak. A report backed by lesson titles, dates, rosters, and evaluation summaries is easier to trust.

## Tie costs to activities

Financial reports should tie to the grant ledger and approved budget. Payroll should tie to time records. Supplies should tie to invoices. Travel should tie to policy, receipts, and the project purpose. Subawards or partner payments should tie to agreements and deliverables.

Use the [allowable costs guide](/resources/guides/allowable-costs-federal-grants) before charging unusual costs. EPA terms and conditions can add award-specific duties, so keep the current terms with the file.

## Track partners and subawards

Environmental education projects often include schools, community groups, local agencies, or technical partners. Keep partner agreements, roles, deliverables, and reports. If a partner is a subrecipient, follow EPA subaward policy and Uniform Guidance monitoring duties.

Do not treat every partner as a vendor by default. The relationship depends on what the partner does. If the partner carries out part of the federal award, subrecipient rules may apply.

## Explain changes early

If the project falls behind, the report should say why and what will change. If a budget line needs to move, ask for approval before making changes that require it. Keep approval with the report and budget file.

Plain explanations are better than vague progress claims. Say "two school events moved from March to May because the district closed for weather" if that is what happened.

## Reconcile before submission

Before each report, compare the activity log, participant support, partner records, and budget ledger. The dates should line up. If a workshop is reported in April, the attendance record and related costs should fall in the same period or have a clear timing note.

This review helps catch two errors: reporting activities before they happen and charging costs to a project activity that was canceled or moved.

## Close with a complete file

At the final report, compare the work plan, outputs, outcomes, final costs, and remaining commitments. Confirm all reports were submitted and all costs were recorded. Keep final reports, submission proof, partner records, source support, and closeout correspondence.

2 CFR 200.329 covers performance reporting. EPA terms and conditions may add more detail. Use the [federal grant reporting requirements guide](/resources/guides/federal-grant-reporting-requirements) to keep the full reporting file organized.
