# Nonprofit grant calendar quarterly planning guide

A grant calendar should show more than due dates. It should show the work needed before each due date. That includes applications, reports, renewals, reimbursement requests, budget reviews, evidence checks, and closeout tasks.

Quarterly planning keeps the calendar from becoming a list people ignore. Once each quarter, the grants lead should bring finance, program, and leadership into the same view. The team should decide what is due, what is at risk, and what needs help.

## Build one shared calendar

Many nonprofits have more than one grant calendar. Development tracks applications. Finance tracks reimbursement requests. Program staff track report data. The executive director tracks funder meetings. That split creates risk.

Create one shared calendar with fields for:

- Grant or prospect name
- Funder
- Deadline type
- Due date
- Owner
- Reviewer
- Status
- Source document
- Evidence needed
- Related restricted fund
- Notes

The calendar can live in software, a database, or a careful spreadsheet. The format matters less than the discipline. Every active award and serious prospect should appear in one place.

GrantPipe can help teams connect grant deadlines with donor CRM records, restricted funds, audit evidence, and reporting tasks. That connection matters when a report needs both program data and finance support.

## Sort deadlines by type

A due date is easier to manage when the team knows what kind of work it represents.

Use deadline types such as:

- Application
- Letter of inquiry
- Renewal
- Interim report
- Final report
- Financial report
- Reimbursement request
- Budget revision
- Board approval
- Funder meeting
- Closeout
- Record retention review

Federal awards may include financial and performance reports. Grants.gov describes grant reporting as part of the post-award process. 2 CFR 200.329 covers performance reporting for federal awards, and 2 CFR 200.344 covers closeout. Private funders may use different names, but the same calendar logic applies.

## Run a quarterly planning meeting

Schedule one meeting near the start of each quarter. Keep it focused.

Review the next 120 days:

- What is due?
- What is late?
- What needs finance review?
- What needs program data?
- What needs executive approval?
- What needs board action?
- What can be declined or deferred?

Do not let the meeting become a full proposal review. The goal is to assign work and reduce surprises.

## Add preparation dates

The due date is not the first work date. For each major deadline, add internal preparation dates.

For a report, add:

- Data pull date
- Finance review date
- Draft date
- Program review date
- Executive review date
- Submit date
- Evidence save date

For an application, add:

- Fit review date
- Budget draft date
- Attachment request date
- Narrative draft date
- Review date
- Portal check date
- Submit date

This makes hidden work visible. It also shows when one staff member has too many deadlines in the same week.

## Tie the calendar to monthly close

Grant planning and finance close should talk to each other. A report deadline may need current expenses. A reimbursement request may need payroll allocation support. A renewal may need a budget to actual view.

Each month, finance should flag:

- Grants with unusual variances
- Grants with late reimbursements
- Costs waiting on support
- Restricted funds that need review
- Reports due before the next close
- Awards near end date

Put those flags on the calendar. The team should not discover a budget issue while writing the final report.

## Track evidence before reports are due

Audit evidence should be saved during the grant, not rebuilt at the end. Add evidence tasks to the calendar.

Examples:

- Save award agreement
- Save approved budget
- Save report submission proof
- Save payroll support
- Save match records
- Save invoices
- Save attendance logs
- Save outcome summary
- Save closeout confirmation

2 CFR 200.334 covers federal record retention requirements. Funder terms may require a longer period. The calendar should point staff to the retention rule or award term so records are not deleted too early.

## Use a color or status system

Keep status labels simple:

- Not started
- On track
- Needs review
- At risk
- Submitted
- Complete

Do not use too many labels. Staff should know what each one means. "At risk" should trigger action, not shame. It means a deadline, source record, owner, or decision needs help.

## End each quarter with cleanup

At the end of the quarter, archive completed items and update open ones.

Ask:

- Were any deadlines missed?
- Were any reports harder than expected?
- Did any funder change a date?
- Did any award need a budget revision?
- Are next quarter owners assigned?
- Are completed reports saved?

A grant calendar is a control tool. It helps the nonprofit protect deadlines, reduce rushed work, and keep grant compliance visible before problems pile up.
