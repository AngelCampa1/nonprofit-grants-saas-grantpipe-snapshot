Month-end close is where fund accounting becomes real. The chart of accounts may have fund codes. The budget may have grant codes. The donor CRM may have gift restrictions. The board report may have a net asset summary. If those views do not tie together, leaders make decisions from numbers they cannot trust.

A month-end tieout does not need to be fancy. It needs to be repeatable. It should show what changed this month, what remains restricted, which grant deadlines are near, and which numbers still need review.

This guide gives a practical checklist for mid-sized nonprofits. It is not accounting advice. Use your own policies and adviser for final treatment.

## Start with the fund trial balance

Pull the trial balance by fund. Review each active fund for beginning balance, revenue, expenses, transfers, releases, and ending balance.

Look for strange patterns first. A restricted fund with a negative balance needs review. A fund with revenue but no cash receipt may need receivable support. A fund with expenses outside the grant period may need a correction.

Do not wait until every bank item is perfect before scanning the fund view. Early review catches coding problems while the close is still open.

## Tie restricted activity to source records

Each restricted gift or grant should tie to a source record. Use the donor letter, grant agreement, award notice, sponsor letter, or board designation record.

The source record should answer:

- Who gave or awarded the funds?
- What amount was restricted?
- What is the purpose or time limit?
- Are there conditions?
- What report or deadline applies?
- Who owns follow-up?

GrantPipe can help connect donor CRM notes, grant compliance tasks, restricted funds, deadlines, reporting, and audit evidence. The month-end tieout still depends on finance review, but the support should be easier to find.

## Reconcile releases from restriction

Pull release entries for the month. For each release, confirm the restriction was met. Tie purpose releases to qualifying costs or program records. Tie time releases to the date. Tie grant releases to award terms and expenses.

The release entry should not be a plug. It should have support. If support is missing, mark the release as pending and assign the fix.

Use a rollforward:

```
Beginning restricted balance
+ New restricted revenue
- Releases from restriction
+ or - corrections
= Ending restricted balance
```

Then compare the ending balance to the financial statement line for net assets with donor restrictions.

## Tie grants to budget and reports

For each active grant, compare monthly and life-to-date spending to the approved budget. Review reports due soon, budget lines near limits, and costs waiting on support.

If a grant is reimbursement based, tie receivables to submitted or prepared reimbursement requests. If it uses advances or drawdowns, tie cash to spending and payment rules. For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.302 expects records that identify the source and use of funds.

This is where deadlines matter. A clean ledger is not enough if the report is late or the file is missing evidence.

## Review cash without overusing it

Cash should be reconciled to bank records. It should also be reviewed against restricted needs. But cash is not the same as restricted net assets.

Create a short cash view:

- Bank balance
- Restricted cash estimate
- Grant reimbursement receivables
- Open payables tied to grants
- Cash expected soon
- Cash needed before reimbursement

This helps the executive director understand which dollars are flexible and which dollars are tied to donor or grant terms.

## Review receivables and payables

Receivables can make restricted funds look healthy before cash arrives. Payables can make spending look lower if bills are not posted. Review both.

Tie each grant receivable to a reimbursement request, award schedule, or pledge support. Tie each grant payable to the right fund, grant, and period.

If an expense belongs in the month but the invoice is late, note the accrual treatment under your policy. If a receivable looks old, explain the collection plan.

## Save corrections and approvals

Month-end corrections are normal. The control is whether they are clear. Each correction should show the original entry, corrected entry, reason, support, and approval.

Avoid vague memos. "Move $1,260 from unrestricted supplies to Grant A because the invoice supports approved program supplies dated June 18" is useful.

Save reviewer notes with the close packet. A future audit request should not require staff to rebuild the month from memory.

## Give leaders a short close summary

After the tieout, prepare a short summary for leadership. Include total restricted balance, releases, large new restricted gifts, grant reports due soon, open evidence issues, and cash pressure.

Keep it plain. Board and staff leaders need enough to act. They do not need every ledger line.

## Common mistakes

Common mistakes include closing before release support is ready, treating restricted cash as the restricted balance, ignoring grant receivables, missing payables, and leaving old fund codes open.

Another mistake is letting each system tell a different story. The donor CRM says one purpose. The ledger says another. The board report says only a total. A month-end tieout forces those facts into one review.

A good tieout makes the next report easier. It also makes audit evidence easier to collect because the organization saved the proof while the month was fresh.
