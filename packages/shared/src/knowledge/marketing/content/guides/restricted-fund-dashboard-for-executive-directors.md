A separate dashboard design guide already covers the visual layout of a restricted funds dashboard. This page has a narrower job: help a nonprofit turn that data into a monthly executive briefing the executive director can use before leadership meetings, board packets, and funder conversations.

The briefing should answer four questions: what changed, what money is restricted, what can be released, and what needs leadership action.

This is especially important for grant funded nonprofits. A healthy total cash balance can hide a short unrestricted position. A large restricted balance can look strong while the organization struggles to cover general costs.

## Start with the decision, not the chart

Before choosing briefing fields, list the decisions the executive director needs to make. Common decisions include whether to slow spending, pursue a budget revision, ask a funder for clarification, approve a release, notify the board, or plan cash coverage.

Each briefing element should support one of those decisions. If a number does not help a decision, move it to a detail report.

## Show restricted balances by type

Group balances in a way leaders can understand. Useful groups include donor restricted funds, grant restricted funds, board designated funds, endowments, reimbursable grants, and temporarily restricted net assets.

Do not mix board designated funds with donor restricted funds without a label. Board designations can usually be changed by the board. Donor and funder restrictions require meeting the terms.

FASB's not for profit reporting model uses net assets with donor restrictions and net assets without donor restrictions. Your dashboard can use plain labels, but the accounting treatment should stay correct.

## Show release conditions

A balance alone is not enough. The dashboard should say what must happen before funds can be released or spent.

Examples include time period met, eligible costs incurred, program milestone completed, report approved, reimbursement submitted, or donor purpose fulfilled.

This helps leaders avoid two mistakes: treating restricted funds like free cash or leaving releasable funds sitting too long.

## Add spenddown and timing

For each major grant or restricted fund, show period end date, remaining balance, expected spending pace, and risk.

A grant with $80,000 left and 10 months remaining may be fine. A grant with $80,000 left and 30 days remaining needs attention.

Use simple risk labels like on track, watch, and action needed. Add owner and next step for every action item.

## Include cash context

Restricted fund reports should connect to cash when cash is a leadership issue. Show whether restricted cash, reimbursable grant timing, or delayed releases affect the operating cash picture.

For reimbursement grants, show costs incurred but not yet reimbursed. That helps leaders understand why cash may be tight even when grant revenue is expected.

For federal awards, 2 CFR 200.302 points to records that identify the source and application of funds. The dashboard should not replace accounting records, but it should reflect them.

## Show risk and exceptions

Add a short section for items that need attention. Examples include missing release support, unclear donor terms, reports past due, costs coded to the wrong fund, fund balances that do not tie to the ledger, and grants near end date with large balances.

Each exception needs an owner and next action. A dashboard full of warnings but no owners will not change behavior.

## Keep donor and client privacy in mind

Executive views do not always need donor names, client names, or detailed documents. Use fund names, grant names, and summary notes unless detail is needed for a decision.

If the dashboard is used in a board packet, review what should be shared with the full board and what belongs in a finance committee detail packet.

## Tie the dashboard to month end

Update the dashboard after the restricted fund reconciliation. If it is updated before finance closes the month, label it as preliminary.

The dashboard should tie to the ledger, restricted fund schedule, grant spenddown, and release entries. If it does not tie, leaders will lose trust in it.

GrantPipe can help connect funds, grants, reports, and evidence so the dashboard points back to the records behind each balance.

## Use a short board note

For board packets, add a short note under the dashboard. Explain the main change since last month, the largest risk, and any decision needed.

Example: "Restricted balances decreased because two program milestones were met and released. The housing grant remains on watch because reimbursement is pending. No board action is needed this month."

That note helps board members read the numbers without guessing.

## Review before sharing

Before sharing the dashboard, finance should confirm balances and releases. Grants should confirm report and funder status. The executive director should confirm the action notes.

Save each monthly version. Over time, the dashboard becomes a record of oversight and management response, not just a snapshot.
