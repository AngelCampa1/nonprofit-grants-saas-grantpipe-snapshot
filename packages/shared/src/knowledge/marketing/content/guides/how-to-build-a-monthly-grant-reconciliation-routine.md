steps:
  - title: "Step 1: Bank and ledger reconciliation"
    content: "The first step confirms that money received for grants has been properly deposited and recorded. Run the bank statement for the month and compare it against the grant receipts posted in the general ledger. For each grant cash receipt on the bank statement, verify that the corresponding accounting entry exists, that it was coded to the correct fund, and that the amount matches the bank deposit exactly. Flag any bank receipts without a corresponding GL entry - these are unrecorded grant income and must be entered before the month is closed. Flag any GL entries for grant income without a corresponding bank receipt - these may be premature entries for pledged grants not yet received. This step should take thirty to sixty minutes for a typical month. Months with significant grant activity will take longer."
  - title: "Step 2: Grant-to-ledger reconciliation"
    content: "The second step compares each active grant's cumulative expenditures in the grant management system (or grant tracking spreadsheet) against the corresponding cumulative expenditures in the general ledger. Run an expenditure report from your accounting system for each active grant fund code for the period. Compare against the grant record's budget vs. actual tracking. Every expense posted to a grant fund code in the GL should appear in the grant tracking record with the same amount, the same budget category, and the same posting date. Discrepancies indicate one of three problems: an expense was coded to the wrong grant in the GL, an expense was recorded in the grant tracking system but not yet posted to the GL, or an expense was posted to the GL under the grant fund code but should not have been charged to the grant at all. Each discrepancy requires investigation and correction before the month closes."
  - title: "Step 3: Restricted balance check"
    content: "The third step verifies that each restricted fund's balance in the accounting system correctly reflects the grant award minus expenditures to date. For each active restricted grant, pull the fund balance from the accounting system and compare it to the expected balance: award amount received to date, minus cumulative expenditures, equals expected remaining balance. If the balance in the accounting system differs from the expected balance, there is a posting error somewhere - either a grant receipt was not properly recorded as restricted, an expenditure was not charged to the correct fund, or an incorrect journal entry was made. Do not close the month with unexplained restricted balance discrepancies. These discrepancies, if carried forward without resolution, compound over time and create the reconciliation nightmares that finance directors discover at year-end audit. Document the expected balance, the GL balance, and the outcome of any investigation."
  - title: "Step 4: Upcoming drawdown review"
    content: "The fourth step is forward-looking: review drawdown eligibility and upcoming obligations for the next thirty to sixty days. For federal grants using advance or reimbursement payment systems (ASAP, HHS Pay.gov, Grants.gov), check whether drawdown requests are needed for the period. Confirm that the expenditures supporting the drawdown are posted and documented - you cannot draw down against expenses that are not in the system. For grants with quarterly or semi-annual funder reports due in the next sixty days, confirm that expenditure data is current and that any documentation needed for the report (receipts, time records, contractor invoices) is on file. This step prevents the common problem of discovering a drawdown is past due or a funder report is missing documentation at the moment it needs to be submitted."
  - title: "Document the reconciliation for auditors"
    content: "The reconciliation is only as useful as its documentation. Create a reconciliation summary for each month that includes: the period covered, the date completed, the name of the staff member who performed the reconciliation, a summary of each grant reviewed (fund balance per GL, expected balance, variance if any, resolution), a list of any exceptions or errors found and how they were corrected, and sign-off by the finance director or CFO. This document goes into the grant file for each grant and into the monthly close documentation for the period. When an auditor tests restricted fund compliance, this documentation demonstrates that the organization actively monitored fund balances throughout the year - not just at year-end. A grant file with twelve months of reconciliation documentation is a substantially more defensible audit position than one with only year-end documentation."
  - title: "Handle discrepancies that do not resolve"
    content: "Some discrepancies require more investigation than the monthly close process allows. When a discrepancy cannot be resolved within the reconciliation period, document it as an open item: what was found, what investigation was initiated, what evidence was reviewed, and what the remaining question is. Open items should be reviewed at the next monthly reconciliation and resolved or escalated. An open item that carries for more than two consecutive monthly reconciliations should be escalated to the finance director or executive director. Unresolved reconciling items are audit findings waiting to happen - they do not go away by being left open."
definitions:
  - term: Fund balance
    definition: The net assets in a specific restricted fund, calculated as all revenue received under the restriction minus all allowable expenses charged to the fund. A fund balance should equal zero at grant closeout if all funds were expended correctly, or a positive amount if there are unspent funds to be returned.
  - term: Drawdown
    definition: A request to receive grant funds from a federal payment system (such as ASAP or HHS Payment Management System). The grantee draws funds either in advance of anticipated expenditures or as reimbursement for actual costs. Drawdowns must be supported by posted expenditures and reconciled to the accounting system.
  - term: GL coding error
    definition: An expense posted to the wrong general ledger fund code - for example, a salaries expense that should have been split between two grant funds was charged entirely to one. GL coding errors affect restricted fund balances, compliance documentation, and funder reports. They should be corrected in the period they are discovered, not left to correct at year-end.

The monthly grant reconciliation routine is the practice that separates organizations that are in compliance from organizations that think they are in compliance. The difference is not effort - it is timing.

An organization that reconciles monthly catches a GL coding error in the month it occurs. The correction is straightforward: reclassify the entry, update the fund balance, document the change. An organization that reconciles annually catches the same error eleven months later, after the error has affected eleven subsequent months of reporting, after staff who might remember the context have moved on, and after the restricted fund balance has been reported incorrectly in interim financial statements.

The work is the same. The consequences are not.

## Why Monthly Is the Right Cadence

Grant compliance documentation is time-sensitive in a way that general accounting is not. General ledger errors can be corrected retroactively with journal entries. Grant compliance errors - expenses charged to the wrong grant, drawdowns not supported by posted expenses, restricted fund balances that do not match the accounting records - have compliance implications if they are not caught and corrected.

The monthly cadence keeps the investigation window small. When something does not reconcile, the finance staff member performing the reconciliation has just reviewed that month's transactions and knows what happened. The bank statements are available. The invoices are accessible. The staff member who approved the expense is still in the same role.

At the annual close, none of those advantages exist. The investigation reaches back twelve months into transactions that no one remembers clearly. The paperwork trail has gaps. The corrections take longer and are less certain.

The monthly reconciliation also creates the documentation trail auditors want to see. A grant file with twelve reconciliation summaries showing that balances were checked monthly, discrepancies were found and corrected, and open items were tracked to resolution is a grant file that passes audit. A grant file with one year-end reconciliation that magically shows everything is in order raises questions.

## How the Four Steps Connect

The four steps are designed to move from general to specific: bank confirms the cash is right, grant-to-ledger confirms the coding is right, restricted balance confirms the fund math is right, and drawdown review confirms the forward obligations are managed.

Each step builds on the previous one. If the bank reconciliation finds an unrecorded deposit, that affects the grant-to-ledger comparison, which affects the restricted balance calculation. Doing the steps in order prevents rework.

Each step also has a specific documentation artifact: the bank reconciliation workpaper, the grant-to-GL comparison, the restricted balance summary, and the drawdown checklist. These four documents, produced monthly and signed by the preparer and reviewer, are the reconciliation documentation. They should live in the grant file and in the monthly close folder, organized by period.

## What Good Documentation Looks Like

The purpose of documentation is to enable a reader who was not present during the reconciliation to understand what was done, what was found, and how exceptions were resolved. Good documentation is complete enough to stand alone.

A useful reconciliation summary includes the period covered, the grants reviewed, the expected and actual balances for each, a note on any discrepancies and how they were resolved, and the signature of the preparer and reviewer. It does not need to be elaborate. A one-page summary per month is sufficient. The underlying workpapers - the GL reports, the comparison spreadsheets - are attachments.

What the documentation should not do is simply state "reconciliation complete" without showing the work. That is not documentation; it is an assertion. Auditors test assertions. They cannot test documentation that shows the comparison, the discrepancies, and the resolutions.

## Building the Routine Into the Monthly Close Calendar

Monthly grant reconciliation works best when it is a scheduled calendar item rather than a task that happens when finance staff find time. Put it on the monthly close calendar with a specific date - typically five to seven business days after month-end, after bank statements are available and after the last period transactions have been posted.

Assign a specific person as the preparer and a different person as the reviewer. The preparer performs the reconciliation; the reviewer confirms it. The two-person process catches the errors the preparer missed and creates an independent check on the work.

For small finance teams where two people are not available, the finance director can serve as reviewer. The preparer completes the reconciliation workpapers; the finance director reviews and signs off before the monthly close is certified.

The routine becomes reliable when it is built into the calendar, assigned to specific people, and tied to a deliverable - the reconciliation documentation - rather than a general intention to stay current on grant tracking.
