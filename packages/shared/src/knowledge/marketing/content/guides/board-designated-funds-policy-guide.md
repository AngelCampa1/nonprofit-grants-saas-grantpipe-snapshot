Board designated funds are useful. They help a nonprofit plan for reserves, building repairs, program growth, cash gaps, or strategic projects. But they can also confuse reports if the policy is weak.

The most important rule is simple: board designated funds are not donor restricted funds. They are still net assets without donor restrictions under ASC 958. The board has chosen to set them aside, and the board can usually change that choice.

## State the purpose

The policy should explain why the board uses designations. Common purposes include:

- Operating reserve
- Cash flow reserve
- Facility reserve
- Technology reserve
- Strategic project fund
- Emergency fund

Use plain names. Avoid vague labels like "special fund" unless the policy explains the use.

For reserve planning, link to [nonprofit reserve fund guide](/resources/guides/nonprofit-reserve-fund-guide).

## Make the restriction distinction clear

Add a section that separates board designations from donor restrictions.

Donor restrictions come from donors or grantors. They may limit use by purpose, time, or both. The board cannot remove them just because cash is needed.

Board designations come from the board. They are internal. They help manage money, but they do not change the donor restriction class.

This distinction matters for financial statements, board reports, and audit work. For a detailed comparison, link to [donor restricted vs board designated funds](/resources/guides/donor-restricted-vs-board-designated-funds).

## Define approval rules

The policy should say who can create a designation. For most nonprofits, the full board should approve material designations. The finance committee may recommend them.

The approval should include:

- Name of the fund
- Purpose
- Target amount
- Source of funding
- Conditions for use
- Review date

Put the approval in board minutes. Minutes help show that the designation was intentional and internal.

## Define release rules

Because designations are internal, the board can release or change them. The policy should say how.

For example:

The executive director may request use of designated funds.

The finance committee reviews the request.

The full board approves releases above a set threshold.

Finance records the change and updates board reports.

The policy should also allow urgent use if cash is needed before the next board meeting, but that rule should include a reporting step after the fact.

## Set targets, not vague goals

A reserve policy should use a measurable target. For example, the operating reserve target may be three months of average operating expenses. A facility reserve may target a board-approved capital plan.

Do not write that the organization will keep "adequate reserves" without defining adequate. The board needs a clear test.

If the organization cannot fund the reserve yet, say that. The policy can include a plan to build the reserve over time.

## Report designations separately but correctly

Board reports can show board designated balances in a separate table. Financial statements may disclose board designations in notes or show them in internal schedules.

Still, the total belongs within net assets without donor restrictions. Do not combine board designated balances with net assets with donor restrictions.

A good board report shows:

- Total net assets without donor restrictions
- Board designated portion
- Undesignated portion
- Net assets with donor restrictions

This helps leaders see both board choices and donor limits.

## Review at least once a year

Board designations should not sit untouched forever. Review them during budget work or year-end close.

Ask:

Does the purpose still matter?

Is the target amount still right?

Was money used as approved?

Should the designation be increased, lowered, or released?

Are board reports clear?

Document the review in minutes.

## Avoid common errors

Do not call a board designation restricted unless the donor restricted it.

Do not spend donor restricted funds for a board reserve.

Do not create designations that hide financial stress.

Do not let old designated funds remain without a purpose.

Do not use designations to avoid honest budget decisions.

The policy should help the board govern, not decorate the balance sheet.

## Connect the policy to budget work

The policy should show how designations fit the annual budget. If the budget assumes use of a reserve, the board should see that planned use before approving the budget. If the budget plans to add money to a reserve, show the amount and source.

This avoids surprises later. The treasurer can explain whether a transfer was part of the approved budget, a new board action, or an emergency use. Finance can then report the designation change in the same month it happens.

Keep the language plain in the board packet. Say "the board set aside $40,000 for the building reserve" or "the board approved using $15,000 from the operating reserve." Do not make internal designations sound like donor limits.
