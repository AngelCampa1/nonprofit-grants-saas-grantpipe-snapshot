# Subrecipient risk assessment scoring guide

When your nonprofit passes federal funds to another organization, you become a pass-through entity. Under 2 CFR 200.332, you must evaluate each subrecipient's risk of noncompliance and use that risk to choose monitoring. The regulation names risk factors, but it does not give a point scale. That part is management's job.

This guide gives a simple scoring model. Use it with the [subrecipient monitoring requirements guide](/resources/guides/subrecipient-monitoring-requirements-uniform-guidance) and your written monitoring policy.

## First, confirm it is a subrecipient

Do not score a vendor as a subrecipient. Apply the 2 CFR 200.331 substance test first. A subrecipient carries out part of the federal program and is accountable for program results. A contractor sells goods or services in normal business.

Document the decision before the risk score. If the relationship is mixed, explain why management chose subrecipient or contractor treatment.

## Use a consistent point scale

A simple 1 to 3 score works for small teams:

- 1 point: low risk
- 2 points: moderate risk
- 3 points: high risk

Score each factor, total the points, and assign a tier. For example, 6 to 8 points may be low, 9 to 13 moderate, and 14 or more high. Adjust the ranges if your policy uses more factors.

The exact math matters less than consistency. Use the same model across subrecipients unless the policy explains an exception.

## Factor 1: prior experience

Score the subrecipient's experience with similar federal awards. A long-time partner with clean reports may score 1. A new partner with no federal award history may score 3.

Look at the same kind of work, not just any grant. A food pantry with strong USDA experience may still be new to a workforce grant with participant eligibility rules.

## Factor 2: audit history

Review the most recent single audit if the subrecipient had one. Findings tied to the same program or compliance area should raise the score. Repeated findings should raise it more.

If the subrecipient is below the single audit threshold, ask for financial statements, board-approved budget, or other proof of financial controls. No audit does not mean no risk.

## Factor 3: staff and system changes

2 CFR 200.332 points to new personnel or new systems as a risk factor. Score higher when the subrecipient has a new finance lead, new grant manager, new accounting system, or major turnover.

System changes can be good, but they create transition risk. A new accounting system may need extra report review for the first few months.

## Factor 4: award size and complexity

A larger subaward deserves more attention. So does a subaward with participant eligibility, procurement, equipment, match, program income, or multiple reporting streams.

Do not score only by dollars. A small award with hard eligibility rules can be riskier than a larger award with simple salary reimbursement.

## Factor 5: report timeliness and quality

Late, incomplete, or frequently corrected reports are risk signals. Score the subrecipient higher if it misses deadlines, submits reports with unexplained variances, or cannot tie costs to support.

This factor should update during the award. A low-risk partner can become moderate risk if reports begin to slip.

## Factor 6: prior monitoring results

Use your own monitoring history. Site visit findings, desk review issues, unresolved corrective action, or weak documentation should affect the score.

If past issues were fixed and stayed fixed, lower the score when evidence supports it. The goal is not to punish old problems. The goal is to set the right monitoring plan now.

## Turn the score into monitoring

Risk assessment is not complete until it changes monitoring. A low-risk subrecipient may get standard financial and performance report review. Moderate risk may add quarterly desk reviews or sample testing. High risk may require more frequent reports, technical help, site visits, or special conditions.

Write the plan next to the score. Auditors will ask whether the plan matches the risk and whether the monitoring happened.

## Reassess when facts change

Do not wait until renewal if the facts change during the year. Update the score when reports are late, costs are questioned, leadership changes, a new audit finding appears, or a monitoring visit finds weak support. Also update the score when a high-risk subrecipient proves the fix worked over several reporting cycles.

Keep the old score and the new score. The history shows that management responded to new information instead of using a once-a-year form.

## Keep the scoring file short

The risk file does not need to be long. One page can be enough if it shows the factors, score, tier, monitoring plan, reviewer, and date. Attach key support, such as an audit report, financial statement, or prior monitoring letter.

Avoid vague notes like "good partner" or "seems fine." Use facts: clean audit, no late reports in the last year, new finance director started in May, or two questioned costs found in desk review.
