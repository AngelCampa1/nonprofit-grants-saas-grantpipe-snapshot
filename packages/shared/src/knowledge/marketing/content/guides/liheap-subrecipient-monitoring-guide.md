# LIHEAP subrecipient monitoring guide

LIHEAP helps low-income households with home energy costs. Many state, tribal, and territorial grantees work through local agencies. That creates a monitoring duty. The pass-through entity must know whether each subrecipient is following program rules, paying benefits correctly, and keeping support.

The Uniform Guidance at 2 CFR 200.332 sets pass-through monitoring duties. LIHEAP also has its own statute, block grant rules, state plan requirements, and program guidance. Your monitoring process should connect both.

## Start with risk

Rate each subrecipient before the monitoring year starts. Use factors like award size, prior findings, staff turnover, late reports, audit results, payment volume, and new program duties. A higher-risk subrecipient should get more review.

Use the [subrecipient risk assessment guide](/resources/guides/subrecipient-risk-assessment-scoring-guide) to make the score consistent. Keep the score in the monitoring file so the review scope makes sense later.

## Review the agreement

The subaward agreement should name the LIHEAP funding, period, reporting duties, allowable activities, records required, monitoring rights, and corrective action process. It should also explain how benefits and administrative costs are handled.

If the agreement is vague, monitoring becomes harder. The reviewer needs a standard to test against. Update future agreements when monitoring shows a repeated gap.

## Test eligibility files

Sample household eligibility files. Check income, household size, identity or residency rules used by the program, crisis documentation if relevant, benefit category, approval, and date. The sample should match state or tribal policy.

Do not collect more personal data than needed in the monitoring file. Record the sample ID, finding, and result. Keep sensitive applicant records in the approved system or secure review folder.

## Review intake controls

Eligibility testing should include the intake process, not only final files. Check who can approve an application, who can change a household record, and how duplicate applications are found. If the subrecipient uses a local system, ask for the user role list and recent access review.

This step helps catch control gaps before they become payment errors. A clean sample is useful, but it does not prove that only approved staff can change benefits.

## Test benefit calculations

Benefit calculation errors can affect households and federal reporting. Recalculate a sample of benefits using the approved matrix or formula. Confirm the payment amount, vendor, account, date, and household file all match.

If a benefit was denied, check the denial reason and notice. Monitoring should test both approvals and denials because denial errors can be just as serious.

## Check vendor payment files

Many LIHEAP benefits are paid to energy vendors. Test whether vendor payments match approved household benefits. Keep the vendor invoice or payment file, payment date, household reference, and reconciliation. If a vendor refund or returned payment occurred, trace it through the subrecipient ledger.

Vendor records also help detect duplicate payments. Compare account numbers, household IDs, and payment dates in the sample.

## Review fiscal controls

Compare subrecipient reports to ledger support, payment records, and bank activity. Administrative costs should be tracked separately from benefit payments if the program requires it. Vendor payments should tie to approved household benefits.

Use the [grant documentation checklist](/resources/guides/grant-documentation-checklist-audit-ready) to review support. A summary report is not enough if source records cannot be found.

## Check reporting

Review the subrecipient's monthly or quarterly reports. Confirm counts, benefit dollars, administrative costs, crisis cases, refunds, and other fields against source records. If the state report has definitions, test those exact definitions.

Late reports should affect the next risk score. Repeated late reports may mean the subrecipient needs training or more frequent monitoring.

For seasonal programs, review reports during the busy period, not only after the season ends. Real-time review gives the pass-through entity a chance to correct benefit errors while households are still being served.

## Issue clear findings

A finding should say what was tested, what rule applied, what failed, and what must change. Avoid vague findings like "improve documentation." Name the missing record or wrong calculation.

Corrective action should include an owner and due date. After the due date, verify completion. Do not close a finding based only on a promise to fix it.

## Feed results into the next cycle

Monitoring is not a one-time binder. Use results to adjust risk scores, training, agreement language, and payment review. If several subrecipients miss the same rule, the pass-through entity may need clearer forms or training.

Keep monitoring records through the federal retention period and longer if an audit or finding is open. The [subrecipient monitoring requirements guide](/resources/guides/subrecipient-monitoring-requirements-uniform-guidance) covers the broader Uniform Guidance duties.

GrantPipe can help pass-through teams track subrecipient risk, monitoring schedules, findings, documents, and corrective action. That gives leadership a current view of which local partners need help before the next LIHEAP season is under way.
